<h3>第五章</h3>
<h3>牧羊犬学徒</h3>
<p>普拉克茜去寄宿学校后的第二天，托马斯把天狼星送到了凯尔·布莱的普格那里。一路上，他同天狼星谈了许多未来的事，答应说，在普格这里待满一年之后，就会让他见识一下羊区以外的人类世界，甚至可能让他定居剑桥。天狼星听着，也同意了；可他心里焦虑又悲伤，尾巴怎么也神气不起来。</p>
<p>有一点倒给了他实在的安慰：他知道普格是个不错的人。天狼星习惯按人类对待狗的态度给他们分类；甚至后来成年以后，他仍觉得这是一块很有用的试金石。第一类人只是对狗漠不关心，缺少足够的想象力，无法同它们建立任何相互回应的关系。第二类是“爱狗人士”，天狼星讨厌他们。这些人把狗浪漫化，实际上并不真正了解狗；他们夸大狗的智力和可爱之处，把狗娇养、喂得过饱，又把它们在性、争斗和狩猎方面的自然冲动饿死。对这类人来说，狗不过是会动的、又“可怜地像人”的洋娃娃。再有一类是厌恶狗的人，他们要么自命清高，不肯屈尊同不会说话的动物为伴，要么其实是害怕自己身上的动物性。最后一类，则是“对狗真正有兴趣的人”：他们既比较准确地理解人与狗之间的差异，又愿意把狗当作狗来尊重，把它视为一种虽相隔甚远、却本质上心意相通的亲族。普格就属于这一类。</p>
<p>到了农场，普格目前养着的两条超级牧羊犬立刻用一阵喧闹迎接他们。农夫从牛棚里走出来。他是个面色红润的中年人，留着稀疏的红褐色小胡子，蓝眼睛里仿佛永远带着笑意。天狼星还挺喜欢他身上的气味，猜想这人一定很爱笑。大家进了厨房，普格太太端来饮料，两个男人则谈起正事。普格仔细打量蹲在托马斯脚边的天狼星。“说真的，他当牧羊犬可大得过头了，特里隆先生，”普格用带歌唱般起伏的威尔士口音说，“他该去赶犀牛，反正不该赶我们这种小小的威尔士山羊。可是，老天！这脑袋可真不得了！如果脑子越大越聪明，特里隆先生，那他准是个天才，对不对！我都看见了——以后是他经营这个农场，我在后头替他跑腿赶羊。可惜我风湿太重啦！”托马斯承认，拿狗来说，天狼星确实算很聪明。“他会有用的。不过别期待太高，毕竟他只是个不会说话的动物。”“当然，”普格说，随后出人意料地冲天狼星眨了眨眼。“我用过你培育的狗，特里隆先生，都是好家伙。这里现在有伊德瓦尔，已经十二岁了，可还是一身力气；对一条终日工作的牧羊犬来说，这很不寻常。还有你两年前送来的那只母狗，我们叫她朱诺。老天，她学这行的门道可快得很！现在她还跟老伊德瓦尔生了一窝六只。可父母身上的魔法没传给它们——六个小傻瓜。不过我还是把它们全卖了个好价钱。”“好吧，”托马斯说，“我早就告诉过你，第二代别指望有什么特别。”“是啊，确实，你说过的，特里隆先生。”普格叹了口气，“我也把你的话告诉那些买主了，可他们死活不信；那我还能怎么办？只能收下他们的好价钱，再告诉他们自己是傻瓜。”点起烟斗后，普格问：“那这只多大了，特里隆先生？”托马斯迟疑了一下，说：“十五岁了，对吧，天狼星？”狗顺口漏出一声“是”，可普格似乎没觉得这声突然的咕哝有什么异样。“十五！摩西在上，特里隆先生，大多数狗活不到这岁数早死了，可这一只还不比幼犬大多少。”托马斯提醒他，延长寿命本就是实验目标之一。“那好，”普格笑着说，“只要他肯留下来，我就把女儿简嫁给他，等我死了让他接管农场。不过，特里隆先生，你刚才说他平时叫什么？”“我叫他天狼星。”托马斯说。普格抿起嘴，皱起眉：“这个名字隔着山谷喊可不顺口呀！”他停下来抽了几口烟，又说：“也许，天狼星先生，你肯允许我换个名字叫你。‘布兰’怎么样？”天狼星把脑袋歪到一边，装作怎么也听不懂这句显然是对他说的话。托马斯说：“很好。他很快就会习惯。”</p>
<p>发现连自己的名字都要被拿走，天狼星更加沮丧。他想，自己大概真的要被改造成另一个生物了；旧生活除了记忆之外，什么都不会留下。在家里，他虽然一直和普拉克茜习惯于共同拥有大多数东西，但这两个年轻生物也各自有些私人物品。几乎所有玩具原本都是共有的；可普拉克茜上村里的学校以后，也开始获得同新生活相连的个人财产——书、钢笔、铅笔，还有许多同同伴交往后得来的、说不清用途的小宝贝。天狼星也积攒了一点自己的东西，不过比普拉克茜少得多；因为没有手，真正能使用的物件本来就不多。家里分给他的那间极小房间里，有一层架子保存着几件古老珍宝：一根橡胶骨头、一块闪闪发亮的白色石英、一只羊头骨，还有几本图画书。后来又添了些东西——更多的书和乐谱、三只写字手套，以及几支钢笔和铅笔。</p>
<p>到了新生活里，他甚至必须比圣方济各更加一无所有，因为他只是一条狗——谁听说过狗还能有财产？好在财产对他本来就没什么意义。他天生有点共产主义倾向，也许源自犬类强烈的社会性。不过必须记得，狗虽在许多方面比人更具群居倾向，在另一些方面却有很敏锐的私有观念，例如对骨头、母狗、人类朋友和地盘。至少对天狼星来说，被剥得什么都不剩，连珍贵的写字手套也没有，确实等于被降格成了一头普通畜生。现在，他们甚至还要拿走他的名字。语言当然也同样被夺走了，原因很简单：农场里没有人能听懂他说话。甚至大多数时候，他连他们都听不懂，因为普格一家彼此交谈时用的是威尔士语。</p>
<p>天狼星的注意力早已从谈话上飘开；直到托马斯起身告辞，他才猛地重新留神。三人一起走到院子里。托马斯和普格握了手，又拍了拍天狼星，说：“再见，老伙计。你留在这儿。”天狼星假装困惑，作势要跟着托马斯走，被喝止回来后，便发出茫然的呜咽，退了回去。</p>
<p>下午，普格带着天狼星和伊德瓦尔来到一条高处的山谷，山坡上有他的一些羊正在吃草。他用威尔士语发出一道命令。伊德瓦尔立刻飞奔出去，开始把羊群赶拢。天狼星焦虑地望着普格。那道命令又重复了一遍，这一次还加上了他的新名字：“布兰。”他猛地冲出去帮伊德瓦尔。后者正在羊群后方绕出一个巨大的半圆，试图把羊逐渐挤向山谷下方的普格。天狼星立刻看懂局面，决定从半圆的另一端开始，同伊德瓦尔在中间会合。两条狗自然而然地各管自己的一段弧线。不过，伊德瓦尔负责的范围远比天狼星大，一方面因为经验不足的新手总得花时间把自己放跑到山坡上的羊追回来，另一方面也是因为伊德瓦尔速度更快。直到山坡上所有羊都被赶到普格站着的洼地里，行动才结束。普格说了一个威尔士词，伊德瓦尔马上蹲下，喘着气。天狼星也照做，同时拼命把那个词记进脑子里。</p>
<p>接着，普格让两条狗围着羊做了各种动作：把羊赶进石圈，再赶出来；聚成一团沿山谷前进；分成数量相等的两群；再重新赶散；最后还要把普格用手杖指中的某一只单独挑出来。所有命令都用威尔士语发出，并辅以不同形式的口哨。过了一阵，他只给伊德瓦尔下命令，把天狼星留在身边。伊德瓦尔被要求挑出一只指定的阉羊，用“眼神”控制住它。它像蛇一样贴着地面，慢慢爬到离羊只有几英尺的地方，始终死死盯着对方。随后它伏住不动，腹部紧贴地面，四肢随时准备爆发，鼻子伸在前方草地上，尾巴拖在身后。那只阉羊也盯着它，有时刚想挪动，伊德瓦尔只凭几个细小动作便把它制止。羊便耐心地站着等，或者带着轻微烦躁动一动。显然，它并不真正害怕。它早已习惯这种游戏，也认得伊德瓦尔眼神中那种必须服从的命令。</p>
<p>天狼星知道，自己正在亲眼看牧羊犬那种著名的、靠“眼神”控羊的本领。显然，伊德瓦尔已经把这种“眼力”练到了近乎完美。</p>
<p>随后，伊德瓦尔又完成了几套动作，天狼星焦急地看着。很快轮到他自己表演。这个新手已经竭尽全力观察，可真正上场时仍然手忙脚乱。羊不断从他身边溜走，惹得普格带着善意的怒气大声咆哮；更糟的是，疲劳使他越来越难精确控制身体，经常被石头绊倒，或一脚踏进坑里。那颗硕大的脑袋也越来越沉，稍微一滑，他就会像中枪的兔子一样整个翻倒。除此之外还有语言问题。一次又一次，普格用越来越疯狂的音量重复某个古怪的威尔士音节，伊德瓦尔则在旁边焦躁地呜咽，可天狼星还是完全不知道该干什么。要是这人肯说英语就好了，他想。</p>
<p>不过，轮到用“眼神”压住羊时，天狼星却惊喜地发现，自己一点也不无能。当然，这种本领还得磨炼。有一两次，那只羊差点逃开；显然，在天狼星的控制下，它不像面对伊德瓦尔时那样感到自己被牢牢制住，但它仍承认天狼星的权威。普格显然很满意。</p>
<p>不久，普格又让两条狗同时工作，不过会按名字分别发不同命令，而且给每条狗用不同的声调。天狼星必须习惯于听见较尖的那种声调就立即行动，不管有没有叫“布兰”；而较低沉的声调是给伊德瓦尔的，他必须学会忽略。</p>
<p>训练终于结束。普格沿着长草的山谷往回走，两条狗跟在脚后。天狼星累到了前所未有的程度——真是字面意义上的“累成狗”。尾巴垂着，脑袋几乎拖到地上；身体下侧全结着泥炭沼泽的黑泥，脚掌疼，脑袋也疼。一想到整整一年都要这样过，身边除了智力低下的狗和疏远的普格，绝望便涌上来。也许自己最后会把语言彻底忘掉；等再次见到普拉克茜时，他真的已经变成一头不会说话的畜生。但即便精疲力尽又心灰意冷，他仍能唤起自己最根本的那股犬类执拗，告诉自己绝不能被这种新生活打败。就在这时，他发现普格正因为自己那副狼狈样，用带着友好嘲弄的目光打量他；于是他硬把尾巴竖起来摇了几下，同时咧嘴一笑，仿佛在说：“哦，我有的是胆气，你等着瞧吧。”这反应明显得太像人，把普格吓了一跳，也让他开始思索。</p>
<p>回到农场后，两条狗吃到了普格一家午饭剩下的食物。狼吞虎咽地吃完，它们便被关进一间外屋过夜。稻草铺垫下面是粗糙的石地。天狼星觉得自己仿佛才刚躺下睡着，便又听见伊德瓦尔在关着的门边呜咽，于是醒了过来。阳光正从墙缝里射进屋。</p>
<p>接下来几周，天狼星一直不停地同羊打交道，很快便开始掌握这份工作的窍门。熟练之后，他不必再花那么多力气弥补自己的错误，回家时也没那么疲惫了。他不仅成功学会了那些威尔士语命令，还记住了各块田地的名字。一天，普格带两条狗深入山区，去巡视偏远高地牧场的羊，同时教天狼星各个山坡、溪流和 cwm——山谷盆地——的名字。这里对天狼星并不陌生，因为从前他常同托马斯往这个方向散步。走到某处山口时，他们离加思只剩两英里左右。他甚至觉得自己从风里闻到一丝家中特有的气味，虽然这大概只是幻觉。</p>
<p>没过多久，天狼星便积累了足够经验，可以独自执行命令。例如，人们可以派他把所有蕨丛都搜索一遍，寻找生病的羊；因为羊一旦不舒服，便会害怕同伴那种仿佛带着责备的目光，于是躲进蕨丛，若没人发现，就可能因得不到照料而死。天狼星也知道怎样帮助陷进泥沼或卡在岩壁间的羊脱困。他会小心咬住它往外拉，提供额外的力，让它挣扎着回到安全处。他还能逮住一只羊，把它掀倒，并按在地上，让普格或雇工检查。</p>
<p>他的“眼力”也进步得很快。在这一点上，不同的狗会落在两个极端之间：有的对羊过于温和，有的则过于冷酷。伊德瓦尔总体上偏强硬，有时把羊弄得过分紧张不安；天狼星天性却往往太温和，因此不得不有意识地学会更坚定的作风，权威才真正建立起来。两条狗的天然差异也体现在整体工作方式上。伊德瓦尔属于“固执型”，坚持凡事按自己的办法做；普格一旦阻止，它就会挑衅似的扬起尾巴，干脆小跑离开现场，仿佛“老子不玩了”。值得普格称道的是，这种时候他通常都会带着幽默的骂声让步，因为他知道伊德瓦尔按自己的方式一样能把事情做好。天狼星则属于“听使唤型”。他极度渴望学习，却不太相信自己的直觉。牧羊人一般认为，这类狗长期看不如前一类出色，因为它们缺少天才式的自信；不过普格很快就明白，布兰的顺从绝不是奴性。学会一套办法后，他常会主动加入新花样，大幅改进原有方法。即使后来已成为牧羊高手，他仍随时愿意观察别的工作犬，从它们身上学新招。</p>
<p>天狼星可以独自被派进山里，从羊群中挑出指定的一批——无论是年轻母羊、第一次剪毛前的“hoggs”青年羊，还是阉羊——然后不靠人帮忙，把它们从山上赶回农场。这些才是真正的超级牧羊犬工作。为了充分利用自己那些聪明的狗，普格把所有栅门都改成了狗也能开关的门闩。</p>
<p>秋天将近时，需要把一批批羔羊，或年老、体弱的母羊，从山里赶下来，运去出售。普格几乎把这项工作完全交给伊德瓦尔和天狼星，有时朱诺也会帮忙。但那只聪明的母狗天性很不稳定，常因抽搐发作而无法工作。几条狗会穿过高处荒原，挑出合适的羊；有时羊会在云雾里再次走失，它们就靠嗅觉重新找回，最后把羊群成团赶上高山谷里的草道。普格所有的羊臀部都有一个红色记号，可色盲的狗当然看不见。除此之外，每只羊左耳上还剪有三道小口，标明它属于普格。这对伊德瓦尔和天狼星非常有用，可以用来再次确认凯尔·布莱羊群共有的特殊气味。凡是从邻近牧区误闯进来的羊，很快就会被识别出来并护送回去。顺便说一句，除了整群共有的气味，每只羊还各有独特的体味。天狼星只用了几周，就能仅凭气味，甚至仅凭叫声，认出羊群里每一只羊。偶尔，它们会发现有羊受伤，这时必须有一条狗跑回农场把普格叫来。狗之间有一套约定俗成的吠叫法：“有羊受伤”是一种；另一种较不急促，表示“羊没受伤，但被困在岩壁，无法接近”；还有一种表示“羊死了”。</p>
<p>为了出售而收拢羊只的工作，会在好几个星期里断断续续反复进行。羔羊或母羊从荒原赶下来以后，还要搭火车，或者坐专门租来的卡车，送去低地的牲畜拍卖场。牧羊犬也跟着一起去，天狼星非常喜欢这种进入“广阔世界”的短途旅行。光是再次听见英语，发现自己仍然听得懂，他就已经十分愉快。</p>
<p>拍卖季结束、秋意真正浓起来以后，狗们的主要任务是阻止羊进入山谷高地的草场。山地羊常习惯在高处睡觉，早晨再下到草更肥美的低处；但秋天必须阻止它们这样做，因为山谷里的青草冬天更加急需。秋季还得阻止母羊去沼泽地吃草，以免感染肝片吸虫。秋天也是整群羊药浴的时候。普格有好几百只羊，这是项大工程；连续很多天，狗们都累得要命，不断分批把羊赶下来、驱进围栏，由普格或帮工一只只抓住，强行按进药液池。天狼星高兴地发现，自己承受这种高强度工作的能力已经不逊于伊德瓦尔，虽然此时速度和灵活性还稍差一点。</p>
<p>接着又来了另一项任务。必须把当年的小母羊全部集中起来，送去低地农场过冬，让它们避开山上的严酷冬季——恶劣天气和贫乏食物。直到第二年五月，它们才会被带回来。</p>
<p>尽管工作如此繁重，也总有些日子，狗们什么都不用干，只能在院子里闲晃，跟着普格四处巡视，或者替人跑腿去村里送东西。村里有一家兼卖文具和报纸的小店，很吸引天狼星。门外张贴的海报让他得以知道最耸人听闻的新闻。有时他会把前爪搭在窗台上，读陈列在里面的报纸大标题，或者看看那一小排廉价小说的书名。在村里，他也会遇到别的狗。它们已经不会给他找麻烦了，因为此时他体形很大，而且“硬得像铁钉”。出于对托马斯的忠诚，他还试着研究这些动物的心理；可除了一些简单的气质差异外，它们的精神世界都惊人地相似，单调得令人沮丧。它们最明显的区别，反而大多是人类训练塑造出来的：有些天生愿意亲近所有人；有些对陌生人冷淡，却近乎强迫地依恋主人；有些总爱献媚；有些则习惯畏缩。</p>
<p>一天，天狼星在村里碰见一条漂亮的年轻红色塞特母犬，正处在发情期。生活突然又值得过了。她的气味和身体接触令他陶醉。普格进酒馆时，两条狗就在村里的空地上追逐嬉闹。（普格似乎认为，若强迫超级牧羊犬陪他坐在酒馆里，它们一定会无聊得发疯。）这段结合最后在两个男学生和一个失业采石工猥亵又好奇的注视下完成。</p>
<p>从此，天狼星总渴望进村去找那只母狗。他一度很想从农场溜走，趁她仍在兴头上尽量多同她待在一起；但他没有这样做，因为他曾亲眼见过邻近农场的一条狗，仅仅因为擅离职守就被狠狠鞭打。天狼星早已下定决心，绝不做任何可能招致这种羞辱的事。他一生从未挨过正式鞭打，虽然有人愤怒时偶尔也会打他或踢他。可有计划地抽打，在他看来是对一个有智力、有自尊的人格最致命的侮辱。假如普格敢这么对他，不论后果如何，天狼星都认为自己必须当场杀死普格。可是普格从来没有这样做。他属于那一派牧羊犬主人：他们以靠善意而不是残暴获得服从为荣。天狼星从未见他对任何狗施暴。即使天狼星真的严重冒犯了他，普格大概也不会打他，因为他始终有一种坚定却模糊的感觉：这条新来的狗不知怎么地不仅仅是一条狗，甚至不仅仅是一条超级狗。</p>
<p>有几件事逐渐引起了普格的怀疑。有一次，他让天狼星叼着篮子和一张十先令纸币进村，去取鞋匠修好的靴子。狗按吩咐带回了靴子和找零。回到院子时，普格正待在一间黑暗的外屋里，恰好看见天狼星把靴子取出来，又低头研究篮子里的钱。他困惑地看了好一阵，突然沿原路小跑回去，一路贴着地面闻。过了一会儿，他发现一个很小的东西，又费尽力气把它捡起来，显然十分满意地带了回来。等他把东西丢进篮子，普格看清那是一枚棕色小圆片——一便士硬币。随后天狼星把篮子送到普格面前。里面有靴子、已注明收款的账单，以及找零：两枚半克朗、一枚两先令硬币和七枚便士。普格还没异想天开到认为狗真的数过找零、又拿账单核对；但至少，他显然看出了六枚便士和七枚便士的差别。</p>
<p>另一件事让普格怀疑，这条狗身上有某种“人的东西”——他自己就是这么说的。农夫养了几头母牛，还有一头很漂亮的年轻公牛。天狼星以前被牛角顶过一次，又听过许多关于公牛的可怕故事。附近农场有时会牵母牛过来，让凯尔·布莱的公牛配种。每逢这种时候，狗们必须进围场，把公牛圈住，再沿小路赶进农场院子，让它去完成求偶交配。事后，狗再把公牛赶回围场。天狼星每次都非常紧张，工作做得很糟。伊德瓦尔会以凶猛的坚持正面顶住公牛，又总能在牛角低下刺来时千钧一发地闪开；天狼星却因为太紧张，一味想同公牛保持距离。公牛很快发现他是个胆小鬼，甚至养成了追着他跑的习惯。</p>
<p>顺便说一句，普格还很注意两条狗在公牛和母牛被赶到院子配种时的不同表现。那时四周通常会围着一小群兴致勃勃的男人和男孩，而女人们则识趣地待在屋里。伊德瓦尔会在院里东闻西嗅，或者索性趴下休息。天狼星却和那些人类旁观者一样，兴致勃勃地把整个过程看完。显然，他的兴趣带有性意味；因为公牛完成那次笨拙拥抱时，天狼星自己也显出毫无疑问的性兴奋。</p>
<p>不过，最让普格吃惊、也最令他怀疑天狼星的智力已经和人一样敏捷的事情，还是同那头公牛欺负胆小天狼星的习惯有关。一天，普格带伊德瓦尔去了村里，雇工欧文又正在远处田里犁地。不知怎么，那头公牛冲破围场跑进了小路。它一路小跑进院子，看见简提着一篮洗好的衣服，便喷着鼻息向她走去。简本来就胆小，立刻尖叫一声，扔下篮子溜进马厩。公牛花了几分钟用角翻弄那些衣服，随后沿小路跑掉。与此同时，普格太太小心翼翼地从屋里探了出来。就在这时，天狼星出现，沿路追向公牛。直到公牛已经跑上大路，他才追上。随后他一声不响地猛冲过去，一口咬住牛尾。公牛怒吼着转身，可天狼星已经松口，边吠边朝农场小路后退。公牛追着他，天狼星便把它一路引回院子，再引进原来的围场。这时公牛已经有些气喘，天狼星却又领着它在地里一圈一圈跑，直到它的怒火逐渐降下去。公牛越不愿再追，天狼星就越大胆。等它终于站住，他猛扑上去，狠狠咬了一口后腿。被重新激怒的公牛再次追他，可很快又筋疲力尽。这个过程反复了好几次，直到天狼星发现两个女人已经在树篱缺口处拉起了几股带刺铁丝。于是他昂着得意的尾巴撤退，留下那头已经彻底被吓服的公牛。从那以后，天狼星再也不怕这头公牛，处理它或其他牛都不成问题。</p>
<p>这件事之后不久，天狼星又做了一件远远超出“超级牧羊犬”能力范围的事。</p>
<p>整个第一个学期，他都孤独得要命。他想念自己的人，尤其想念普拉克茜。要是能写封信就好了！可他没有写字手套，也没有纸笔。而且无论如何，过去给信贴邮票这件事，他始终办不到。</p>
<p>他知道，如果嘴里叼着铅笔，自己勉强能很难看地写几个字——前提是能找到铅笔和纸。他曾见普格从橡木餐具柜的抽屉里拿出钢笔、墨水和信纸。一天，趁普格太太和简正在挤奶，他悄悄溜进厨房，打开抽屉，果然找到几张纸、几个信封、一支钢笔、一瓶墨水，还有一支笔尖已经折断的铅笔。他偷走一张纸和一个信封。钢笔和墨水太复杂，那支铅笔又没法用，所以他把它们留下，只叼着纸和信封回到狗住的外屋，藏进一个旧包装箱下面的稻草里。</p>
<p>现在只能等着哪天有人需要把铅笔削尖。天狼星逮住一切机会偷偷溜进厨房，查看抽屉。与此同时，他花了很多心思，预先设计自己要怎样写这封信、写些什么。有时还会练习。他嘴里叼着一小片板岩，在门前的石板台阶上乱写。过程非常困难，因为鼻子总挡着视线，他根本看不清自己在写什么；而且那片薄板岩往往很快就断掉。</p>
<p>终于，在等了许多天以后，他发现铅笔被人削好了。于是他把它叼回外屋。</p>
<p>又过了好几天，天狼星才终于找到机会写信。歪歪斜斜、像蜘蛛腿一样的大写字母写着：“亲爱的普拉克茜，希望你过得快乐。没有你我很孤独，非常非常孤独。爱你的，天狼星。”他极其小心地在信封上写地址，只能希望自己的记忆没出错。把信纸折起来塞进信封，也让他吃尽苦头。随后，他舔湿带胶的封口，合上信封，再用爪子压住。本来他打算不贴邮票就寄出去；可一想到那样普拉克茜就得补交三便士的双倍邮资，他难受得受不了，于是决定继续等，看看抽屉里会不会出现邮票。终于有一天，一小版六枚、每枚一便士半的邮票出现了，他立刻叼走，开始设法取下一枚。起先，他用两只爪子压住整版邮票，再用牙齿拉扯。结果邮票从中间撕裂，叼在嘴里的那一半又黏到湿牙齿上，怎么也甩不掉。这番经历把他弄得慌乱，只好停下来更认真地想办法。最后，他想到一招：先用爪子压住信封，舔湿右上角；然后极其小心地用牙叼起整版邮票，放到信封上，让其中一枚角上的邮票大致落到正确位置。这非常困难，因为鼻子照例挡着视线。他松口检查，发现邮票歪得厉害，而且有一部分还悬在信封外面，于是赶紧重新提起，再把它放得更靠里。又检查一次后，他小心地把整版稍稍拖动到更合适的位置，然后用爪子按紧。等他觉得胶已经干了，就用爪子压住整版邮票，牙齿轻轻拉信封。信封果然分开了，那枚邮票完整留在上面，只是旁边一枚邮票的一小部分也被带下来，伸出信封边缘。他用牙把多出来的部分修掉，再把那张已经被折腾得惨不忍睹的邮票纸放回抽屉。直到重新回到信边，他才发现那枚邮票竟贴倒了。</p>
<p>他把信藏回稻草底下，等下一次有人派他去村里跑腿。又过了好几天，这个机会才来。人们本来常让他带信去邮局，可这一次，篮子里只有他自己的信。他叼着篮子和一张杂货店订单小跑出门，同时带上那封信。他直奔邮局，放下篮子，叼出信，前身撑到墙上，把这份珍贵文件塞进了邮筒。</p>
<p>村里人见狗做这种事并不觉得特别罕见。正好路过的休·威廉斯医生甚至几乎没留意；不过第二天，他碰见普格先生打招呼时，顺口提起这件事，还称赞他的狗聪明。可普格那天根本没寄过信。他开始琢磨，是不是妻子写信给巴拉的母亲，或者简偷偷托布兰送了封情书。后一种可能让他很不安；因为普格虽然天性友善，习惯尊重、信任别人，却绝不是什么现代派父亲。回家后，他立刻盘问。普格太太和简都否认给过布兰任何信。普格走到抽屉旁，看见那版邮票被弄得一塌糊涂：少了一枚，另两枚也被撕破。他一时怒火上头，指责女儿偷偷通信、偷邮票、撒谎，而且手脚笨拙。简强烈为自己辩护，最后讽刺道：“那你去问问布兰是谁的信啊。”这句气话却在普格脑子里点亮了一个荒唐念头。他又走到抽屉边，拿起那支铅笔。上面有牙印——是布兰咬的，还是自己咬的？这怀疑简直疯狂。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a572"></span>CHAPTER V</h3>
<p><span class="t28">O</span><span class="t30">N </span><span class="t31">THE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">DAY</span><span class="t30"> </span>after Plaxy went to boarding school Thomas took Sirius over to Pugh at Caer Blai. On the way he talked a great deal to the dog about his future, promising that when he had been with Pugh a year he should see something of the human world beyond the sheep-country, and possibly settle in Cambridge. Sirius listened and consented; but he was an anxious and a sorrowing animal, and his tail would not stay proud.</p>
<p>One source of solid comfort lay in the fact that he knew Pugh for a decent sort. Sirius classified human beings in respect of their altitude to dogs; and even in later life he found this a useful touchstone of human character. There were those who were simply indifferent to dogs, lacking sufficient imagination to enter into any reciprocal relation with them. There were the “dog-lovers,” whom he detested. These were folk who sentimentalized dogs, and really had no accurate awareness of them, exaggerating their intelligence and loveableness, mollycoddling them and over-feeding them; and starving their natural impulses of sex, pugnacity and hunting. For this sort, dogs were merely animate and “pathetically human” dolls. Then there were the dog-detesters, who were either too highbrow to descend to companionship with a dumb animal or too frightened of their own animal nature. Finally there were the “dog-interested,” who combined a fairly accurate sense of the difference between dog and man with a disposition to respect a dog as a dog, as a rather remote but essentially like-minded relative. Pugh was of this sort.</p>
<p>At the farm they were greeted with an uproar by the two super-sheep-dogs at present in Pugh’s possession. The farmer issued from the byre. He was a fresh-complexioned middle-aged man with a scrubby reddish moustache and blue eyes with a permanent twinkle. Sirius rather liked the smell of him. He guessed the man must do a lot of laughing. They were taken into the kitchen, where drinks were provided by Mrs. Pugh, while the two men talked. Pugh had a good look at Sirius, who was squatting on the floor by Thomas. “He’s really far too big for a sheep-dog, Mr. Trelone,” said Pugh in his singing Welsh voice. “He should be herding rhinoceroses, or not the little Welsh mountain sheep, anyhow. But, my! What a head he has on him! If it’s brain that counts, Mr. Trelone, he must be a genius, isn’t it! I can see it’s he that’ll be running this farm and me running after the sheep for him. Pity I’m so rheumatic!” Thomas admitted that Sirius was pretty bright, for a dog. “He’ll be useful. But don’t expect too much. After all, he’s only a dumb animal.” “Of course,” said Pugh, and then surprisingly he winked at Sirius. “I have had experience of your dogs, Mr. Trelone, and fine animals they are. There’s Idwal here now. He’s full of strength, though he is twelve, which is very unusual for a hard-working sheep-dog. Then there’s the bitch you sent me two years ago. Juno, we call her. My! She was quick to learn the tricks of the trade! And now she has had that litter of six by old Idwal. But the magic did not go into them from the parents. They are six little fools. But I have sold them all for a good price.” “Well,” said Thomas, “I told you not to expect anything from the second generation.” Pugh replied with a sigh. “Yes, indeed, and you did, Mr. Trelone. I told the purchasers what you said, but they would not believe it, whatever; so what could I do but take the good price and tell them they were fools.” After lighting his pipe Pugh asked, “And how old is this one, Mr. Trelone?” Thomas hesitated, then said, “Fifteen, aren’t you, Sirius?” The dog let slip a “Yes,” but Pugh apparently did not notice anything unusual in his sudden grunt. “Fifteen! Holy Moses, Mr. Trelone, but most dogs are dead long before that, and this one is not much more than a puppy.” Thomas reminded him that longevity had been one of the aims of the experiment. “Well,” said Pugh laughing, “if he will stay on with me he shall marry my daughter Jane and take over the farm when I am gone. But what is the name he answers to, Mr. Trelone, did you say?” “I call him Sirius,” said Thomas. Pugh pursed his mouth and frowned. “That is not a handy name for calling across the valley, is it!” He paused, puffing at his pipe, then added, “Perhaps, Mr. Sirius, you will permit me to call you by some other name. How would Bran do?” Sirius had tilted his head over on one side, as though he were vainly trying to understand this remark, obviously addressed to him. Thomas said, “That’s fine. He’ll pick it up in no time.”</p>
<p>Sirius’s despondency was increased by the discovery that even his name was to be taken from him. Surely, he thought, he would be changed into a new being. Nothing whatever of the old life was to be left to him but the memory of it. At home, though he had grown up in the custom of sharing ownership of most things with Plaxy, each of the two young creatures had possessions of their own. Nearly all their toys had been held in common, but when Plaxy had gone to the village school she had also begun to acquire personal property connected with her new life — books, pens, pencils and many little nondescript treasures gained through intercourse with her fellows. Sirius also had begun to collect a few personal possessions, though far fewer than Plaxy; for, owing to his lack of hands, there were few things that he could use. There were certain ancient treasures preserved on a shelf in the minute room that had been allotted him — a rubber bone, a lump of gleaming white quartz, a sheep’s skull, several picture books. And there were later-acquired possessions — more books, and music, his three writing gloves and several pens and pencils.</p>
<p>In his new life he had to be even more propertyless than St. Francis, for he was just a dog, and whoever heard of a dog with property? Fortunately property meant little to him; he had a propensity towards communism, due perhaps to his strong canine sociality. It should be remembered, however, that though dogs in many ways show a far more social disposition than human beings, in some respects they have a keen sense of personal ownership, for instance over bones, bitches, human friends, and localities. For Sirius, at any rate, to be completely stripped, even of his precious writing gloves, was indeed to be reduced to the status of a brute beast. And now they intended to take away his very name. Speech, too, was of course stolen from him by the simple fact that no one on the farm could understand him. Nor was he to be able as a rule to understand them, for among themselves the Pughs talked Welsh.</p>
<p>Sirius’s attention had wandered from the conversation, but it was recalled when Thomas rose to leave. All three went out into the yard. Thomas shook hands with Pugh, then patted Sirius, and said “Good-bye, old man. You stay there.” Sirius feigned perplexity, made as if to follow Thomas, was shooed back and retreated with a puzzled whimper.</p>
<p>In the afternoon Pugh took Sirius and Idwal to a high valley, on the slopes of which some of his sheep were grazing. He gave a word of command in Welsh. Idwal raced off and began to round up the sheep. Sirius looked anxiously at Pugh. The command was repeated, this time along with his new name, “Bran.” He shot away to help Idwal, who was moving round behind the sheep in a great semi-circle, so as to bunch them towards Pugh down in the valley. Sirius tumbled to the situation at once, and decided to start at the opposite horn of the semi-circle and meet Idwal in the middle. Automatically each dog took charge of his own arc. Idwal’s, however, was much wider than Sirius’s, partly because the less experienced dog had to spend time in retrieving sheep that he had allowed to slip away up the hill, partly because Idwal was the faster animal. The operation continued till all the sheep on the hillside had been brought down into the hollow where Pugh was standing. He said a Welsh word, and Idwal at once squatted, panting. Sirius followed suit, anxiously trying to fix the word in his memory.</p>
<p>Pugh then put the two dogs through sundry manœuvres with the sheep, folding them in a stone pen, fetching them out, taking them in a bunch along the valley, separating them into two equal groups, scattering them again, picking out a particular individual at which Pugh pointed with his stick. All this was done with commands in Welsh, aided by various kinds of whistling. After a while he issued commands to Idwal alone, keeping Sirius at his side. Idwal was made to single out a particular wether and hold it with his eye. He crept up to within a few feet of it, flat on the ground like a snake, all the while staring fixedly at it. Then he lay still, belly to earth, legs ready for sudden action, nose stretched out on the grass in front of him, tail on the grass behind him. The wether stared back, or made incipient movements, which Idwal checked by mere gestures. The animal just stood patiently waiting, or fidgeted with mild exasperation. Obviously it was not really afraid. It was used to this sort of game, and it recognized in Idwal’s eye a command that must be obeyed.</p>
<p>Sirius knew that he was witnessing the famous sheep-dog trick of control by “the eye.” Idwal evidently had developed “the eye” almost to perfection.</p>
<p>Idwal was then put through other manœuvres, which Sirius anxiously watched. Presently it was his turn to perform. The novice had strained every nerve to follow the proceedings, but he found himself badly at sea. Not only did the sheep constantly slip past him, causing Pugh to bellow with amiable rage, but also he found that fatigue was preventing him from managing his body with precision, so that he often stumbled over rocks or into holes. His great head became increasingly heavy, so that any slight slip might bowl him over like a shot rabbit. In addition to all this there was the language difficulty. Again and again Sirius found himself completely at a loss while Pugh repeated some strange Welsh noise in a frantic crescendo, and Idwal whimpered impatiently at his side. If only the man would talk English, thought Sirius.</p>
<p>But when it came to the exercise of holding the sheep by the power of the eye, Sirius found to his delight that he was by no means incompetent. Of course the process needed perfecting. Once or twice the sheep nearly broke away. It evidently did not feel itself as masterfully held as it did under Idwal, but it recognized Sirius’s authority. Pugh was obviously pleased.</p>
<p>Presently Pugh worked the two dogs together again, but issued different commands to each by name, and also in a different tone of voice for each. Sirius had to get accustomed to acting promptly to the shriller tone, whether his name, Bran, was mentioned or not; and to ignore the deeper tone which was meant for Idwal.</p>
<p>At last the lesson was over. Pugh walked back along the grassy valley with the two dogs at his heels. Sirius was more tired than he had ever been before, “dog-tired,” as we say. His tail hung, his head almost touched the ground. The under surface of his body was caked with bog-mud. His feet were sore, his head ached. With despair he looked forward to a whole year of this sort of thing, with no companion but the sub-human dogs and the remote Pugh. Perhaps he would forget language altogether, and when he met Plaxy again he would indeed be a dumb animal. But, worn out and despondent as he was, he was able to summon his fundamental doggedness, and promise himself that he would not be beaten by this new life. And when he caught Pugh’s eye quizzing him with friendly ridicule because of his abject appearance, he stuck up his tail and wagged it, at the same time grinning, as though to say, “Oh, I have spunk all right, you’ll see.” This unmistakably human response startled Pugh, and set him thinking.</p>
<p>When they reached the farm, the two dogs were given the remains of the family midday dinner. After they had devoured this they were put into an outhouse for the night. Under the straw bedding there was a rough stone floor. It seemed to Sirius that he had hardly lain down and gone to sleep when he woke up to the sound of Idwal whining at the shut door. Sunlight streamed through the chinks.</p>
<p>During the following weeks Sirius was given constant work with the sheep, and he soon began to get the hang of the job. With practice he wasted less energy on retrieving his mistakes, and arrived home less tired. He was successfully learning not only the Welsh commands but the names of the fields. One day Pugh took both dogs far up among the hills to inspect the sheep on the remote high pastures and teach Sirius the names of the hillsides, streams and cwms. Here he was in familiar country, for he had often walked in this direction with Thomas. At one point the tour brought him to a bwlch within a couple of miles of his home. He even seemed to catch a faint characteristic whiff of it on the wind, but this was probably a delusion.</p>
<p>It did not take long for Sirius to gain sufficient experience to carry out orders unattended. For instance he could be sent to search all the bracken areas for sick sheep; for when sheep feel ill they grow fearful of their disapproving fellows, and so they hide themselves in the bracken, where, if they are not found, they may die through lack of attention. Sirius knew also how to help a bogged or crag-bound sheep to free itself. He would carefully tug at it, till the extra force enabled it to struggle into safety. And he could catch a sheep and throw it, and hold it down for Pugh or his man to inspect.</p>
<p>The power of his “eye”, too, was greatly improving. In this matter dogs vary between excessive gentleness towards the sheep and excessive ruthlessness. Idwal was on the whole ruthless, sometimes making the sheep unduly nervous and restless. Sirius, on the other hand, was often too mild by disposition, so that his authority was not established until he had deliberately learnt a firmer policy. The difference of natural style appeared also in the whole method of the two dogs. Idwal was of the “obstinate” type, insisting on doing everything in his own way. If Pugh prevented him he would raise his tail defiantly and simply trot off the field of action, “refusing to play.” It was to Pugh’s credit that on these occasions he generally gave in, with humorous vituperation, knowing well that Idwal could be trusted to do the job efficiently in his own style. Sirius, on the other hand, was of the “biddable” type. He was desperately anxious to learn, and had little faith in his own intuition. Shepherds regard this type of dog as less brilliant in the long run than the other, since they lack the conviction of genius; but it soon became clear to Pugh that Bran’s docility was not due to a servile disposition. When he had learnt his lesson he often introduced novelties which greatly improved the method. Yet even when he had become an expert with sheep, he was always ready to pick up new tips from observation of other dogs at work.</p>
<p>Sirius could be sent out into the hills alone to select a required bunch of sheep from the flock, whether young ewes or “hoggs” (young sheep before their first shearing) or wethers; and he could bring them down from the hills to the farm without human aid. All this was real super-sheep-dog work. In order to make full use of his clever animals Pugh had arranged all his gates with latches that a dog could open or shut.</p>
<p>As autumn neared, the time came for bringing groups of lambs or of old or unhealthy ewes down from the mountains to be taken away for sale. This task Pugh entrusted almost entirely to Idwal and Sirius, helped sometimes by Juno. But that bright creature was of a distressingly unstable nature, and was often incapacitated by convulsions. The dogs would travel over the high moors, picking out the appropriate animals, sometimes losing them again in the cloud, and recovering them by scent, finally bringing them in a bunch along the turfy track in the high valley. All Pugh’s sheep bore a red mark on the rump, but this, of course, was invisible to the colour-blind dogs. In addition the sheep bore three little slits in the left ear to mark them as Pugh’s. This was invaluable to Idwal and Sirius, as a confirmation of the distinctive smell of the Caer Blai flock. Any sheep that had strayed into Caer Blai territory from a neighbouring run was soon detected and piloted home. In addition to the common smell of the flock, by the way, each individual sheep had its own peculiar odour. It did not take Sirius more than a few weeks to recognize every sheep in the flock by its smell, or even by its voice. Occasionally the dogs found a sheep that had been damaged, and then one of them had to set out to the farm to fetch Pugh. There was a recognized way of barking to signify “damaged sheep”; another, less excited, meant “sheep undamaged but crag-bound and inaccessible”; yet another meant “dead sheep.”</p>
<p>The collecting of sheep for sale was a process which occurred now and again over many weeks. When the lambs or ewes had been brought down from the moors they had to be taken by train, or in lorries hired for the purpose, to the auction sales in the lower country. The dogs accompanied them, and Sirius thoroughly enjoyed these excursions into the great world. It was a pleasure merely to hear the English language spoken again, and to find that he could still understand it.</p>
<p>When the sales were over and autumn was well under way, the main task of the dogs was to guard the high valley pastures from the sheep. It is often the custom of mountain sheep to sleep on the heights and come down in the morning to the richer grazing; but in the autumn they must be prevented from doing this because the valley grass will be more urgently needed in the winter. In the autumn, too, the ewes must be prevented from grazing in marshy places, lest they should become infected by liver fluke. And autumn is a time for dipping the whole flock. As Pugh had many hundreds of sheep, this was a great undertaking, and the dogs were desperately hard worked for many days, bringing the sheep down in batches and driving them into the pen, where Pugh or one of his helpers could seize each animal in turn and force it into the dip. Sirius was pleased to find that he stood up to the strain of this great undertaking as well as Idwal, though he was not at this stage quite so fast or quite so agile.</p>
<p>Presently came another task. The ewe lambs had to be collected and sent to a lowland farm so that they might escape the grim conditions on the mountains during winter, the savage weather and poor food. Not till the following May would they be brought home.</p>
<p>In spite of all this hard work, there were days when the dogs had nothing to do but hang about the yard or accompany Pugh on his rounds, or run messages to the village. A little stationery-cum-newspaper shop in the village used to attract Sirius. Outside were posters from which he gained the most sensational news. Sometimes he put his paws on the windowsill and read the headlines of the papers displayed within, or the titles of the little rank of cheap novels. In the village he met other dogs. They gave him no trouble, because he was by now very large and “hard as nails.” Out of loyalty to Thomas he tried to study the psychology of these animals; but apart from simple temperamental differences, they were mentally all depressingly alike. The most obvious differences between them had been imposed by human conditioning. Some were disposed towards friendliness with all human beings, some were cold to strangers but obsessively devoted to their masters, some habitually fawned, some cringed.</p>
<p>One day in the village Sirius came upon a fine young bitch in heat, a red setter. Suddenly life was worth living once more. Her odour and touch intoxicated him. In their love-play they careered about the open space in the village while Pugh was in the pub. (Pugh seems to have had an idea that super-sheep-dogs would be cruelly bored if they were forced to sit with him inside.) The consummation of this union took place under the lascivious eyes of two schoolboys and an unemployed quarryman.</p>
<p>Henceforth Sirius had a constant hunger for the village and the bitch. He was tempted to run away from the farm and have all he could of her while she was still in the mood; but he did not, because he had once seen a dog on a neighbouring farm thrashed for absenting himself from duty. Sirius was determined that he would never do anything to incur such an indignity. He had never been thrashed in his life, though occasionally hit or kicked in anger. To be deliberately thrashed seemed to him to be a mortal insult to his dignity as an intelligent and self-respecting person. If Pugh ever tried it on him, Pugh must be killed on the spot, whatever the consequences. But Pugh never did. Pugh belonged to the school of sheep-dog owners who pride themselves on obtaining obedience by kindness rather than ferocity. Sirius never saw him use violence on any dog. Probably he would never have beaten Sirius even if Sirius had given him serious provocation, for he had a firm though vague conviction that the new dog was somehow more than a dog, even more than a super-dog.</p>
<p>Several incidents had aroused this suspicion. Once he sent Sirius to the village with a basket and a ten-shilling note to fetch a pair of boots which the cobbler had repaired. The dog duly brought back the boots and the change. When he arrived in the yard, Pugh, who was in the darkness of an outhouse, saw Sirius take out the boots and study the money in the basket. After looking puzzled for some time he trotted back along his tracks, nosing the ground. Presently he came on a small object which he managed with great difficulty to pick up. With obvious satisfaction he brought it back with him. When he dropped it into the basket it was visible to Pugh as a small brown disc, in fact a penny. Sirius then brought the basket to Pugh. It contained the boots, the receipted bill and the change, which consisted of two half-crowns, a florin, and seven pennies. Pugh was not so fanciful as to suppose that the dog had actually counted the change and checked it by the bill, but at least he must have spotted the difference between six pennies and seven.</p>
<p>Another incident made Pugh suspect that there was something “human” (as he put it) about this dog. The farmer had a few cows and a fine young bull. Sirius had once been prodded by a cow, and he had heard alarming stories about bulls. From time to time a cow was brought from one or other of the neighbouring farms to be served by the Caer Blai bull. On these occasions the dogs had to enter the paddock, round up the bull, and bring it down the lane into the farmyard for its love-making. When the deed had been done, the dogs drove the bull back to its paddock. Sirius was always very nervous, and did his stuff very badly. Idwal would face the bull with fierce persistence, and slip away from its lowered horns in the nick of time; but Sirius was far too anxious to keep his distance. The bull discovered that Sirius was a coward, and formed a habit of chasing him.</p>
<p>Pugh, by the way, was struck with the different ways in which the two dogs behaved when bull and cow were brought together in the farmyard, generally surrounded by a small group of interested men and boys, while the women kept discreetly indoors. Idwal nosed about the yard or lay down to rest. Sirius watched the whole performance with the same cheerful interest as the human spectators. It was evident that his interest was sexual, for when the bull effected his clumsy embrace, the dog himself gave unmistakable signs of sexual excitement.</p>
<p>But the incident which impressed Pugh most, and made him suspect that Sirius’s intelligence was as quick as a man’s, occurred in connection with the bull’s habit of taking the offensive against the cowardly Sirius. Pugh had gone to the village with Idwal. Owen, the hired man, was ploughing in a remote field. Somehow the bull managed to break out of its paddock into the lane. It trotted down into the yard, saw Jane with a basket of washing, and approached her, snorting. Always a nervous girl, she screamed, dropped the basket and slipped into the stable. The bull spent a few minutes tossing the clothes, then made off down the lane. Meanwhile Mrs. Pugh had made a tentative sortie from the house. Then Sirius appeared, and raced down the lane after the bull. He did not overtake it till after it had reached the main road. Then he silently rushed at it, and seized its tail. With a roar the bull swung round, but Sirius had let go, and was retreating towards the lane, barking. The bull followed him, and he led it back to the farmyard and into its field. It was now rather blown, but Sirius led it round and round the field, till its ardour was cooled. The less anxious it was to follow him, the more bold grew Sirius. When it came to a standstill, he rushed in and nipped its hind leg. The reinfuriated beast chased him once more, but was soon exhausted. This process was repeated several times, till Sirius noticed that the two women had put some strands of barbed wire across the gap in the hedge. Then he retreated, with a proud tail, leaving a bull that was thoroughly cowed. Henceforth Sirius was always able to deal with the bull or any other cattle.</p>
<p>Some time after this incident Sirius did something which was far beyond super-sheep-dog capacity.</p>
<p>Throughout his first term he was desperately lonely. He longed for his own people, and most of all for Plaxy. If only he could write a letter! But he had no writing-glove, and no stationery. And anyhow the task of putting a stamp on a letter had always defeated him.</p>
<p>He knew he could write a few words very badly by holding a pencil in his mouth; if he could find one, and paper. He had once seen Pugh take out pen, ink and paper from the drawer in the oak dresser. One day, when Mrs. Pugh and Jane were milking, he slipped into the kitchen, opened the drawer, and found in it several sheets of paper, also envelopes, a pen, an ink-pot, and a pencil with a broken point. He stole a sheet and an envelope. Pen and ink seemed too complicated, and the pencil was useless, so he left these, and took merely an envelope and paper to the dog’s outhouse. He put them in an old packing case under some straw.</p>
<p>There was now nothing for it but to wait until someone should need to sharpen the pencil. He seized every opportunity to sneak into the kitchen and look into the drawer. Meanwhile he spent much thought in planning exactly how he would write his letter, and what he would say. Sometimes he practised. Holding a splinter of slate in his mouth, he scribbled on the slate doorstep. The process was difficult, because his nose was always in the way, and he could not see what he was doing; and generally the slate-splinter snapped.</p>
<p>At last, after many days, he found that the pencil had been sharpened. He took it away to the outhouse.</p>
<p>Not till several days later did Sirius find an opportunity of writing his letter. In spidery capitals it said, “Dear Plaxy, I hope you are happy. I am lonely without you, terribly. Love, Sirius.” With great care he addressed the envelope, hoping that his memory was trustworthy. He had serious difficulty in folding the paper and putting it into the envelope. Then he licked the gummy-edge, closed it, and held his paw on it. He had intended to post it unstamped; but the thought that Plaxy would have to pay threepence, double postage, on it distressed him so much that he decided to wait in the hope of finding some stamps in the drawer. When at last a sheet of six three-halfpenny stamps appeared, he ran off with them and set about trying to detach one. First he held the sheet between his paws and pulled with his teeth. The stamp tore across the middle, and the bit in his mouth stuck to his wet teeth, so that he could not get rid of it. Flustered by this experience, he decided to think the problem out more carefully. He hit on a plan. He held the envelope down with his paw and licked the right-hand top corner. Then with extreme care he took up the stamp-sheet in his teeth and laid it on the envelope so that one of the corner stamps was roughly in the right position. This was difficult, because as usual his nose was in the line of sight. He let go, and inspected the result. The stamp was crooked, and not wholly on the envelope. Hastily he lifted it off again and replaced it, well on the envelope. Again he inspected it, then carefully pulled it into better position. Then he pressed it on with his paw. When he thought the gum was dry, he held the sheet of stamps down with his paw and gently pulled the letter with his teeth. The letter came away, with the stamp intact, and part of the next one projecting over the edge. This he trimmed off with his teeth. He then restored the mangled sheet of stamps to the drawer. Not till he came back to the letter did he notice that the stamp was upside down.</p>
<p>He hid his letter under the straw and waited till his next errand to the village. This did not occur till some days later. It was fairly common for him to be sent with letters to the post, but on this occasion his own was the only one. He trotted off with his basket and an order for the grocer; and his letter. He went straight to the post office, put down his basket, took out the letter, raised himself against the wall, and slipped the precious document into the box.</p>
<p>This was no unusual sight in the village. Dr. Huw Williams, who was passing, scarcely noticed it; but when, next day, he met Mr. Pugh and wished him good day, he mentioned the incident, complimenting him on his dog’s intelligence. Now Pugh had sent off no letter that day. He wondered whether his wife had written to her mother in Bala, or if Jane had entrusted Bran with a love-letter. This possibility disturbed him, for though by nature a friendly man, prone to treat people with respect and trust, he was no modern parent. When he reached home he made inquiries. Mrs. Pugh and Jane both denied that they had given Bran a letter. Pugh went to the drawer and saw that the stamps had been badly mauled. One was missing, and two others were torn. In a burst of indignation he charged his daughter with clandestine correspondence, theft, lying and clumsiness. Jane defended herself with vigour, and added, “Go and ask Bran whose letter it was.” That sarcasm put a wild idea into Pugh’s head. He went to the drawer again and picked up the pencil. There were toothmarks on it — Bran’s or his own? Fantastic doubt!</p>
