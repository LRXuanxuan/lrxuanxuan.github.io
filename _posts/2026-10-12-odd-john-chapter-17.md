<h3>第十七章　恩贡科与洛</h3>
<p>还记得，我们已经预订了三个人搭乘东方航线客轮前往土伦，再去英国的船票。第三位同行者在开船前三小时才出现。</p>
<p>约翰解释说，他之所以能发现这个名叫恩贡科（Ng-Gunko）的惊人孩子，阿德兰帮了很大的忙。身处过去的老人曾同这个与约翰同时代的孩子建立过联系，又帮助两人彼此接通。</p>
<p>恩贡科来自阿比西尼亚境内或附近某片偏远的森林山地。虽然还只是个孩子，他却按照约翰的要求，经历了一连串我不打算详述的冒险，独自从故乡一路来到塞得港。</p>
<p>时间一点点过去，他却始终没有出现，我越来越怀疑，也越来越不耐烦；约翰却始终确信他会来。就在我费力合上船舱行李箱时，他突然出现在我们住的旅馆里。他是个又脏又怪的小黑人，我一想到接下来还得同他共住，就很不高兴。他看上去大约八岁，实际却已经十二岁以上。他穿着一件很长、蓝色而且脏得厉害的长袍，头上戴着一顶破旧菲斯帽。后来我们才知道，这些衣服是他旅途中为了减少别人注意而弄来的。然而，他根本不可能不引人注目。我第一眼看到他时，反应简直就是赤裸裸的不相信。“世上哪有这种东西。”我在心里说。随后我想起，一个物种发生突变时，经常会一口气产生许多荒诞得惊人的性状，其中不少新类型甚至根本无法存活。恩贡科当然活得很好，但他的确是个怪胎。他的脸色深暗，既有黑人特征，也有闪族特征，还明显带着某种蒙古人种的影子；可是他那一头黑人式的卷发却不是黑色，而是暗红色。他的右眼是一颗巨大的黑色眼球，配他深色的皮肤倒还不算突兀；左眼却小得多，虹膜还是深蓝色。这种极不协调的组合让他的整张脸带着一种阴森的滑稽感，而他的表情更强化了这种印象。他厚厚的嘴唇经常咧成怪笑，露出上面三颗、下面一颗小小的白牙。其余牙齿显然还没长出来。</p>
<p>恩贡科英语说得很流利，却错误百出，发音也十分粗野。这门外语是他沿尼罗河谷向下走的六周旅途中现学来的。等我们抵达伦敦时，他的英语已经同我们一样好了。</p>
<p>要把恩贡科收拾到适合登上一艘东方航线客轮的程度，实在是一项艰巨工程。我们把他从头到脚狠狠刷洗了一遍，又涂了杀虫剂。他腿上有好几处化脓的伤口。约翰把小刀上最锋利的一片刀刃消了毒，把腐坏的肉全部割掉。恩贡科躺着一动不动，却满头大汗，脸上不断挤出极其骇人的怪相，那表情竟同时表现出痛苦和觉得好玩。我们给他买了欧洲服装，他当然厌恶透顶；又给他拍了护照照片——约翰此前已经同埃及当局把证件办妥。最后，我们得意地把他带上船；他穿着崭新的白短裤和白衬衫。</p>
<p>整个航程里，我们一直忙着教他欧洲人的规矩。他不能当众挖鼻孔，更不能用最“天然”的办法擤鼻涕；不能直接用手抓肉和蔬菜；必须学会怎么使用浴室和抽水马桶；不能在不合适的地方解决大小便；哪怕只是个孩子，也绝不能赤身裸体地晃进挤满人的餐厅；不能让别人看出他聪明得过头；不能盯着其他乘客看。最重要的是，我们一再强调，他必须压住那种仿佛无法抗拒的冲动——拿别人恶作剧。</p>
<p>恩贡科虽然轻浮，智力却毫无疑问远超常人。比如，一个在森林里生活了十四年的孩子，竟能轻而易举理解蒸汽轮机的原理，还能向带我们参观机舱的轮机长提出一连串问题，把那个经验丰富的苏格兰老头问得直挠头，这实在令人吃惊。也正是在那次参观中，约翰不得不恶狠狠地低声警告这个小怪物：“你要是再不肯费点力气把你那该死的好奇心装进瓶子里，我就把你扔下海。”</p>
<p>回到我们北郊的住处后，恩贡科被安置进韦恩赖特家。为了避免他造成不必要的轰动，我们把他的头发染成黑色，又让他戴一副眼镜，其中一只镜片是深色的。只有在家里，他才能把眼镜摘掉。可惜他年纪实在太小，抵抗不了故意吓唬当地人的诱惑。跟约翰或我在街上走时，他会把自己裹得严严实实，只露出眼睛以适应这个陌生气候，老老实实戴着眼镜，一副规规矩矩的样子。可一旦前面出现某个老妇人或孩子，他有时就故意落后一步，然后突然把下巴从围巾上方伸出来，啪地摘下眼镜，做出一种充满仇恨的疯子怪笑。我不知道他有多少次这样干而没被抓住；不过有一次效果太好了，受害者当场尖叫起来。约翰猛地转身扑向自己的门生，一把掐住他的脖子。“你要再来一次，”他说，“我就把你那只眼睛整个挖出来，再一脚踩烂。”此后只要约翰在场，恩贡科再也没玩过这招。可跟我在一起时，他仍照玩不误，因为他知道我脾气太好，不会告他的状。</p>
<p>不过几周以后，恩贡科渐渐更认真地投入这场伟大冒险。那种阴谋活动的气氛很合他的胃口，而为自己将要承担的角色做准备，也逐渐占据了他的全部注意力。但骨子里，他仍然是个小野人。甚至连他对机器那种异乎寻常的狂热，也带着一种原始心灵第一次撞见我们文明奇迹时那种不加批判的喜悦。他拥有惊人的机械天赋，在某些方面甚至超过约翰。来到这里没几天，他就已经会骑摩托车，还能让它做出匪夷所思的“特技”。没多久，他又把整辆车拆开，再完整装了回去。他很快掌握了约翰那套心身动力装置的原理，并且狂喜地发现，那个装置最核心的“奇迹”自己也能做到。渐渐地，所有人都理所当然地认定，将来游艇以及殖民地的首席工程责任应由他承担，好让约翰腾出手来处理更高层次的事情。然而，恩贡科的一切行动、乃至他整个对生命的态度，都带着一种强烈甚至近乎激情的色彩，同约翰一贯的冷静极为不同。有时我甚至怀疑，在情感层面上他到底算不算真正的超常者；也许除了一流智力之外，他身上并没有别的特别之处。我把这个想法告诉约翰时，他笑了。“恩贡科还是个小孩，”他说，“不过他完全没问题。别的不说，他天生就有心灵感应的才能。等我稍微训练他一下，他也许会在这方面超过我。不过，我们两个现在都只是初学者。”</p>
<p>我们从埃及回来没多久，又有一个超常者到了。这就是约翰在莫斯科找到的那个女孩。和她的同类一样，她看起来比实际年龄小得多。她看上去还只是个没有跨进少女期的孩子，实际已经十七岁。她从家里逃出来，在一艘苏联轮船上找到侍应生的工作，然后趁船停靠英国港口时偷偷上岸。她在俄国已经弄到足够的英国货币，于是一路找到了韦恩赖特家。</p>
<p>乍看之下，洛比恩贡科或约翰都正常得多。她几乎可以被当作雅克利娜最小的妹妹。毫无疑问，她的头仍大得醒目，双眼在脸上占据的面积也超过常人；但五官很端正，乌黑顺滑的头发也足够长，可以梳成当时流行的齐短发。她显然具有亚洲血统：颧骨很高，那双虽然巨大的眼睛却深陷在半合、斜挑的眼睑里；鼻子宽而扁，像猿猴，肤色则明确属于所谓“黄色”。她让我想到一件忽然活过来的雕塑——仿佛艺术家用猫科动物的形式把“人”重新风格化了一遍。她的身体同样带着猫科动物的特征。“又瘦又松，”约翰这样形容，“摸上去仿佛一碰就会断，可其实全是钢弹簧，外面只松松包了一层天鹅绒。”</p>
<p>游艇起航前的几个星期里，洛住在从前属于约翰姐姐安妮的房间。她和帕克斯之间的关系从来谈不上轻松，却始终友善。洛极其沉默。我确信，沉默本身并不会困扰帕克斯，因为她通常反而容易喜欢上话少的人。可是一到洛面前，她似乎总觉得自己有义务说点什么，同时又怎么也无法自然地说话。无论她讲什么，洛都会作出得体、甚至友好的回应，可洛每说一句，气氛仿佛就变得更糟。只要洛在场，帕克斯便显得局促不安。她做事时会犯些愚蠢的小错：把东西放进错误的抽屉，把纽扣缝错地方，折断针，诸如此类。而且什么事都会比平时花更长时间。</p>
<p>我始终没有弄清，帕克斯为什么在洛身边会那么不自在。这个女孩确实让人难以应付，但按理说，帕克斯应该比其他人更有能力同她相处才对。真正扰人的不仅是洛的沉默，还有她脸上几乎完全没有表情——或者更准确地说，几乎没有表情变化；因为她这种“没有表情”的状态，本身反而极其鲜明地表现出一种对周围世界的深刻疏离。在任何普通社交场合，别人会表现出好笑、愉快或恼火，恩贡科更会把强烈情绪毫无遮掩地写在脸上；可洛的五官始终一动不动。</p>
<p>起初我以为她只是迟钝，甚至可能有点愚笨；但不久以后，她身上一件很奇特的事实证明我错了。她忽然疯狂迷上小说，而最爱的是简·奥斯汀。她把那位无与伦比的女作家全部作品一遍又一遍地读，读得次数多到约翰终于忍不住拿她开玩笑，因为他的兴趣完全在别的方向。这一下竟逼出了洛唯一一次长篇大论。“我来的地方，”她说，“没有任何东西像简·奥斯汀。但我自己里面有一点东西像她，而这些老书正在帮助我认识自己。当然，我知道它们毕竟只是‘智人’作品；可这反而有一半的乐趣。把这一切转置过来，让它适合我们，是件特别有意思的事。比如，我常问自己：假如简真能理解我——当然她不可能——她会怎样评价我？我发现这个问题的答案极有启发。我们的心灵当然完全超出她能够处理的范围，但她的态度却可以应用到我们身上。她面对自己那个小小世界时，那种聪明、轻快的态度，赋予了它一种那个世界自己永远无法发现的意义。好吧，我也想用一种‘简式’的眼光来看待我们，甚至来看待我们那高尚正直的殖民地。我想给它一种意义，而这种意义也许连它那认真、崇高的领袖本人都会漏掉。你知道吗，约翰，我觉得智人关于‘人格’这件事，恐怕还有相当多东西能教你。或者，如果你忙得没空学，那就得让我来学，不然这个殖民地会让人受不了。”</p>
<p>令我吃惊的是，约翰的回答竟是结结实实地吻了她一下。洛仍一本正经地说道：“怪约翰，你确实还有很多东西要学。”</p>
<p>这件事也许会让读者以为洛缺乏幽默感。事实并非如此。她其实有一种并不刻薄的机智天赋。她本人似乎完全不会微笑，却经常能把别人逗笑。然而，正如我说过的，她对我们大多数人而言始终有种神秘的压迫感。甚至约翰有时在她身边也会不自在。有一次，他正在向我交代一些财务上的事情，突然停下来，说：“那姑娘在笑我，明明脸上还是那副庄严样子。她从来不笑，可她又一直在笑。说吧，洛，你到底觉得什么这么好笑？”洛回答：“亲爱而重要的约翰，在我这里笑你的其实是你自己——你是在笑你映在我身上的倒影。”</p>
<p>洛在英国那几个星期最主要的任务，是掌握医学的科学与技术，同时熟悉胚胎学领域最先进的一切工作。她为什么要这样做，我直到很久以后才明白。她的专业训练一部分来自本地大学一位颇有名望的胚胎学家，对方给她进行高强度指导；另一部分则靠同约翰长时间讨论。</p>
<p>随着游艇即将完工、真正的冒险即将开始，洛的学习越来越苛刻。她开始显出透支的迹象。我们劝她休息几天。“不，”她说，“起航之前我必须把这件事学完。等上船以后我会休息。”我们问她睡眠是不是正常，她含糊其辞。约翰开始起疑。“你到底睡不睡觉？”他问。她犹豫了一下才说：“能不睡的话，我从来不睡。事实上，我上一次睡觉已经是好几年前了。那一次我睡了很久很久。不过只要我还能控制，以后永远不会再睡。”约翰难以置信地问“为什么？”她最初的回答只是打了个寒战，随后才像临时想到一个理由似的补充道：“浪费时间。我会躺上床，但整晚都在看书；或者只是思考。”</p>
<p>我记不清自己此前是否提过，其他所有超常者睡眠时间都很短。比如约翰，一晚上睡四小时就完全满足，而且连续三个夜晚彻底不睡，也不会有什么问题。</p>
<p>这件事过去几天后，我得知洛没有下楼吃早餐，而帕克斯发现她仍躺在床上睡着。“可这不对劲，”帕克斯说，“看着更像发作。她躺在那里，眼睛死死闭着，脸上不断掠过特别可怕的恐惧和愤怒表情；嘴里还一直嘟囔俄语还是什么东西，两只手不停地抓自己的胸口。”</p>
<p>我们试着叫醒她，却完全做不到。我们把她扶起来坐着，往她身上泼冷水，对她大喊，摇晃她，用针扎她，都毫无用处。到了当天晚上，她开始尖叫。那一夜，她断断续续叫了整整一晚。我留在韦恩赖特家，虽然什么忙也帮不上；可不知道为什么，我就是没法离开。整条街都被她吵得无法入睡。有时那只是毫无词义的凄厉尖叫，像一头因为痛苦与狂怒而彻底失控的动物；有时又会变成一大串俄语，以最大的声音喊出来，可含糊得连约翰都听不懂。</p>
<p>第二天早晨，她渐渐安静下来。此后一星期多，她一直一动不动地沉睡。某天早晨，她像什么也没发生过一样下楼吃早餐；只是按约翰的话说，她看起来“像一具被地狱来的灵魂驱动的尸体”。坐下以后，她对约翰说：“现在你明白了吧，为什么我喜欢简·奥斯汀，比如说，比陀思妥耶夫斯基更喜欢？”</p>
<p>她花了相当长时间才恢复体力，也重新找回平常那种冷静自持。有一天，她已经重新开始工作，便向帕克斯讲了一点自己的往事。很久以前，在她尚处婴儿期、革命还没有发生的时候，他们一家住在乌拉尔山以东的一座小城。那时她每晚都会睡觉，可也经常做噩梦。她说，那些梦可怕到极点，而且完全无法用任何正常经验来描述。她唯一能说的是，在梦中，她会感到自己变成一头发疯的野兽，或者一个魔鬼；可在内心深处，她始终还是那个清醒的小小自我，只能毫无力量地旁观自己的疯狂。随着年龄增长，这些幼年的恐怖逐渐离开了她。革命期间以及紧接着的几年里，她的家庭经历了内战和饥荒带来的巨大苦难。她外表仍只是个婴儿，精神上却已经完全能够理解周围事件的意义。比如，她早已形成一个判断：内战双方在残忍和慷慨方面都同样做得出来，可从总体精神上看，一方是对的，另一方是错的。即使在那么小的时候，她也已经模糊却坚定地感觉到，自己生活里的全部恐怖——炮击、火灾、集体处决、寒冷、饥饿——必须以某种方式被接受、被拥抱，而不是躲开。她以一种胜利者的姿态做到了这一点。可后来，白军洗劫了她的城市。她父亲被杀，母亲带着她逃上一列挤满伤员的难民列车。那趟旅程当然令人精疲力竭。洛终于睡着，再次坠入自己的噩梦；不同的是，这一次梦里挤满了内战的一切恐怖，而她被迫毫无力量地看着另一个自己犯下最骇人的暴行。</p>
<p>从那以后，任何巨大的压力都有可能把她拖入睡眠，也把她拖回那些恐怖之中。不过她说，这类发作如今已经少了很多；另一方面，梦的内容却变得更加可怕。因为——她自己也无法解释清楚——它越来越具有普遍性，越来越像某种形而上的东西，越来越带着宇宙尺度的意义；与此同时，它也越来越明确地表现出某种“撒旦式”的东西——这是她自己的用词——而那个东西竟就存在于她这个自我最深处。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a347"></span>CHAPTER XVII. NG-GUNKO AND LO</h3>
<p><span class="t28">I</span><span class="t31">T</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">WILL</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">BE</span><span class="t30"> </span>remembered that we booked passages for three persons by Orient to Toulon and England. The third member of the party turned up three hours before the ship sailed.</p>
<p>John explained that in discovering this amazing child, who went by the name of Ng-Gunko, he had been helped by Adlan. The old man in the past had been in touch with this contemporary of John’s, and had helped the two to make contact with one another.</p>
<p>Ng-Gunko was a native of some remote patch of forest-clad mountain in or near Abyssinia; and though only a child he had at John’s request found his way from his native country to Port Saïd by a series of adventures which I will not attempt to describe.</p>
<p>As time advanced and he failed to appear, I became more and more sceptical and impatient, but John was confident that he would arrive. He turned up at our hotel as I was trying to shut my cabin trunk. He was a grotesque and filthy little blackamoor, and I resented the prospect of sharing accommodation with him. He appeared to be about eight years old, but was in fact over twelve. He wore a long, blue and very grubby caftan and a battered fez. These clothes, we subsequently learned, he had acquired on his journey, in order to attract less attention. But he could not help attracting attention. My own first reaction to his appearance was frank incredulity. “There ain’t no such beast,” I said to myself. Then I remembered, that, when a species mutates, it often produces a large crop of characters so fantastic that many of the new types are not even viable. Ng-Gunko was decidedly viable, but he was a freak. Though his face was a dark blend of the Negroid and the Semitic with an unmistakable reminiscence of the Mongolian, his Negroid wool was not black but sombre red. And though his right eye was a huge black orb not inappropriate to his dark complexion, his left eye was considerably smaller, and the iris was deep blue. These discrepancies gave his whole face a sinister comicality which was borne out by his expression. His full lips were frequently stretched in a grin which revealed three small white teeth above and one below. The rest had apparently not yet sprouted.</p>
<p>Ng-Gunko spoke English fluently but incorrectly, and with an uncouth pronunciation. He had picked up this foreign tongue on his six-weeks’ journey down the Nile valley. By the time we reached London his English was as good as our own.</p>
<p>The task of making Ng-Gunko fit for a trip on an Orient liner was arduous. We scrubbed him all over and applied insecticide. On his legs there were several festering sores. John sterilized the sharpest blade of his penknife and cut away all the bad flesh, while Ng-Gunko lay perfectly still, but sweating, and pulling the most hideous grimaces, which expressed at once torture and amusement. We purchased European clothes, which, of course, he detested. We had him photographed for his passport, which John had already arranged with the Egyptian authorities. In triumph we took him off to the ship in his new white shorts and shirt.</p>
<p>Throughout the voyage we were busy helping him to acquire European ways. He must not pick his nose in public, still less blow it in the natural manner. He must not take hold of his meat and vegetables with his hands. He had to acquire the technique of the bathroom and the watercloset. He must not relieve himself in inappropriate places. He must not, though a mere child, saunter into the crowded dining-saloon without his clothes. He must not give evidence that he was excessively intelligent. He must not stare at his fellow passengers. Above all, he must, we said, restrain his apparently irresistible impulse to play practical jokes on them.</p>
<p>Though frivolous, Ng-Gunko was certainly of superior intelligence. It was, for instance, remarkable that a child who had lived his fourteen years in the forest should easily grasp the principle of the steam turbine, and should be able to ask the chief engineer (who showed us round the engine room) questions which made that experienced old Scot scratch his head. It was on this expedition that John had to whisper fiercely to the little monster, “If you don’t take the trouble to bottle up your blasted curiosity, I’ll pitch you overboard.”</p>
<p>When we reached our northern suburb Ng-Gunko was installed in the Wainwright household. As we did not want him to cause more of a sensation than need be, we dyed his hair black and made him wear spectacles with a dark glass for one eye. Only in the house might he be without them. Unfortunately he was too young to be able to resist the temptation of startling the natives. Walking along the street with John or me, muffled to the eyes against the alien climate, duly spectacled and demure, he would sometimes drop a pace behind as we were approaching some old lady or child. Then, projecting his chin above his scarf, he would whip off his glasses and assume a maniacal grin of hate. How often he did this without being caught I do not know, but on one occasion he was so successful that the victim let out a scream. John turned upon his protégé and seized him by the throat. “Do that again,” he said, “and I’ll have that eye of yours right out, and step on it.” Never again did Ng-Gunko play the trick when John was present. But with me he did, knowing I was too amiable to report him.</p>
<p>In a few weeks, however, Ng-Gunko began to enter more seriously into the spirit of the great adventure. The conspiratorial atmosphere appealed to him. And the task of preparing himself to play his part gradually absorbed his attention. But he remained at heart a little savage. Even his extraordinary passion for machinery suggested the uncritical delight of the primitive mind in its first encounter with the marvels of our civilization. He had a mechanical gift which in some ways eclipsed even John’s. Within a few days of his arrival he was riding the motor-bicycle and making it perform incredible “stunts.” Very soon he took it to pieces and put it together again. He mastered the principles of John’s psycho-physical power unit, and found, to his intense delight, that he could perform the essential miracle of it himself. It began to be taken for granted that he would be the responsible engineer of the yacht, and of the future colony, leaving John free for more exalted matters. Yet in all Ng-Gunko’s actions, and in his whole attitude to life, there was an intensity and even a passion which was very different from John’s invariable calm. Indeed I sometimes wondered whether he was emotionally a true supernormal, whether he had anything unusual in his nature beyond brilliant intelligence. But when I suggested this to John he laughed. “Ng-Gunko’s a kid,” he said, “but Ng-Gunko’s all right. Amongst other things he has a natural gift for telepathy, and when I have trained him a bit he may beat me in that direction. But we are both beginners.”</p>
<p>Not long after our return from Egypt another supernormal arrived. This was the girl whom John had found in Moscow. Like others of her kind, she looked much younger than she was. She seemed a child, not yet on the threshold of womanhood, but was actually seventeen. She had run away from home, taken a job as stewardess on a Soviet steamer, and slipped ashore at an English port. Thence, equipped with a sufficiency of English money, which she had secured in Russia, she had found her way to the Wainwrights.</p>
<p>Lo was at first glance a much more normal creature than either Ng-Gunko or John. She might have been Jacqueline’s youngest sister. No doubt her head was strikingly large, and her eyes occupied more of her face than was normal, but her features were regular, and her sleek black hair was long enough to pass for a “shingle.” She was clearly of Asiatic origin, for her cheek bones were high, and her eyes, though great, were deeply sunk within their half-closed and slanting lids. Her nose was broad and flat, like an ape’s, her complexion definitely “yellow.” She suggested to me a piece of sculpture come to life, something in which the artist had stylized the human in terms of the feline. Her body, too, was feline, “so lean and loose,” said John. “It feels breakable, and yet it’s all steel springs covered with loose velvet.”</p>
<p>During the few weeks which passed before the sailing of the yacht, Lo occupied the room which had once belonged to Anne, John’s sister. Relations between her and Pax were never easy, yet always amicable. Lo was exceptionally silent. This, I am sure, would not trouble Pax, for she was generally drawn to silent persons. Yet with Lo she seemed to feel constantly an obligation to talk, and an inability to talk naturally. To all her remarks Lo would reply appropriately, even amiably, yet whatever she said seemed to make matters worse. Whenever Lo was present, Pax would seem ill at ease. She would make silly little mistakes in her work, putting things into wrong drawers, sewing buttons on in the wrong place, breaking her needle, and so on. And everything took longer than it should.</p>
<p>I never discovered why Pax was so uncomfortable with Lo. The girl was, indeed, a disconcerting person, but I should have expected Pax to be more, not less, able to cope with her than others were. It was not only Lo’s silence that was so disturbing, but also her almost complete lack of facial expression, or rather of changes of expression, for her very absence of expression was itself expressive of a profound detachment from the world around her. In all ordinary social situations, when others would show amusement or pleasure or exasperation, and Ng-Gunko would register intense emotion, Lo’s features remained unmoved.</p>
<p>At first I imagined that she was simply insensitive, perhaps dull-witted; but one curious fact about her soon proved that I was wrong. She discovered a passion for the novel, and most of all for Jane Austen. She read all the works of that incomparable authoress over and over again, indeed so often that John, whose interest ran in very different channels, began to chaff her. This roused her to deliver her one long speech. “Where I come from,” she said, “there is nothing like Jane Austen. But in me there is something like that, and these old books are helping me to know myself. Of course, they are only ‘sapient,’ I know; but that is half the fun. It’s so interesting to transpose it all to suit us. For instance, if Jane could understand me, which she couldn’t, what, I ask myself, would she say about me? I find the answer extraordinarily enlightening. Of course, our minds are quite outside her range, but her attitude can be applied to us. Her attitude to her little world is so intelligent and sprightly that it gives it a significance that it could never have discovered in itself. Well, I want to regard even us, even our virtuous Colony, in a Jane-like manner. I want to give it a kind of significance that would have remained hidden even from its earnest and noble leader. You know, John, I fancy Homo sapiens has still quite a lot to teach you about personality. Or if you are too busy to learn, then I must, or the colony will be intolerable.”</p>
<p>To my surprise John replied by giving her a hearty kiss, and she remarked, demurely, “Odd John, you have indeed a lot to learn.”</p>
<p>This incident may suggest to the reader that Lo was lacking in humour. She was not. Indeed she had a gift of not unkindly wit. Though she seemed incapable of smiling, she often roused others to laughter. And yet, as I say, she was mysteriously disconcerting to most of us. Even John was sometimes uncomfortable in her presence. Once when he was giving me some instructions about finance he broke off to say, “That girl’s laughing at me, in spite of her solemn face. She never laughs at all, and yet she’s always laughing. Now tell me, Lo, what’s amusing you.” Lo replied, “Dear and important John, it is you who are laughing, at your own reflection in me.”</p>
<p>Lo’s chief occupation during her few weeks in England was to master the science and art of medicine, and to make herself acquainted with all the most advanced work on the subject of embryology. The reason for this I did not learn till much later. Her vocational training she pursued partly by means of an intensive study under an embryologist of some distinction at the local university, partly by prolonged discussion with John.</p>
<p>As the time approached when the yacht was to be ready and the adventure to begin, Lo’s studies became more and more exacting. She began to show signs of strain. We urged her to take a holiday for a few days. “No,” she said, “I must get to the end of this business before we sail. Then I will rest.” We asked if she was sleeping all right. She was evasive. John became suspicious. “Do you sleep, ever?” he asked. She hesitated, then replied, “Not ever, if I can help it. In fact it is some years since I last slept. And then I slept for ages. But I will never sleep again if I can help it.” Her first answer to John’s incredulous “Why?” was a shudder; then she added as an afterthought, “It is a waste of time. I do go to bed, but I read all night; or just think.”</p>
<p>I forget whether I mentioned that all the other supernormals were brief sleepers. John, for instance, was satisfied with four hours a night, and could comfortably do entirely without sleep for three nights at a stretch.</p>
<p>A few days after this incident I learned that Lo had not come down to breakfast, and that Pax had found her still in bed, and asleep. “But it’s all wrong,” said Pax. “It’s more like a fit. She’s lying there with her eyes tight shut and awful expressions of horror and rage passing over her face; and she keeps muttering Russian or something, and her hands keep clawing at her chest.”</p>
<p>We tried to wake her, but could not. We sat her upright. We put cold water on her. We shouted at her. We shook her and pricked her, but it was no good. That evening she began to scream. She kept it up, off and on, all that night. I stayed with the Wainwrights, though I could do nothing. But somehow I couldn’t go. The whole street was kept awake. It was sometimes just an inarticulate screech like an animal beside itself with pain and fury, sometimes a torrent of Russian, shouted at the top of her voice, but so blurred that John could make nothing of it.</p>
<p>Next morning she quietened down, and for more than a week she slept without stirring. One morning she came down to breakfast as though nothing had happened, but looking, so John said, “like a corpse animated by a soul out of Hell.” As she sat down she said to John, “Now do you understand why I like Jane Austen, better for instance than Dostoievski?”</p>
<p>It took her some time to regain her strength and her normal equanimity. One day, when she had settled down to work again, she told Pax a bit about herself. Away back in her infancy, before the Revolution, when her people lived in a small town beyond the Urals, she used to sleep every night; but she often had bad dreams, which she said were extremely terrifying, and completely indescribable in terms of any normal experience. All she could say of them was that she felt herself turn into a mad beast or a devil, yet that inwardly she always remained her sane little self, an impotent spectator of her own madness. As she grew older, these infantile terrors left her. During the Revolution and the years immediately following it her family experienced terrible sufferings from civil war and famine. She was still in appearance an infant but mentally well able to appreciate the significance of events going on around her. She had, for instance, already reached a conviction that, though both sides in the civil war were equally capable of brutality and generosity, the spirit of the one was on the whole right, the other wrong. Even at that early age she felt, vaguely but with conviction, that the horror of her life, the bombardments, the fires, the mass executions, the cold, the hunger, must somehow be embraced, not shunned. Triumphantly she did embrace them. But there came a time when her town was sacked by the Whites. Her father was killed. Her mother fled with her in a refugee train crowded with wounded men and women. The journey was, of course, desperately fatiguing. Lo fell asleep, and was plunged once more into her nightmare, with the difference that it was now peopled with all the horrors of the civil war, and she herself was forced to watch impotently while her other self perpetrated the most hideous atrocities.</p>
<p>Ever since those days any great strain was liable to bring sleep upon her, with all its horrors. She reported, however, that the attacks were now much less frequent; but that on the other hand the content of her dreams was more terrible, because — she couldn’t properly explain — because it was more universal, more metaphysical, more cosmically significant, and at the same time more definitely an expression of something Satanic (her own word) within her very self.</p>
