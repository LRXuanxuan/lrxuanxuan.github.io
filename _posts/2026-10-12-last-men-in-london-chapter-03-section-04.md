<h2>iv. 保罗不断变化的世界</h2>
<p>在学生时代，保罗承受的成长痛苦比通常更多，因为他的心灵不仅在正常影响下迅速向上生长，也同时受到异常影响的推动。他不只是逐渐对玩具和儿童兴趣失去兴趣；他正在长出学校生活强加给他的整个世界。就像一只已经大得装不进旧壳的螃蟹，必须费力从里面挣脱出来，保罗如今也已经大得装不进自己的世界。那个世界在他旺盛生命活动的压力下裂开了，他正从裂口里往外钻，身上只有一层柔软、脆弱，却也灵活而具有扩张能力的新外壳。总有一天，这层新壳会硬化，成为一个比原来宽广得多的栖居之所。</p>
<p>学校里有两股彼此冲突的力量主宰着他。一股冲动要求他套上那套刻板的“学生”性格，舒舒服服生活在学生的世界里；与之相反的另一股冲动，则逼着他去寻找真正的自己，也寻找真正的现实。他努力按照学生社会的伦理去生活，一部分因为确实欣赏这种伦理，一部分也是为了像周围环境一样获得保护色。但他始终无法真正成功。体内运作的力量太强，根本不肯允许他彻底适应。早期学校生活中，保罗并没有清楚意识到，自己正在同时努力过两种彼此不相容的生活。他不知道：就在自己认真尝试适应学校宇宙的同时，也正在设法把这个宇宙撑裂，并让心灵不断扩张的组织长出一个全新的宇宙来容纳自己。</p>
<p>这种成长——或者更准确地说，这场深刻的变态发育——受若干种经验控制，而这些经验后来都对他产生了持久作用。可以说，它们是一系列“重新组织”的中心；围绕这些中心，幼虫状态的保罗一点一点变成成年的成虫。这些具有种子作用的经验大体分成两类，虽然保罗本人从未清楚区分过它们。他的注意力会被两类在世界中无处不在的事实牢牢攫住，并受到刺激，产生兴奋而强烈的敬佩：第一，是物理事件的精密复杂与毫不留情；第二，是生物结构的完善，或者更广泛地说，各种功能上的完善。</p>
<p>保罗很早便产生了对物理宇宙那种严峻性的感觉。还在育儿室时，他就已经对自然事件的机械运行产生一种自己也无法理解的敬意。这股自发朝向“自然”的虔敬冲动，后来不仅发展成强烈的科学兴趣，还发展出一种奇异而振奋的敬畏；从理性上看，这份敬畏似乎根本没有充分理由。</p>
<p>最早让他注意到山间湖泊上交叉波系的人，是他的父亲。父亲用富有感染力的描述，使他感觉整个物理世界在某种意义上也像一片湖水，被无数相互交叉的波纹布满：有大有小，有快有慢。这次看似微不足道、后来却意义重大的经验，发生在他们到威尔士高地度假期间。当时父子二人站在一处峭壁上，俯瞰灰色的湖水。他们数出了五套彼此不同的波，有些短促锐利，有些宽阔而微弱；水面上偶尔还出现短暂的“猫爪纹”，进一步把整个图样扰乱。</p>
<p>父子俩又走到湖泊背风、较为平静的一侧，观察那里的缓慢起伏。保罗带着一种近乎亵渎神圣的感觉，把手杖伸进水中搅动，于是一圈又一圈波纹向外扩散。父亲说：“你自己就是这样的东西——水被搅动，于是波纹向整个世界传出去。等搅动停止，波纹也就没有了。”他们离开湖边时，又谈起光、声音、起伏的天空，以及作为巨大波源的太阳。于是，这位富于想象的业余爱好者，竟凭一次幸福的猜测，预先触碰到了后来构成第一人类物理学最高成就之一的“波动力学”。</p>
<p>保罗由此得知，无论自己的身体还可以被怎样描述，至少它肯定也是一团波动：复杂到难以想象，却并不比湖面上的波纹缺少秩序。对这条新知识，他理解得很混乱，却受到极大震撼。它给了他一种感觉：存在既极其精微，又不可避免。甚至自己的身体竟然也是这种东西，在他看来既奇怪，又非常美。然而几乎刚一听明白，他便问：“如果我的身体全是波，那我自己在哪里？是我制造这些波，还是这些波制造了我？”父亲回答得信心十足，却并不怎么清楚：“你就是这些波。真正搅动它们的是上帝。”</p>
<p>这次经验终生留在保罗心中。后来，它成了他理解一切物理科学的原型，同时也成了生命之谜的一个缩影。成年以后，他每次碰到平静水面，常会停下来故意破坏它的宁静。这个小动作在他看来隐约有些不敬，却又同时具有创造意味。他会低声念叨：“上帝搅动水面。”有时，他会抓起一把小石子，从不同方向一颗接一颗扔进池塘；然后一动不动站着，看那些交叉圆环不断扩大、变淡、消失，直到水面终于完全恢复平静。</p>
<p>第一次透过父亲那架并不高级的望远镜看月亮，也是一次难忘经验。在此之前，他已经见过一艘船逐渐沉到地平线以下，也听说过地球和月球都是圆的。然而，真正亲眼看见圆滚滚的月亮——不再是一枚贴在天空上的扁平白先令，而是一个遥远、布满山岭的世界——这种震撼此后一生都没有减弱。保罗后来无论什么时候，只要有望远镜或双筒镜可用，都愿意一连十分钟凝视月球昼夜交界附近那些明亮峰顶和漆黑山谷。</p>
<p>这个景象对他的支配力极其惊人，而他本人根本无法用理性说明原因。它会在最普通的学生生活中突然闪回，产生令人困惑、甚至具有摧毁性的效果：可能出现在拉丁文课上，晨祷时，板球比赛中，或者一个下流笑话说到一半的时候。在这类经验影响下，他开始狼吞虎咽地读通俗天文学书籍。过去毫无意义的学校地图册第一页，也忽然让他用全新的眼光去看。学生的小宇宙很快不再等同于整个真实宇宙，而缩小成一幅极不完善的图，只描绘了真实宇宙的微不足道一角；而那个真正宇宙里遍布恒星与有人居住的世界。在这个阶段，保罗还想当然地认为，每颗恒星周围大概都有十来颗人口繁盛的行星。</p>
<p>让他不解的是，大多数成年人虽然完全相信类似的宇宙，却似乎根本不知道该拿这知识怎么办。对保罗而言意义重大得惊人的东西，在他们那里不是令人厌烦，就是令人害怕。甚至父亲——正是帮助他发现这个新世界的人——似乎也没有给予它应有的重视。当然，父亲确实觉得宇宙非常奇妙，会称它为“崇高”；但对他来说，它终究只是一个“崇高却无关紧要”的东西。它会抓住他的注意，也会在某种程度上引起赞叹；然而每次谈起这类问题，他语气里都带着一种隐藏的迟疑，甚至近乎不满。虽然对科学兴趣浓厚，他似乎还是在《伊利亚特》或者《仙后》那类世界里活得更自在、更像回家。儿子则相反；尽管努力欣赏这些梦境般的文学世界，却始终无法真正被它们打动。而群星却能给他一种强烈的振奋；一旦他试着向父亲解释为什么，理由便会变得——或者至少听起来——完全不合逻辑。</p>
<p>还有另一个压倒性的事实，也逐渐进入童年保罗的视野；一部分借助父亲，一部分靠他自己的直觉。后来，他把它称作“所有活物的活着”。他小时候最亲密的伙伴是一只梗犬。保罗常偷偷和它分享自己的黄油面包，一口给自己，一口给狗。当然，他那时以为这只动物的心灵远比实际上更像自己；但长期同它相处，也确实教会了他怎样在想象中同一种非人的心灵产生共感。这段犬类友谊在他心中深深刻下了两种感觉：所有生命彼此亲缘相通，同时又彼此不同。</p>
<p>杰克可以一起疯跑玩闹，却怎么也不肯陪保罗玩火车；更无法被说服相信兔子和自己一样是生命，应该尊重它们的生存。这个道理当然还得由保罗自己慢慢发现。不过，即便在最早那段折磨苍蝇、踩死甲虫的时期，他其实已经开始发现了。后来有一个阶段，其他生命那种陌生的神秘性，几乎成了他最主要的兴趣，也成了最主要的困惑。他会看蚂蚁在草丛构成的丛林中艰难运输食物，搬进公共储藏地；会看它们在“道路”上相遇时彼此“交谈”；也会看它们在有组织的战斗中一直打到死亡。当然，他始终把太多属于人的智力投射到这些奇异生物身上。不过，哪怕很久以后已经知道自己犯了这种错误，他仍然坚定地相信，自己和它们在最根本意义上属于同一个共同体。</p>
<p>甚至那些用盲目的头端在土里摸索前进的蚯蚓，在保罗看来也比它们吞食的泥土无限更像自己。可当一条蚯蚓被画眉一下叼走，他又会陷进极其痛苦的犹豫：究竟应该救下蚯蚓，还是让鸟安安静静吃自己的晚饭？看见猫抓住老鼠，他会愤怒地冲过去营救；可事后又忍不住怀疑，自己是不是只不过多管闲事。</p>
<p>总而言之，在成长的这一阶段，保罗被一种感觉彻底压倒：有人也许会把它叫作对一切生命“内在存在”的神秘主义式直觉。他同时又被另一事实震惊：生命彼此之间如此迟钝、如此缺少感受，而且从根本上说，总在相互伤害。他因为自己吃肉、穿皮靴而不安；更令他惊讶的是，许多非常善良的人明明溺爱自己的宠物，却做着同样的事，而且毫不犹豫。更使他痛苦的是，他自己体内仍保留着一小股真正的施虐冲动。有一阵，他会抓黄蜂，把它们切成两段，只为了观察两部分各自怎样奇异地活动。</p>
<p>更严重的，是他对猎鹿的反应。保罗极少进入所谓运动生活，而且这些经历直到在学校最后一年才开始，大学时期便结束了。父亲有一位家境富裕、很受尊敬的朋友，有时会邀请保罗到他位于德文郡的家里度过一部分暑假。这人教会保罗骑马，而且一心想把他培养成一个真正爱运动的绅士。等保罗的骑术被认为已经合格以后，便被带出去跟猎犬追逐鹿群。</p>
<p>一路上，他怀着令人作呕的罪恶感，怀着对自己糟糕骑术同样令人作呕的羞耻，又夹杂着对其他猎手令人作呕的崇拜和轻蔑，任凭马匹把自己一路颠过荒地和灌木丛，穿过乡间小路和村庄。出人意料的是，最后他竟真的赶上了猎杀现场。那头鹿终于就在眼前：有时下巴以下都泡在河水里；有时在村庄街道某间附属建筑的屋顶上打滑；有时则更庄严地被逼到两道树篱形成的角落里负隅顽抗。</p>
<p>而这时，保罗竟会兴奋欢呼，哪怕同时又因羞耻和怜悯恶心得发冷。他不明白自己为什么要这样做。为什么站在那里，看着那头已经精疲力竭的巨大动物被猎犬撕咬，而猎人们从后面寻找机会抓住它一只鹿角？为什么刀真正刺进去的那一瞬间，他竟会有短短一刹那突然感到快意和胜利？为什么当鹿被剖腹、猎犬吵嚷着等待分食时，他站在马头旁边嚼蛋糕和苹果，心里却那么焦虑地感到自我满足？为什么自己如此渴望被这些爱运动的绅士——这些长得太大的男学生——视为同类？</p>
<p>这些问题，保罗始终没有答案。显然，他那尚处幼虫阶段的天性正在拼命拒绝我强行灌给他的更发达心态。尽管自己在这类运动中笨拙无能，他对那种英国绅士型猎手却不仅怀有尊敬和羡慕，还感到一种深刻亲缘。于是，当他对原始猎人、战士和贵族产生反感时，他其实是在反对自己的一部分。几年以后，战争逼迫他在这两种忠诚之间作出一次最终选择时，折磨他的本质仍是同一个冲突。当然，在战争所造成的困惑中，那种原始动机又得到许多绝非原始的因素强化。</p>

<h2>iv. PAUL’S CHANGING WORLD</h2>
<p>DURING HIS SCHOOLDAYS Paul suffered more than the normal growing pains, for his mind was shooting upward in response not only to normal but to abnormal influences. He was outgrowing not only toys and childish interests; he was outgrowing the world which school life imposed upon him, Like a crab that has become too big for its shell and must struggle out of it, Paul was now too big for his world. It had cracked under the stress of his vital movement, and he was in the act of issuing from it in a soft vulnerable, but flexible and expansible covering, which some day would harden into a new and ampler dwelling-place.</p>
<p>Two conflicting influences dominated him at school, the impulse to assume the stereotyped’ schoolboy’ nature and live comfortably in the schoolboy world, and in opposition to this the impulse to find himself and to find reality. He strove to live according to the schoolboy ethic, partly because he genuinely admired it, partly so as to assume the protective colouring of his surroundings. But he could never succeed. The forces at work within him were too strong for him. In his early schooldays Paul never clearly understood that he was trying to live two inconsistent lives at once. He did not know that, while he was so earnestly trying to adjust himself to the school universe, he was also trying to burst that universe apart and grow an entirely new universe to suit the expanding tissues of his mind.</p>
<p>This growth, or rather this profound metamorphosis, was controlled by certain experiences which had an enduring effect on him. They were, so to speak, the centres of reorganization which, little by little, changed the larval Paul into the adult imago. These seminal experiences were of two kinds, though he did not clearly distinguish between them. His attention was arrested, and he was stung into excited admiration by two widespread facts: by the intricacy and relentlessness of physical happenings, and also by biological perfection, or at least functional perfection of every sort.</p>
<p>Paul’s sense of the austerity of the physical universe awoke early. Even in the nursery he had conceived an uncomprehending respect for the mechanism of natural events. This spontaneous movement of piety toward ‘nature’ developed not only into a strong scientific interest, but also into strangely exultant awe, for which there seemed to be no rational justification.</p>
<p>It was his father who first pointed out to him the crossing wave-trains of a mountain tarn, and by eloquent description made him feel that the whole physical world was in some manner a lake rippled by myriads of such crossing waves, great and small, swift and slow. This little significant experience took place during a holiday spent on the Welsh moors. They were standing on a crag overlooking the grey ‘llyn’. They counted five distinct systems of waves, some small and sharp, some broad and faint. There were also occasional brief ‘cat’s paws’ complicating the pattern. Father and son went down to the sheltered side of the lake and contemplated its more peaceful undulations. With a sense almost of sacrilege, Paul stirred the water with his stick, and sent ripple after ripple in widening circles. The father said, ‘That is what you are yourself, a stirring up of the water, so that waves spread across the world. When the stirring stops, there will be no more ripples.’ As they walked away, they discussed light and sound and the rippled sky, and the sun, great source of ripples. Thus did an imaginative amateur anticipate in a happy guess the’ wave mechanics’ which was to prove the crowning achievement of the physics of the First Men. Paul was given to understand that even his own body, whatever else it was, was certainly a turmoil of waves, inconceivably complex but no less orderly than the waves on the tarn. His apprehension of this novel information was confused but dramatic. It gave him a sense of the extreme subtlety and inevitability of existence. That even his own body should be of this nature seemed to him very strange but also very beautiful. Almost at the outset, however, he said, ‘If my body is all waves, where do I come in? Do I make the waves, or do the waves make me?’ To this the father answered, with more confidence than lucidity, ‘You are the waves. What stirs is God.’</p>
<p>This experience remained with Paul for ever. It became for him the paradigm of all physical sciences, and at the same time an epitome of the mystery of life. In his maturity he would often, when he came upon still water, pause to disturb its serenity. The little act would seem to him darkly impious yet also creative. He would mutter to himself, ‘God stirs the waters.’ Sometimes he would take a handful of little stones and throw them into the pond, one after the other in different directions. Then he would stand motionless, watching the intersecting circles spread and fade and die, till at last peace was wholly restored.</p>
<p>Paul’s first view of the moon through his father’s modest telescope was another memorable experience. He had already seen a ship disappearing below the horizon, and had been told that the earth and the moon were round. But actually to see the rotund moon, no longer as a flat white shilling, but as a distant world covered with mountains, was an experience whose fascination he never outgrew. Throughout his life he was ready to gaze for ten minutes at a time through telescope or field-glass at the bright summits and black valleys along the line between lunar night and day. This vision had a startling power over him which he himself could not rationally justify. It would flash mysteriously upon him with bewildering and even devastating effect in the midst of some schoolboy activity, in the midst of a Latin lesson, or morning prayers, or a game of cricket, or a smutty story. Under the influence of this experience he began to devour popular astronomy books. He looked with new eyes at the first page of the school atlas, which had hitherto meant nothing to him. Rapidly the school-boy universe ceased to be the whole real universe, and was reduced to a very faulty picture of a tiny comer of a universe which teemed with suns and inhabited worlds. For at this stage he imagined that every star was attended by a dozen or so populous planets.</p>
<p>He was perplexed to find that most adults, though many of them fully believed in some such universe, did nothing with the knowledge. That which to him was so significant was to them either tiresome or terrifying. Even his father, who had helped him to discover the new world, did not seem to appreciate it as it deserved. To the father it did indeed seem wonderful. He called it ‘sublime’. But for him it remained merely a sublime irrelevance. It compelled his attention, and in a manner his admiration also; but the tone of his voice, when he was talking of it, suggested a veiled reluctance, almost resentment. He seemed, in spite of all his scientific interest, to be happier and more at home in the world of the Iliad or of the ‘Faerie Queene ‘. The son, on the other hand, though he did his best to appreciate these dream worlds, was never moved by them. But the stars gave him an intense exhilaration, which, when he tried to justify it to his father, turned out to be, or to seem, wholly irrational.</p>
<p>Another overwhelming fact that gradually emerged into the child Paul’s ken, partly through the help of his father, partly through his own unaided apprehension, was what he came later to call’ the aliveness of all living things ‘. His boon companion of early days had been a terrier, with whom he used surreptitiously to share his bread and butter, bite by bite. Of course he believed that this creature had a mind much more like his own mind than was actually the case; but also, by long experience of this animal, he learned to enter into imaginative sympathy with a mind that was not human. This canine friendship drove deep into his own mind and heart both a sense of the kinship of all living things and a sense of their differences. Though Jack could enter into a romp, he could never be induced to play trains. Nor could he be persuaded that rabbits were beings like himself, whose lives should be respected. Paul himself, of course, had to make that discovery; but even in his earliest fly-tormenting and beetle-crushing phase he was already making it. Later there came a stage when this mystery of alien lives was his chief absorption and his chief perplexity. He would watch ants toiling through the grass-jungle with food for the public store, or ‘talking’ to one another as they met on one of their highways, or fighting to the death in organized battle. Always, of course, he imputed far too much of human intelligence to these strange beings. But even when at a much later date he discovered this error, he remained firm in his sense of their fundamental community with himself. Even worms, exploring the soil with their blind noses, seemed to him infinitely more like himself than the soil they devoured. And when one of them was snapped up by a thrush, Paul suffered an agony of indecision, debating whether to save the worm or let the bird have its dinner in peace. When he saw a cat with a mouse, he rushed indignantly to the rescue; yet somehow, after the event, he fell to wondering if he had merely been meddlesome.</p>
<p>In short, during this phase of his growth he was overwhelmed with what some would call a mystical apprehension of the inner being of all living things, and shocked by their insensitivity to one another, their essential harmfulness to one another. He was troubled by the fact that he himself ate meat and wore leather boots, and amazed that even kind-hearted persons, who doted on their own pets, did likewise without any hesitation. He was still more distressed by a streak of real sadism which survived in himself. At one time he used to catch wasps and cut them in two, so as to observe the strange behaviour of the parts. Worse still was his reaction to stag-hunting. His rare excursions into the world of sport did not occur until he was in his last year at school. They ceased while he was at the University. A well-to-do and much respected friend of his father sometimes invited Paul to spend part of the summer holidays at his house in Devon. He taught Paul to ride, and was determined to make a sportsman of him. When the lad was considered proficient, he was taken out to follow the hounds. With a sickening guiltiness, a sickening sense of his own bad horsemanship, with a sickening blend of adulation and contempt for the rest of the field, he let himself be bumped over moors and through covers, down lanes and through villages, till surprisingly he found himself in at the death. There at last was the stag, chin-deep in a river, or slithering on the roof of some outhouse in a village street, or more majestically at bay in the angle of two hedges. Then Paul exulted, even while he sickened with shame and pity. Why did he do it? he wondered. Why did he stand there watching the great weary beast mauled by the hounds, while the huntsmen tried to grab one of its antlers from behind? And when the knife went home, why did he feel, just for a fraction of a second, a sudden glee and triumph? Why was he so anxiously self-complacent as he stood at his horse’s head chewing cake and apples, while the stag was disembowelled and the hounds clamoured for their share? Why did he so desire to be taken for one of these sporting gentry, these overgrown schoolboys? These questions remained for Paul unanswered. Clearly his larval nature was making a desperate effort to reject the more developed mentality that I was forcing on him. In spite of his incompetence, he felt toward the sporting English gentleman not merely respect and envy but a deep kinship. In his revulsion against the primeval hunter and fighter, and aristocrat, he was divided against himself. Essentially the same conflict racked him a few years later when the war forced him to choose once and for all between the two allegiances. But of course in his war-perplexity, the primitive motive was reinforced by much that was by no means primitive.</p>
