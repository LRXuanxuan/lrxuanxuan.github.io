<h3>第六章　许多发明</h3>
<p>虽然当时我并不知道那起谋杀是约翰干的，但我确实注意到他发生了变化。他不再那么愿意同人谈话，以某种方式同朋友——无论孩子还是成年人——都更加疏远；与此同时，他又变得更体贴，甚至更温柔。我说“以某种方式”更加疏远，是因为他虽然越来越不愿谈论自己，也更喜欢独处，却仍然有很合群的时候。事实上，他可以成为一个极富同情心的伙伴，令人忍不住把各种秘密的希望和恐惧都告诉他——甚至有些希望和恐惧，自己平时都不肯承认。比如有一天，在约翰的存在以及我努力向他解释自己的过程中，我忽然发现，自己其实已经强烈地爱上了一个很像帕克斯的年轻女子；更进一步，我又发现，之所以此前一直没有承认这份感情，是因为内心存在一种模糊的、对约翰的忠诚感。发现自己对约翰的感情究竟有多强，反倒比发现自己对那个姑娘的感情更让我震惊。我当然知道自己深深关注约翰，可直到那一天，我才明白这个怪孩子究竟用怎样细密、怎样伸展深远的触手渗入了我的精神。</p>
<p>我的反应是一场激烈而颇带恐慌色彩的反叛。我故意在约翰面前炫耀那份刚刚发现、而且恰恰还是他替我指出来的正常异性吸引，同时嘲笑“我在心理上已经成了他的俘虏”这种说法。他回答：“那你可得小心。别为了我把自己的一辈子毁了。”同一个不到十岁的孩子这样谈话，实在古怪。更叫人不舒服的是，我感觉他了解我，比我了解自己还多。因为无论嘴上怎样否认，我心里知道他说得对。</p>
<p>现在回头看，我明白约翰之所以关心我的情况，一部分是出于对一种自己尚且无法体验的人际关系的好奇；一部分是因为对一个熟悉伙伴的直接感情；还有一部分，则是因为他既然打算为自己的目的利用我，就必须尽可能彻底地理解我。显然，他确实打算利用我，而且从来没有一秒钟真打算让我摆脱他。他希望我和那个像帕克斯的姑娘之间的关系继续发展并得到完成，不仅因为作为朋友，他站在我真正的需要一边；也因为如果我为了他而放弃这段关系，我最终就不会成为一个心甘情愿的奴仆，而会变成一个满怀怨恨的奴仆。我想，比起一头被链住、饿着肚子的狼，他更愿意由一条自由、可以四处游荡的猎犬为自己服务。</p>
<p>他对这个物种中具体个人的感情，是一种奇怪的混合物；而作为一个物种，他又发自内心地鄙视我们。他既轻蔑，又尊重；既疏离，又有感情。他鄙视我们的愚蠢和无能，却又尊重我们偶尔试图克服自身天生缺陷的努力。虽然他能冷静而超然地把我们用作自己的工具，可一旦命运或者我们自己的蠢行把我们拖进麻烦，他又可能以令人意外的谦卑和忠诚为我们效劳。</p>
<p>他逐渐增强的、同低等物种成员建立个人关系的能力，在他同一个六岁小女孩的非凡友谊中表现得最有趣。朱迪家离约翰家很近，而她早已把约翰视作自己的私人财产。他陪她玩闹得震天响，帮她爬树，教她游泳和滑旱冰；给她讲想象力放纵得没边的故事；耐心给她解释《漫画剪报》里那些可怜巴巴的笑话；还专门为了让朱迪开心，画战争和杀戮、海难和火山喷发。他修理她的玩具，视情况嘲笑她笨，或者夸她聪明。谁要是对她稍微不友善，约翰就立刻冲出来保护她。所有集体游戏里，大家都理所当然地认为约翰和朱迪必须在同一边。作为回报，她会又拉又扯地折腾他，笑他，骂他，叫他“笨笨的敦”，对他那些惊人的能力一点敬意也没有；同时又会把自己在学校“手工课”上做出来、最宝贝的那些成果统统送给他。</p>
<p>有一次我故意问约翰：“你为什么这么喜欢朱迪？”他立刻模仿她那种明显比同龄人幼稚的婴儿腔回答：“朱迪就是让人稀饭哒。不能不稀饭朱迪。”停了一会儿，他又说：“我喜欢朱迪，就像我喜欢海鸟。她做的都只是很简单的事，可每一件都做得有格调。她做‘朱迪’做得又彻底又完美，就像塘鹅做‘塘鹅’那么彻底、那么完美。如果她长大以后做成年人的事，也能像现在做小孩子的事这么好，那她就会很了不起。不过她做不到。等到了要做更难的事情时，我猜她也会像——像你们其他人一样，把自己的格调弄乱。挺可惜的。不过现在，她就是——朱迪。”</p>
<p>“那你自己呢？”我问，“你觉得自己长大以后不会失去格调吗？”</p>
<p>“我还没找到自己的格调。”他回答，“我还在摸索。我已经把一些事情搞得很糟了。不过等我真的找到——好吧，到时候就知道了。”接着，他出人意料地补充道，“当然，上帝看成年人，也许就像我看朱迪一样觉得有趣；因为我想，祂根本没打算让他们拥有比现实中更高明的格调。有时候我自己也能这样看他们。我能觉得，他们那些坏得要命的格调就是他们本身的一部分，而且看着竟然会有一种奇异的吸引力。不过我有一种感觉，上帝对我的期待不同。或者，把‘上帝’这个神话拿掉——我对自己的期待不同。”</p>
<p>谋杀发生几周以后，约翰忽然对一个极其家常的领域产生了惊人兴趣：如何管理一所住宅。他会一次花上一个小时，在女佣玛莎早晨干活时跟着她满屋走，或者观看厨房里的操作。为了逗她开心，他一路不停闲聊，内容是流言、粗俗笑话和拿她那些“男性朋友”开涮的混合物。帕克斯在食品储藏室或食物间工作，或者“收拾”房间、缝补衣服时，他也会进行同样细致的观察，只是谈话方式完全不同。有时他说着闲话会忽然停下来，问一句：“为什么不这样做？”玛莎对这些建议的反应视心情而定，从傲慢的鄙夷到勉强接受都有。帕克斯却每次都会认真考虑新办法，虽然开头偶尔会抗议：“我的办法已经够好用了，干吗还折腾？”但最后，她几乎总会采用约翰的改进，同时露出一种古怪的小笑容；那笑既可能是母亲的骄傲，也可能只是母亲对孩子的纵容。</p>
<p>约翰一点一点给家里引入了不少节省劳动的小装置：把挂钩或搁板挪到成年人手臂最自然能碰到的位置；改变煤斗的配重；重新整理食品间和浴室。他还试图把自己的方法推广到诊所，提出新的试管清洗方式、器械消毒方式以及药品存放方式。但试了几次以后，他就放弃了这条战线，因为按他的话说，“医生喜欢照自己的办法乱糟糟地凑合。”</p>
<p>两三个星期以后，约翰对家务经济的兴趣似乎消退了，只有遇到某个具体问题时才偶尔复发。这时他大多数时间都在外面，名义上是去海岸读书。但随着秋天越来越深，我们开始问他究竟怎么在外面保暖，他便显然又突然爱上了独自长途步行。他也花很多时间跑去邻近那座城市。“我要进城待一天，去看看几个我感兴趣的家伙。”他常常这样告诉我们；傍晚回来时，总是一脸疲惫，又沉浸在自己的思绪里。</p>
<p>到了那个冬天快结束时，约翰大约十岁半，终于把过去半年里一直占据他的那些惊人商业活动告诉了我。一个肮脏的星期日早晨，窗玻璃上糊满雨夹雪，他提议出去散步。我愤怒地拒绝。“走吧，”他坚持，“你会觉得挺有意思的。我想给你看看我的工作室。”说完，他慢吞吞地先眨了一下那只巨大的左眼，又眨了一下右眼。</p>
<p>等我们走到海岸时，我那件不中用的雨衣肩膀已经开始渗水，我一路咒骂约翰，也咒骂自己。我们踩着湿透的沙地往前走，来到一处地方：陡峭的黏土悬崖在那里变成一片斜坡，坡度几乎一样陡，却长满荆棘。约翰跪下来，四肢着地，沿灌木间一条窄道往上爬，还要我跟着。我这副比他大得多的身板要从他轻松钻过的地方挤过去，几乎是不可能的。才爬几码，我就彻底卡住，四面八方的刺都扎进身上。约翰看着我的惨样和咒骂哈哈大笑，随后转回来，用刀割开缠住我的枝条——毫无疑问，就是那把杀死警察的刀。又爬了十码，小路把我们带到陡坡上的一小块空地。终于能直起身以后，我抱怨：“这就是你说的工作室？”约翰笑了，说：“把那个抬起来。”他指的是一块锈迹斑斑的波纹铁皮，像废物一样躺在坡上，一头埋在一堆垃圾下面，露出的部分大约三英尺见方。我拽着自由的一端往上抬了两英寸，手指立刻被锈蚀的锋利断口划伤，只好咒骂着松手。“懒得弄，”我说，“你有本事就自己干脏活。”</p>
<p>“你当然懒得弄，”他回答，“任何偶然发现它的人也都会这样。”然后他把手伸到铁皮自由端两个角的下面，解开几根生锈铁丝。现在铁皮一下就能轻松抬起，像山坡上的一扇活板门翻开，露出三块大石之间的一个黑洞。约翰爬进去，叫我跟上；可在我勉强把身子塞进去以前，他还得先挪开一块石头。我发现自己进入了一个很低的洞穴，只靠约翰的手电照明。原来这就是工作室！看样子，它是直接从黏土斜坡中挖出来的，内壁抹着水泥。天花板铺满粗木板，又在几处用木柱支撑。</p>
<p>约翰点亮一盏嵌在外墙里的乙炔灯。把玻璃灯罩合上后，他说：“它用一根管子从外面进气，废气从另一根管子排出去。这个房间有独立的通风系统。”他指向墙上十来个圆孔，“排水管。”他说。这种管子在海岸一带很常见，是给田地排水用的；不断崩塌的悬崖也经常把它们暴露出来。</p>
<p>我蹲在那里，默默打量这个小洞窟，足足看了好几分钟。约翰则带着孩子气的得意笑容观察我。里面有一张工作台、一台小车床、一只喷灯，还有大量工具。后墙是一排搁板，上面乱七八糟堆着各种东西。约翰拿下一件递给我，说：“这是我早期做的小玩意之一——世界上最完美的绕毛线器。从此再也不需要副牧师帮忙了。教会末日！把绞纱套在这些叉齿上，再把线头塞进这条槽里，然后这么摇动杠杆，你就能得到一个毛线团，光滑得跟副牧师的脑袋一样。整个东西都是铝片和几根铝制毛衣针做的。”</p>
<p>“真他妈巧。”我说，“可这对你有什么用？”</p>
<p>“你这个傻瓜！我要给它申请专利，然后把专利卖掉。”</p>
<p>他又拿出一个很深的皮袋，说：“这是给男孩用的可拆卸、防撕裂裤袋；男人要是有脑子，也可以用。口袋本身像这样扣在这条L形带上，而你的所有裤子内衬里都牢牢缝着同样的带子。这样，所有裤子只用这一对口袋，换衣服时就不用每次把口袋掏空。妈妈也不用再补破洞。你也不会再把宝贝东西弄丢。口袋还能这样紧紧扣死。”</p>
<p>即使我对约翰这个惊人事业充满兴趣——我心里一再告诉自己，它既幼稚又辉煌——也无法阻止自己觉得浑身湿冷。我脱下滴水的雨衣，说：“冬天在这个洞里干活，你不会冷得要命吗？”</p>
<p>“我用这个给屋里加热。”他说着，转向一只小煤油炉，它的烟道绕着房间走了一圈，再穿墙出去。他点起炉子，把水壶放在上面：“来喝咖啡吧。”</p>
<p>随后，他递给我一个“专门清扫墙角的小玩意”。一根长长的管状手柄末端装着一把刷子，形状像一只巨大而钝的开瓶螺旋钻。只要把它朝难扫的墙角里一顶，刷子就会自动旋转。旋转运动来自一种让人想起自动铅笔的机构：刷子的实际转轴嵌在空心手柄内部的一条螺旋槽里。</p>
<p>“我现在做的这东西，也许比别的任何东西都更赚钱，不过光靠手工，连做出一两英寸都难得要死。”约翰这时给我看的东西，后来注定成为现代服装相关装置中最流行、最实用的一种。在欧洲和美洲，它已经繁衍出无数后代。事实上，约翰那些最巧妙、最赚钱的发明，几乎全都取得了如此巨大的成功，以至于今天几乎每个读者都一定熟悉其中每一种。我本可以一口气列出二十种；但由于涉及约翰家庭的一些私人原因，我必须克制。我只能说，除了他对道路交通器材作出的一项后来被全球采用的改良以外，他几乎完全专注于家庭和个人生活中的省力装置。约翰作为发明家最突出的特点，是他不仅偶尔能做出成功产品，而是能够稳定不断地产生“畅销品”。因此，如果我只描述几项次要成功和有趣失败，必然会严重歪曲他的天才。读者必须用自己的想象来补足这份贫乏记录。以后每当他使用现代舒适生活中任何一种构思精巧、效率出众的小器具时，不妨提醒自己：眼前这东西，很可能就是那个街头小鬼般的超人在地下巢穴里构想出的众多“小玩意”之一。</p>
<p>接下来一段时间，约翰继续给我展示他的发明。可以举出的有香芹切碎器、土豆削皮器，还有几种把旧剃刀片改作小刀、剪刀等用途的装置。再说一次，另一些东西则注定无人采用，或者永远流行不起来。其中最值得一提的，也许是一套用于在厕所里节省时间和麻烦、效率高得惊人的办法。就连约翰自己也对一些发明心存疑虑，其中便包括可拆卸口袋。“问题在于，”他说，“不管我的发明有多好，智人（Homo sapiens）的偏见都可能大到让他们根本不肯用。我看他们还是会死抱着那些该死的老口袋。”</p>
<p>水已经烧开，于是他冲了咖啡，又拿出一块帕克斯做的大蛋糕，分量相当豪迈。</p>
<p>我们一边喝、一边嚼，我问他这些设备都是怎么弄来的。“全都付过钱。”他说，“我意外弄到过一点钱。哪天再告诉你怎么来的。不过我需要多得多的钱，而且我也会弄到。”</p>
<p>“你能找到这个洞真走运。”我说。他笑了。“找到？你这蠢货！是我自己造的。拿镐和铲子，用我这双百合花一样白嫩的小手亲自挖出来的。”说到这里，他伸出一团又脏又筋腱分明的“触手”去抓饼干。“那活简直累得见鬼，不过倒把我的肌肉练硬了。”</p>
<p>“那这些设备怎么运进来的？比如那台车床。”</p>
<p>“当然走海路。”</p>
<p>“总不会是用那条独木舟吧！”我抗议。</p>
<p>“我让人全发到X港。”他说出河口另一侧一个小港的名字。“那边有个家伙，替我处理这类小事。他很可靠，因为我知道一些他绝不想让警察知道的事情。总之，有一天晚上，他把装着零件的箱子扔在那边岸上；与此同时，我偷了帆船俱乐部的一条小艇，开过去取货。这事必须挑大潮，偏偏天气当然又糟透了。把东西运过来以后，虽然我已经把它们都拆成小件，从海岸搬到这里还是差点把我累死。而且直到天快亮，我才勉强把那条小艇送回原来的系泊处。谢天谢地，全都过去了。再来一杯？”</p>
<p>我们凑在煤油炉边烤暖身体，随后开始讨论，在他这场荒诞冒险里，他打算让我扮演什么角色。起初我很想把整个计划当笑话嘲弄；可一方面他有魔鬼般的说服力，另一方面他已经真的完成了这么多事情，结果我发现自己竟然答应承担计划中属于我的那一部分。“你看，”他说，“这些东西都得申请专利，再把专利卖给制造商。像我这么个小孩，根本不可能自己去见专利代理人和商人。这就轮到你上场。你要负责把所有这些东西推向市场，有时用你自己的名字，有时用假名。我不想让别人知道，它们全都来自同一个小脑袋。”</p>
<p>“可是约翰，”我说，“我每次都会被人宰。我完全不懂这一行。”</p>
<p>“没关系。”他回答，“每一件事我都会准确告诉你该怎么做。就算你犯几个错误，也没什么。”</p>
<p>他替我们设计的这种关系有一点非常古怪：我们明明预料将来会经手大笔钱财，却完全不准备建立任何正规商业安排；没有正式协议规定利润怎样分、责任怎样承担。我提议写一份合同，他却轻蔑地把想法扔到一边。“我亲爱的先生，”他说，“我要是不从隐蔽处现身——而这是无论如何都不能做的——又怎么可能强制你履行合同？再说，只要你身体和精神都保持健康，我非常清楚你完全可靠。你也应该同样清楚我可靠。这是一场朋友之间的合作。等钱开始进来以后，你想拿多少就拿多少。我敢拿靴子打赌，你连自己服务价值的一半都不会愿意拿。当然，如果你开始每个周末都坐飞机带你那个姑娘去里维埃拉，我们就得开始把事情正规化了。不过你不会。”</p>
<p>我问起银行账户。“哦，”他说，“我早就在伦敦一家——银行的分行开了账户。不过大多数款项都得先打进你的银行账户，好让我继续藏在暗处。这些小玩意要以你的名义推出，而不是我的；或者说成许多虚构人物的发明。你就是他们的代理人。”</p>
<p>“可是，”我抗议，“你没看出来吗？你等于把从头到尾骗光你全部收益的绝对权力交给了我。假如我只是在利用你呢？假如权力的滋味冲昏我的脑子，我把一切全吞了呢？我毕竟只是智人（Homo sapiens），又不是优人（Homo superior）。”这一次，我私下甚至觉得，约翰也许并没有自己想象得那么高明。</p>
<p>约翰听见“优人”这个称呼，开心地大笑，但他说：“我亲爱的东西，你就是不会这么干。不，不，我拒绝搞任何商业安排。那也太‘智人’了。真要这么做，我们反而永远不可能互相信任。说不定我还会为了好玩，从各个方向把你骗得团团转。”</p>
<p>“好吧。”我叹了口气，“那你总会记账，知道钱都怎么走了吧。”</p>
<p>“记账，先生！我要账本干什么鬼？这些账全在我脑子里，不过我从来不去看。”</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a336"></span>CHAPTER VI. MANY INVENTIONS</h3>
<p><span class="t28">A</span><span class="t31">LTHOUGH</span><span class="t30"> I </span><span class="t31">DID</span><span class="t30"> </span>not at the time know that John was responsible for the murder, I noticed that a change came over him. He became less communicative, in a way more aloof from his friends, both juvenile and adult, and at the same time more considerate and even gentle. I say “in a way” more aloof, because, though less ready to talk about himself, and more prone to solitariness, he had also his sociable times. He could indeed be a most sympathetic companion, the sort in whom one was tempted to confide all manner of secret hopes and fears that were scarcely admitted by oneself. One day, for instance, I found myself discovering, under the influence of John’s presence and my own effort to explain myself, that I had already become very strongly attracted to a certain Pax-like young woman, and further that I had been kept from recognizing this feeling through an obscure sense of loyalty to John. The discovery of the strength of my feeling for John was more of a shock than the discovery of my feeling for the girl. I knew that I was deeply interested in John, but till that day I had no idea how subtle and far-reaching were the tentacles with which the strange child had penetrated me.</p>
<p>My reaction was a violent and rather panicky rebellion. I flaunted before John the new-found normal sexual attraction which he himself had pointed out to me, and I ridiculed the notion that I was psychologically his captive. He replied, “Well, be careful. Don’t spoil your life for me.” It was strange to be talking like this to a child of less than ten years old. It was distressing to feel that he knew more about me than I knew about myself. For in spite of my denial, I knew that he was right.</p>
<p>Looking back, I recognize that John’s interest in my case was partly due to curiosity about a relationship which he himself could not yet experience, partly to straightforward affection for a well-known companion, partly to the need to understand as fully as possible one whom he intended to use for his own ends. For it is clear that he did intend to use me, that he did not for a moment intend me to free myself. He wanted my affair with the Pax-like girl to go forward and complete itself not only because, as my friend, he espoused my need, but also because, if I were to give it up for his sake, I should become a vindictive rather than a willing slave. He preferred, I imagine, to be served by a free and roving hound rather than by a chained and hungry wolf.</p>
<p>His feeling for individuals of the species which, as a species, he heartily despised, was a strange blend of contempt and respect, detachment and affection. He despised us for our stupidity and fecklessness; he respected us for our occasional efforts to surmount our natural disabilities. Though he used us for his own ends with calm aloofness, he could also, when fate or our own folly brought us into trouble, serve us with surprising humility and devotion.</p>
<p>His growing capacity for personal relationships with members of the inferior species was shown most quaintly in his extraordinary friendship with a little girl of six. Judy’s home was close to John’s, and she had come to regard John as her private property. He played uproarious games with her, helped her to climb trees, and taught her to swim and roller-skate. He told her wildly imaginative stories. He patiently explained to her the sorry jokes of Comic Cuts. He drew pictures of battle and murder, shipwreck and volcanic eruption for Judy’s sole delight. He mended her toys. He chaffed her for her stupidity or praised her for her intelligence as occasion demanded. If any one was less than kind to her, John rushed to her defence. In all communal games it was taken for granted that John and Judy must be on the same side. In return for this devotion she mauled him, laughed at him, scolded him, called him “stoopid Don,” showed no respect at all for his marvellous powers, and presented him with all the most cherished results of her enterprise in the “hand-work” class at school.</p>
<p>I once challenged John, “Why are you so fond of Judy?” He answered promptly, imitating her unusually backward baby speech, “Doody made for be’n’ fon’ of. Can’t not be fon’ of Doody.” Then after a pause he said, “I’m fond of Judy as I’m fond of sea-birds. She does only simple things, but she does them all with style. She be’s Judy as thoroughly and perfectly as a gannet be’s a gannet. If she could grow up to do the grown-up things as well as she does the baby things, she’d be glorious. But she won’t. When it comes to doing the more difficult things, I suppose she’ll mess up her style like — like the rest of you. It’s a pity. But meanwhile she’s — Judy.”</p>
<p>“What about yourself?” I said. “Do you expect to grow up without losing your style?”</p>
<p>“I’ve not found my style yet,” he answered. “I’m groping. I’ve messed things pretty badly already. But when I do find it — well, we shall see. Of course,” he added surprisingly, “God may find grown-ups as delightful to watch as I find Judy; because, I suppose, he doesn’t want them to have a finer style than they actually have. Sometimes I can feel that way about them myself. I can feel their bad style is part of what they are, and strangely fascinating to watch. But I have an idea God expects something different from me. Or, leaving out the God myth, I expect something different from me.”</p>
<p>A few weeks after the murder, John developed a surprising interest in a very homely sphere, namely the management of a house. He would spend an hour at a time in following Martha the maid about the house on her morning’s work, or in watching the culinary operations. For her entertainment he kept up a stream of small talk compounded of scandal, broad humour, and chaff about her “gentlemen friends.” The same minute observation, but a very different kind of talk was devoted to Pax when she was in the pantry or the larder, or when she was “tidying” a room or mending clothes. Sometimes he would break off his tittle-tattle to say, “Why not do it this way?” Martha’s response to such suggestions varied from haughty contempt to grudging acceptance, according to her mood. Pax invariably gave serious attention to the new idea, though sometimes she would begin by protesting, “But my way works well enough; why bother?” In the end, however, she nearly always adopted John’s improvement, with an odd little smile which might equally well have meant maternal pride or indulgence.</p>
<p>Little by little John introduced a number of small labour-saving devices into the house, shifting a hook or a shelf to suit the natural reach of the adult arm, altering the balance of the coal-scuttle, reorganizing the larder and the bathroom. He tried to introduce his methods into the surgery, suggesting new ways of cleaning test-tubes, sterilizing instruments and storing drugs; but after a few attempts he gave up this line of activity, since, as he put it, “Doc likes to muddle along in his own way.”</p>
<p>After two or three weeks John’s interest in household economy seemed to fade, save for occasional revivals in relation to some particular problem. He now spent most of his time away from home, ostensibly reading on the shore. But as the autumn advanced, and we began to inquire how he managed to keep himself warm, he apparently developed a passion for long walks by himself. He also spent much time in excursions into the neighbouring city. “I’m going to town for the day to see some fellows I’m interested in,” he would tell us; and in the evening he would return tired and absorbed.</p>
<p>It was toward the end of the winter that John, now about ten and a half, took me into his confidence with regard to the amazing commercial operations which had been occupying him during the previous six months. One filthy Sunday morning, when the windows were plastered with sleet, he suggested a walk. I indignantly refused. “Come on,” he insisted. “It’s going to be amusing for you. I want to show you my workshop.” He slowly winked first one huge eye and then the other.</p>
<p>By the time we had reached the shore my inadequate mackintosh was letting water through on my shoulders, and I was cursing John, and myself too. We tramped along the soaked sands till we reached a spot where the steep clay cliffs gave place to a slope, scarcely less steep, but covered with thorn bushes. John went down on his knees and led the way, crawling on all-fours up a track between the bushes. I was expected to follow. I found it almost impossible to force my larger bulk where John had passed with ease. When I had gone a few yards I was jammed, thorns impaling me on every side. Laughing at my predicament and my curses, John turned and cut me adrift with his knife, the same, doubtless, as had killed the constable. After another ten yards the track brought us into a small clearing on the steep slope. Standing erect at last, I grumbled, “Is this what you call your workshop?” John laughed, and said, “Lift that.” He was pointing to a rusty sheet of corrugated iron, which lay derelict on the hillside. One end of it was buried under a mass of rubbish. The exposed part was about three feet square. I tugged its free end up a couple of inches, cut my fingers on the rusty jagged edge, and let go with a curse. “Can’t be bothered,” I said. “Do your own dirty work, if you can.” “Of course you can’t be bothered,” he replied, “nor would anyone else who found it.” He then worked his hand under the free corners of the sheet, and disentangled some rusty wire. The sheet was now easily lifted, and opened like a trap-door in the hillside. It revealed a black hole between three big stones. John crawled inside, and bade me follow; but before I could wedge my way through he had to move one of the stones. I found myself in a low cave, illuminated by John’s flash-light. So this was the workshop! It had evidently been cut out of the clay slope and lined with cement. The ceiling was covered with rough planks, and shored up here and there with wooden posts.</p>
<p>John now lit an acetylene lamp, which was let into the outer wall. Shutting its glass face, he remarked, “Its air comes in by a pipe from outside, and its fumes go out by another. There’s an independent ventilation system for the room.” Pointing to a dozen round holes in the wall, “Drainpipes,” he said. Such pipes were a common sight on the coast, for they were used for draining the fields; and the ever-crumbling cliff often exposed them.</p>
<p>For a few minutes I crouched in silence, surveying the little den. John watched me, with a grin of boyish satisfaction. There was a bench, a small lathe, a blow-lamp, and quantities of tools. On the back wall was a tier of shelves covered with a jumble of articles. John took one of these and handed it to me, saying, “This is one of my earlier gadgets, the world’s perfect wool-winder. No curates need henceforth apply. The Church’s undoing! Put the skein on those prongs, and an end of wool in that slot, then waggle the lever, so, and you get a ball of wool as sleek as the curate’s head. All made of aluminum sheeting, and a few aluminum knitting needles.”</p>
<p>“Damned ingenious,” I said, “but what good is it to you?”</p>
<p>“Why, you fool! I’m going to patent it and sell the patent.”</p>
<p>Producing a deep leather pouch, he said, “This is a detachable and untearable trouser-pocket for boys; and men, if they’ll have the sense to use it. The pocket itself clips on to this L-shaped strip, so; and all your trousers have strips like this, firmly sewn into the lining. You have one pair of pockets for all your trousers, so there’s no bother about emptying pockets when you change your clothes. And no more holes for Mummy to mend. And no more losing your treasures. Your pocket clips tight shut, so.”</p>
<p>Even my interest in John’s amazing enterprise (so childish and so brilliant, I told myself) could not prevent me from feeling wet and chilled. Taking off my dripping mackintosh, I said, “Don’t you get horribly cold working in this hole in the winter?”</p>
<p>“I heat the place with this,” he said, turning to a little oil-stove with a flue leading round the room and through the wall. He proceeded to light it, and put a kettle on the top, saying, “Let’s have some coffee.”</p>
<p>He then gave me a “gadget for sweeping out corners.” On the end of a long tubular handle was a brush like a big blunt cork-screw. This could be made to rotate by merely pressing it into the awkward corner. The rotatory motion was obtained by a device reminiscent of a “propelling” pencil, for the actual shaft of the brush was keyed into a spiral groove within the hollow handle.</p>
<p>“It’s possible the thing I’m on now will bring more money than anything else, but it’s damned hard to make even an inch or two of it by hand.” The article which John now showed me was destined to become one of the most popular and serviceable of modern devices connected with clothing. Throughout Europe and America it has spawned its myriads of offspring. Nearly all the most ingenious and lucrative of John’s inventions have had such outstanding success that almost every reader must be familiar with every one of them. I could mention a score of them; but for private reasons, connected with John’s family, I must refrain from doing so. I will only say that, save for one universally adopted improvement in road-traffic appliances, he worked entirely in the field of household and personal labour-saving devices. The outstanding fact about John’s career as an inventor was his knack of producing not merely occasional successes but a steady flow of “best sellers.” Consequently to describe only a few minor achievements and interesting failures must give a very false impression of his genius. The reader must supplement this meagre report by means of his own imagination. Let him, in the act of using any of the more cunning and efficient little instruments of modern comfort, remind himself that this may well be one of the many “gadgets” which were conceived by the urchin-superman in his subterranean lair.</p>
<p>For some time John continued to show me his inventions. I may mention a parsley cutter, a potato-peeler, a number of devices for using old razor-blades as penknife, scissors, and so on. Others, to repeat, were destined never to be taken up, or never to become popular. Of these perhaps the most noteworthy was a startlingly efficient dodge for saving time and trouble in the watercloset. John himself had doubts about some, including the detachable pocket. “The trouble is,” he said, “that however good my inventions are, Homo sapiens may be too prejudiced to use them. I expect he’ll stick to his bloody pockets.”</p>
<p>The kettle was boiling, so he made the coffee and produced a noble cake, made by Pax.</p>
<p>While we were drinking and munching I asked him how he got all his plant. “It’s all paid for,” he said. “I came in for a bit of money. I’ll tell you about that some day. But I want much more money, and I’ll get it too.”</p>
<p>“You were lucky to find this cave,” I said. He laughed, “Find it, you chump! I made it. Dug it out with pick and spade and my own lily-white hands.” (At this point he reached out a grubby and sinewy bunch of tentacles for a biscuit.) “It was the hell of a grind, but it hardened my muscles.”</p>
<p>“And how did you transport the stuff, that lathe, for instance?” “By sea, of course.” “Not in the canoe!” I protested. “Had it all sent to X,” he said, naming a little port on the other side of the estuary. “There’s a bloke over there who acts as my agent in little matters like that. He’s safe, because I know things about him that he doesn’t want the police to know. Well, he dumped the cases of parts on the shore over there one night while I pinched one of the Sailing Club’s cutters and took her over to fetch the stuff. It had to be done at spring tide, and of course the weather was all wrong. When I got the stuff over I nearly died lugging it up here from the shore, though it was all in small pieces. And I only just managed to get the cutter back to her moorings before dawn. Thank God that’s all over. Have another cup, won’t you?”</p>
<p>Toasting ourselves over the oil-stove, we now discussed the part which John intended me to play in his preposterous adventure. I was at first inclined to scoff at the whole project, but what with his diabolical persuasiveness and the fact that he had already achieved so much, I found myself agreeing to carry out my share of the plan. “You see,” he said, “all this stuff must be patented and the patents sold to manufacturers. It’s quite useless for a kid like me to interview patent agents and business men. That’s where you come in. You’re going to launch all these things, sometimes under your own name, sometimes under sham names. I don’t want people to know they all come from one little brain.”</p>
<p>“But, John,” I said, “I should get stung every time. I know nothing about the job.”</p>
<p>“That’s all right,” he answered. “I’ll tell you exactly what to do in each case. And if you do make a few mistakes, it doesn’t matter.”</p>
<p>One odd feature of the relationship which he had planned for us was that, though we expected to deal with large sums of money, there was not to be any regularized business arrangement between us, no formal agreement about profit-sharing and liabilities. I suggested a written contract, but he dismissed the idea with contempt. “My dear man,” he said, “how could I enforce a contract against you without coming out of hiding, which I must not do on any account? Besides, I know perfectly well that so long as you keep in physical and mental health you’re entirely reliable. And you ought to know the same of me. This is to be a friendly show. You can take as much as you like of the dibs, when they begin to come in. I’ll bet my boots you won’t want to take half as much as your services are worth. Of course, if you start taking that girl of yours to the Riviera by air every week-end, we’ll have to begin regularizing things. But you won’t.”</p>
<p>I asked him about a banking account. “Oh,” he said, “I’ve had one running for some time at a London branch of the —— Bank. But the payments will have to be made to you at your bank mostly, so as to keep me dark. These gadgets are to go out as yours, not mine, and as the inventions of lots of imaginary people. You’re their agent.”</p>
<p>“But,” I protested, “don’t you see you’re giving me absolute power to swindle you out of the whole proceeds? Suppose I just use you? Suppose the taste of power goes to my head, and I collar everything? I’m only Homo sapiens, not Homo superior.” And for once I privately felt that John was perhaps not so superior after all.</p>
<p>John laughed delightedly at the title, but said, “My dear thing, you just won’t. No, no, I refuse to have any business arrangements. That would be too ‘sapient’ altogether. We should never be able to trust one another. Probably I’d cheat you all round, just for fun.”</p>
<p>“Oh, well,” I sighed, “you’ll keep accounts and see how the money goes.”</p>
<p>“Keep accounts, man! What in hell do I want with accounts? I keep ’em in my head, but never look at ’em.”</p>
