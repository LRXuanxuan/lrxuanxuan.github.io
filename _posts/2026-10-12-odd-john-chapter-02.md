<h3>第二章　第一阶段</h3>
<p>约翰的父亲托马斯·温赖特有理由相信，许多代以前，西班牙人和摩洛哥人的血统都曾参与塑造他的家系。他的性情里确实带着某种拉丁气质，甚至也许还有一点阿拉伯气质。人人都承认他颇有才华；但他古怪，而且通常被视为一个失败者。在英格兰北部某个郊区行医，既没有多少机会让他施展本领，却有很多机会让他把别人惹恼。他治好过几个相当棘手的病例；可是他缺乏医生应有的亲切床边态度，病人始终不肯给予他那种对于医生成功极为重要的信任。</p>
<p>他的妻子在血统上也不比丈夫“纯”，但混合的方式完全不同。她有瑞典血统，祖先中也有芬兰人和拉普人。她外貌很斯堪的纳维亚：高大、迟缓、金发，即便做了母亲，仍足以让年轻男人眼前一亮。最初正是因为受她吸引，我才在年轻时成了她丈夫的朋友；后来又成了她那个远比父亲耀眼的儿子的俘虏。有人说她“不过是一头壮丽的雌性动物”，而且迟钝得近乎低能。的确，同她谈话有时几乎像同母牛谈话一样单方面。然而她绝不是傻瓜。她总能把家里收拾得井井有条，却仿佛从未为此费过脑筋；她也用同样漫不经心的熟练手法，应付自己那个颇难相处的丈夫。丈夫叫她“帕克斯”（Pax）。“因为她太安宁了。”他这样解释。奇怪的是，孩子们后来也沿用了这个称呼。他们则一律叫父亲“医生”（Doc）。年长的两个孩子——一女一男——装模作样地嘲笑母亲不懂世事，可真正遇到事情时，又总指望她的意见。小他们四岁的约翰有一次说了句话，让我们意识到，也许大家一直都看错了她。有人提到她异常沉默寡言，约翰那令人不安的笑声顿时迸了出来，随后他说：“根本没人注意帕克斯真正感兴趣的东西，所以她当然就没什么可说的。”</p>
<p>约翰的出生让这具强健的母体经受了严峻考验。她怀了整整十一个月，医生们终于认定，无论如何都必须终止妊娠。然而孩子总算被取出来时，却荒诞地像一个只有七个月的胎儿。医护人员费了极大力气，才靠保温箱把他保住。直到强行分娩整整一年后，人们才认为这座人工子宫终于可以撤掉。</p>
<p>约翰出生后的第一年，我常常见到他。那时我和他父亲之间已经形成了一种奇特的亲密关系；尽管他比我年长许多，我们有共同的智识兴趣，而且也许还因为我们都同样欣赏帕克斯。</p>
<p>我至今记得第一次见到那个被他们称作“约翰”的东西时，自己那种震惊和厌恶。如此迟钝、软塌塌的一小团肉，竟有一天能长成人，简直叫人无法相信。它像某种猥亵的果实，与其说是动物，不如说更像植物，只是偶尔会不协调地突然抽动几下。</p>
<p>不过，约翰一岁时，看上去已经差不多像一个正常的新生儿，只是双眼仍然紧闭。十八个月时，他终于睁开了眼睛；那感觉仿佛一座沉睡的城市突然整个活了过来。对于一个婴儿来说，那双眼睛实在慑人，像是被放大镜放大了：每个巨大的瞳孔都像洞穴的入口，虹膜则只剩一道边缘、一圈明亮的祖母绿色细边。两个漆黑的孔洞竟然能如此闪耀着生命，实在奇怪！就在他睁眼不久之后，帕克斯开始把这个奇怪的儿子叫作“怪约翰”（Odd John）。她说这两个字时总带着一种细微而特别的语调；音调本身几乎不变，却有时像是对这孩子古怪之处的亲昵歉意，有时像挑战，有时像得意，偶尔甚至带着敬畏。这个形容词从此伴随约翰终生。</p>
<p>从那以后，约翰就明确成了一个“人”，而且是一个异常清醒、异常警觉的人。他一周比一周活跃，一周比一周充满兴趣；眼睛、耳朵和四肢仿佛永远都在忙碌。</p>
<p>接下来的两年里，约翰的身体发育始终令人提心吊胆，好在没有发生灾难。喂养问题一直很多，但到三岁时，他已算是个相当健康的孩子，虽然依旧古怪，而且外表上的发育远远落后于年龄。托马斯为这种迟缓忧心忡忡，帕克斯却坚持认为，大多数婴儿长得太快。“他们没给自己的脑子留下好好编织成形的时间。”她这样说。倒霉的父亲只能摇头。</p>
<p>约翰五岁那年，我几乎每天早晨去火车站时都会经过温赖特家，因此常常看见他。他会坐在花园里的婴儿车中，四肢乱舞，嘴里大喊大叫。我总觉得那阵喧闹有种奇特的性质。它和普通婴儿的发声有一种难以描述的差异，就像一种猴子的叫声与另一物种的叫声不同。那是一阵丰富、微妙的喧嚷，充满古怪的抑扬顿挫和变化。很难相信这是一个发育迟缓的四岁孩子。无论举止还是外貌，他都更像一个异常聪明的六个月大婴儿。他太清醒了，不像发育迟缓；又太幼小了，不像四岁。问题不仅在于那双巨大眼睛过分机警、过分具有穿透力。就连他笨拙地摆弄玩具时，也显出一种远超年龄的目的性。虽然手指还完全不听使唤，他的头脑却似乎已经给它们布置了非常明确、非常聪明的任务。每当手指失败，他都会非常懊恼。</p>
<p>约翰当然很聪明。到了这时，我们对这一点已经没有分歧。然而他既没有爬行的迹象，也没有说话的迹象。随后，远在他尝试在这个世界里移动以前，他却突然会说话了。某个星期二，他还像往常一样只会咿咿呀呀。星期三，他异常安静，而且第一次似乎听懂了母亲婴儿语中的某些内容。到了星期四早晨，他突然慢慢而又极其正确地说：“我——要——牛奶。”把全家都吓了一跳。那天下午，他对一个已经让他失去兴趣的客人说：“走——开。我——不——太——喜欢——你。”</p>
<p>这种语言能力的出现方式，显然与普通孩子第一次开口完全不同。</p>
<p>星期五和星期六，约翰都在同欣喜若狂的亲属们谨慎地练习交谈。到下一个星期二——也就是第一次开口一周之后——他的语言能力已经超过了七岁的哥哥，而说话本身也开始失去新鲜感。语言不再是一门刚掌握的新技艺，只成了一种有用的沟通工具；只有当新的经验领域进入他的视野、要求表达时，他才会继续扩展和磨炼它。</p>
<p>约翰一会说话，父母就从他口中得知了几件吃惊的事情。例如，他记得自己的出生。那场痛苦的危机刚结束、他与母体分离之后，他实际上还得自己“学会”呼吸。在任何自主呼吸反射苏醒以前，人工呼吸维持着他的生命；而正是从这种经历中，他发现自己可以控制肺部。他以一种漫长而绝望的意志努力，仿佛手摇发动机一样把身体“摇起来”，直到它终于“点火”，能够自发运转。据他说，他的心脏也在很大程度上受意志控制。早期那些让父母极度惊恐的“心脏问题”，其实是他过于大胆地主动干预心跳造成的。他对情绪反射的控制也远远超过我们。譬如，在某种本应引起愤怒的情境下，如果他不愿发怒，就可以轻易抑制愤怒的反射；而如果他觉得愤怒有用，也可以主动把它制造出来。他确实是“怪约翰”。</p>
<p>约翰学会说话大约九个月以后，有人送了他一架儿童算盘。那一天余下的时间里，他不再说话，也不再嬉闹；吃饭都让他觉得碍事。约翰突然发现了数字那错综复杂的乐趣。一小时又一小时，他在这件新玩具上做着各种运算。然后突然间，他把算盘扔到一边，仰躺着盯住天花板。</p>
<p>母亲以为他累了，便同他说话。他毫无反应。她轻轻摇他的胳膊，仍没有回应。“约翰！”她有些惊慌地喊起来，更用力地摇他。“别吵，帕克斯，”他说，“我正忙着弄数字呢。”</p>
<p>停了一会儿，他又问：“帕克斯，十二后面的那些数叫什么？”她从十二数到二十，又数到三十。“你跟那个玩具一样笨，帕克斯。”她问为什么，他却发现自己没有足够的词来解释。不过，在算盘上示范了几种运算、又从母亲那里学到这些运算的名称以后，他慢慢地、得意地说：“帕克斯亲爱的，你很笨，因为你（还有那个玩具）用十来‘数’，不用十二。这很笨，因为十二有‘四分’和‘三分’——我是说三等分——十却没有。”母亲解释说，所有人都用十进制，是因为人类刚开始计数时用的是自己的十根手指。他定定地看着她，然后发出了那种噼啪作响、近乎啼叫的笑声。过了一会儿他说：“那所有人都很笨。”</p>
<p>我想，这大概是约翰第一次认识到智人（Homo sapiens）的愚蠢，但绝不是最后一次。</p>
<p>托马斯因为约翰表现出的数学敏锐而欣喜若狂，想把这个案例报告给英国心理学会。然而帕克斯却表现出出人意料的坚决，执意“目前必须把这一切藏起来”。“不准让他们拿他做实验，”她坚持说，“他们多半会伤着他。而且不管怎样，他们一定会大惊小怪。”托马斯和我都笑她多虑，可最后还是她赢了。</p>
<p>约翰这时已经快五岁了，外表却仍然只是个婴儿。他不会走，也不会——或者不肯——爬。他的腿还是婴儿的腿。而且，数学很可能严重拖延了他学走路，因为接下来的几个月里，无论怎样劝，他都不肯把注意力放到数字和空间性质以外的任何东西上。他常常在花园里的婴儿车中一躺就是几个小时，做着“心算”和“心内几何”，一块肌肉也不动，一点声音也不出。这对一个成长中的孩子当然极不健康，他开始生病。可无论什么办法，都无法诱使他过一种更正常、更活跃的生活。</p>
<p>来访者常常不肯相信，他在外面躺上那么多个小时，头脑其实一直在活动。他脸色苍白，神情“恍惚”。他们私下认为他处于昏睡状态，正在朝低能儿发展。但他偶尔会主动说出几句话，立刻就让这些人目瞪口呆。</p>
<p>约翰对几何的进攻始于他对哥哥那盒积木和一幅菱形连续花纹壁纸的兴趣。接着有一个阶段，他把奶酪和肥皂切成薄板、立方体、圆锥体，甚至球体和卵形体。起初约翰用刀极其笨拙，常常割伤手指，把母亲吓得够呛。但几天以后，他已经熟练得惊人。和往常一样，他总是很晚才开始一种新活动，可一旦真正把心思放上去，进步速度就快得近乎荒诞。下一阶段，他开始使用姐姐上学用的一套几何绘图工具。整整一周，他沉醉其中，画满了数不清的纸张。</p>
<p>然后，他突然拒绝再对视觉几何发生兴趣。他更愿意仰躺着沉思。一天早晨，有个问题一直困扰他，却又说不清楚。帕克斯完全听不懂他的努力，后来还是父亲帮助他扩展了词汇，他才终于能够问：“为什么只有三个维度？等我长大以后，会不会发现更多维？”</p>
<p>几个星期后，他又提出了一个更加惊人的问题：“如果你一直沿着一条直线走，走啊走啊走，要走多远才会重新回到这里？”</p>
<p>我们都笑了，帕克斯惊呼：“怪约翰！”那是1915年初。随后托马斯想起自己听人谈过一种正在颠覆旧几何观念的“相对论”。渐渐地，他越来越被约翰这个怪问题以及类似的问题打动，最终坚持要从大学请一位数学家来同孩子谈谈。</p>
<p>帕克斯表示反对，可就连她也没有料到，结果会是一场灾难。</p>
<p>那位访客起初居高临下，接着兴奋起来，然后开始困惑；随后又像卸下重担似的重新摆出居高临下的态度，最后则彻底慌了神。帕克斯很有分寸地劝他离开——当然是为了孩子着想——可他临走时问，自己能否带一位同事再来一次。</p>
<p>几天以后，两个人一同出现，和这个婴儿开会似的谈了好几个小时。不巧托马斯正在外出巡诊。帕克斯坐在约翰的高脚椅旁，默默织着毛线，偶尔帮孩子把意思表达清楚。但谈话内容早已远远超出她的理解范围。中途停下来喝茶时，其中一位访客说：“最惊人的，是这孩子的想象能力。他完全不知道我们的术语，也不知道这套思想的历史，可他已经凭自己把一切都看出来了。不可思议。他仿佛能够把根本不可能视觉化的东西视觉化。”</p>
<p>帕克斯后来告诉我们，那天下午再往后，两位访客渐渐变得相当激动，甚至发怒；而约翰那种安静得叫人恼火的笑声只让情况更糟。最后，她以约翰该睡觉为由坚决终止了讨论，这时她发现两位客人显然都已经失去自制。“他们两个眼神都发狂了，”她说，“我把他们从花园里赶出去时，他们还在争个不停，连再见都没说。”</p>
<p>不过几天后听说，大学数学系的两名教师凌晨两点被人发现一起坐在街灯下，一边在人行道上画图，一边争论“空间的曲率”，我们还是吃了一惊。</p>
<p>托马斯始终只把最小的这个孩子看作一个格外醒目的“神童”案例。他最爱说的一句话是：“当然，等他长大些，一切都会烟消云散。”帕克斯却总说：“是吗？我倒想看看。”</p>
<p>约翰又折腾了一个月数学，随后突然把它整个抛在脑后。父亲问他为什么放弃，他说：“数字其实没多少东西。当然，它美得不得了，可等你把它全做完——那也就完了。我已经把数字做完了。这一套游戏里该知道的，我都知道了。我想换一个游戏。你不能永远含着同一块糖。”</p>
<p>此后的十二个月里，约翰没有再给父母什么真正的惊喜。不错，他学会了读写，而且只用一周就超过了哥哥姐姐；但经历过那些数学上的胜利以后，这只能算一个相当普通的成就。真正让人意外的，反而是他直到这么晚才产生阅读的意愿。帕克斯常常把哥哥姐姐的书朗读给他听，而他显然看不出有什么必要让母亲摆脱这项职责。</p>
<p>后来姐姐安妮病了，母亲忙得无暇再给他读书。有一天，他缠着母亲要她开读一本新书，可她不肯。“那你走之前先教我怎么读。”他要求道。母亲笑了，说：“那可要学很久。等安妮好了，我再教你。”</p>
<p>几天以后，她用正统的方法开始教他。但约翰对正统方法毫无耐心，自己发明了一套。他让帕克斯大声朗读，同时用手指沿着行文移动，这样他就能逐词跟上。帕克斯忍不住笑这种方法太野蛮，可在约翰身上，它偏偏有效。他只是把母亲发出的每一个“声音”的“样子”都记住，因为他的记忆保持能力似乎从不出错。不久，他在不打断母亲朗读的情况下，开始自行拆解各个字母对应的声音，很快就对英语拼写毫无逻辑这件事破口大骂。那一课结束时，约翰已经会读书了，当然词汇量仍然有限。接下来一周，他把家里所有儿童读物都吞了个遍，甚至还看了几本“成年人”的书。那些书对他当然几乎毫无意义，即使里面大部分词他都认识。他很快厌恶地把它们放弃了。有一天，他拿起姐姐的学校几何课本，五分钟后就扔到一边，只说了一句：“婴儿书！”</p>
<p>从此以后，凡是约翰感兴趣的东西，他都能够阅读；但他丝毫没有变成一个书虫。阅读只适合那些不能动手的时候——也就是他那双使用过度的手需要休息之际。因为这时，他已经进入一个近乎狂热的手工建造阶段，用硬纸板、铁丝、木头、橡皮泥以及任何到手的材料制作各种精巧模型。绘画也占据了他大量时间。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a332"></span>CHAPTER II. THE FIRST PHASE</h3>
<p><span class="t28">J</span><span class="t31">OHN</span><span class="t30">’</span><span class="t31">S</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">FATHER</span><span class="t30">, T</span><span class="t31">HOMAS</span><span class="t30"> </span>Wainwright, had reason to believe that Spaniards and Moroccans had long ago contributed to his making. There was indeed something of the Latin, even perhaps of the Arab, in his nature. Every one admitted that he had a certain brilliance; but he was odd, and was generally regarded as a failure. A medical practice in a North-country suburb gave little scope for his powers, and many opportunities of rubbing people up the wrong way. Several remarkable cures stood to his credit; but he had no bedside manner, and his patients never accorded him the trust which is so necessary for a doctor’s success.</p>
<p>His wife was no less a mongrel than her husband, but one of a very different kind. She was of Swedish extraction. Finns and Lapps were also among her ancestors. Scandinavian in appearance, she was a great sluggish blonde, who even as a matron dazzled the young male eye. It was originally through her attraction that I became the youthful friend of her husband, and later the slave of her more than brilliant son. Some said she was “just a magnificent female animal,” and so dull as to be subnormal. Certainly conversation with her was sometimes almost as one-sided as conversation with a cow. Yet she was no fool. Her house was always in good order, though she seemed to spend no thought upon it. With the same absent-minded skill she managed her rather difficult husband. He called her “Pax.” “So peaceful,” he would explain. Curiously her children also adopted this name for her. Their father they called invariably “Doc.” The two elder, girl and boy, affected to smile at their mother’s ignorance of the world; but they counted on her advice. John, the youngest by four years, once said something which suggested that we had all misjudged her. Some one had remarked on her extraordinary dumbness. Out flashed John’s disconcerting laugh, and then, “No one notices the things that interest Pax, and so she just doesn’t talk.”</p>
<p>John’s birth had put the great maternal animal to a severe strain. She carried her burden for eleven months, till the doctors decided that at all costs she must be relieved. Yet when the baby was at last brought to light, it had the grotesque appearance of a seven-months fetus. Only with great difficulty was it kept alive in an incubator. Not till a year after the forced birth was this artificial womb deemed no longer necessary.</p>
<p>I saw John frequently during his first year, for between me and the father, though he was many years my senior, there had by now grown up a curious intimacy based on common intellectual interests, and perhaps partly on a common admiration for Pax.</p>
<p>I can remember my shock of disgust when I first saw the thing they had called John. It seemed impossible that such an inert and pulpy bit of flesh could ever develop into a human being. It was like some obscene fruit, more vegetable than animal, save for an occasional incongruous spasm of activity.</p>
<p>When John was a year old, however, he looked almost like a normal new-born infant, save that his eyes were shut. At eighteen months he opened them; and it was as though a sleeping city had suddenly leapt into life. Formidable eyes they were for a baby, eyes seen under a magnifying glass, each great pupil like the mouth of a cave, the iris a mere rim, an edging of bright emerald. Strange how two black holes can gleam with life! It was shortly after his eyes had opened that Pax began to call her strange son “Odd John.” She gave the words a particular and subtle intonation which, though it scarcely varied, seemed to express sometimes merely affectionate apology for the creature’s oddity, but sometimes defiance, and sometimes triumph, and occasionally awe. The adjective stuck to John throughout his life.</p>
<p>Henceforth John was definitely a person and a very wide-awake person, too. Week by week he became more and more active and more and more interested. He was for ever busy with eyes and ears and limbs.</p>
<p>During the next two years John’s body developed precariously, but without disaster. There were always difficulties over feeding, but when he had reached the age of three he was a tolerably healthy child, though odd, and in appearance extremely backward. This backwardness distressed Thomas, Pax, however, insisted that most babies grew too fast. “They don’t give their minds a chance to knit themselves properly,” she declared. The unhappy father shook his head.</p>
<p>When John was in his fifth year I used to see him nearly every morning as I passed the Wainwrights’ house on my way to the railway station. He would be in his pram in the garden rioting with limbs and voice. The din, I thought, had an odd quality. It differed indescribably from the vocalization of any ordinary baby, as the call of one kind of monkey differs from that of another species. It was a rich and subtle shindy, full of quaint modulations and variations. One could scarcely believe that this was a backward child of four. Both behaviour and appearance suggested an extremely bright six-months infant. He was too wide awake to be backward, too backward to be four. It was not only that those prodigious eyes were so alert and penetrating. Even his clumsy efforts to manipulate his toys seemed purposeful beyond his years. Though he could not manage his fingers at all well, his mind seemed to be already setting them very definite and intelligent tasks. Their failure distressed him.</p>
<p>John was certainly intelligent. We were all now agreed on that point. Yet he showed no sign of crawling, and no sign of talking. Then suddenly, long before he had attempted to move about in his world, he became articulate. On a certain Tuesday he was merely babbling as usual. On Wednesday he was exceptionally quiet, and seemed for the first time to understand something of his mother’s baby-talk. On Thursday morning he startled the family by remarking very slowly but very correctly, “I — want — milk.” That afternoon he said to a visitor who no longer interested him, “Go — away. I — do — not — like — you — much.”</p>
<p>These linguistic achievements were obviously of quite a different type from the first remarks of ordinary children.</p>
<p>Friday and Saturday John spent in careful conversation with his delighted relatives. By the following Tuesday, a week after his first attempt, he was a better linguist than his seven-year-old brother, and speech had already begun to lose its novelty for him. It had ceased to be a new art, and had become merely a useful means of communication, to be extended and refined only as new spheres of experience came within his ken and demanded expression.</p>
<p>Now that John could talk, his parents learned one or two surprising facts about him. For instance, he could remember his birth. And immediately after that painful crisis, when he had been severed from his mother, he actually had to learn to breathe. Before any breathing reflex awoke, he had been kept alive by artificial respiration, and from this experience he had discovered how to control his lungs. With a prolonged and desperate effort of will he had, so to speak, cranked the engine, until at last it “fired” and acted spontaneously. His heart also, it appeared, was largely under voluntary control. Certain early “cardiac troubles,” very alarming to his parents, had in fact been voluntary interferences of a too daring nature. His emotional reflexes also were far more under control than in the rest of us. Thus if, in some anger-provoking situation, he did not wish to feel angry, he could easily inhibit the anger reflexes. And if anger seemed desirable he could produce it. He was indeed “Odd John.”</p>
<p>About nine months after John had learnt to speak, some one gave him a child’s abacus. For the rest of that day there was no talking, no hilarity; and meals were dismissed with impatience. John had suddenly discovered the intricate delights of number. Hour after hour he performed all manner of operations on the new toy. Then suddenly he flung it away and lay back staring at the ceiling.</p>
<p>His mother thought he was tired. She spoke to him. He took no notice. She gently shook his arm. No response. “John!” she cried in some alarm, and shook more violently. “Shut up, Pax,” he said, “I’m busy with numbers.”</p>
<p>Then, after a pause, “Pax, what do you call the numbers after twelve?” She counted up to twenty, then up to thirty. “You’re as stupid as that toy, Pax.” When she asked why, he found he had not words to explain himself; but after he had indicated various operations on the abacus, and she had told him the names of them, he said slowly and triumphantly, “You’re stupid, Pax, dear, because you (and the toy there) ‘count’ in tens and not in twelves. And that’s stupid because twelves have ‘fourths’ and ‘threeths’, I mean ‘thirds’, and tens have not.” When she explained that all men counted in tens because when counting began, they used their five fingers, he looked fixedly at her, then laughed his crackling, crowing laugh. Presently he said, “Then all men are stupid.”</p>
<p>This, I think, was John’s first realization of the stupidity of Homo sapiens, but not the last.</p>
<p>Thomas was jubilant over John’s mathematical shrewdness, and wanted to report his case to the British Psychological Society. But Pax showed an unexpected determination to “keep it all dark for the present.” “He shall not be experimented on,” she insisted. “They’d probably hurt him. And anyhow they’d make a silly fuss.” Thomas and I laughed at her fears, but she won the battle.</p>
<p>John was now nearly five, but still in appearance a mere baby. He could not walk. He could not, or would not, crawl. His legs were still those of an infant. Moreover, his walking was probably seriously delayed by mathematics, for during the next few months he could not be persuaded to give his attention to anything but numbers and the properties of space. He would lie in his pram in the garden by the hour doing “mental arithmetic” and “mental geometry,” never moving a muscle, never making a sound. This was most unhealthy for a growing child, and he began to ail. Yet nothing would induce him to live a more normal and active life.</p>
<p>Visitors often refused to believe that he was mentally active out there for all those hours. He looked pale and “absent.” They privately thought he was in a state of coma, and developing as an imbecile. But occasionally he would volunteer a few words which would confound them.</p>
<p>John’s attack upon geometry began with an interest in his brother’s box of bricks and in a diaper wallpaper. Then came a phase of cutting up cheese and soap into slabs, cubes, cones, and even into spheres and ovoids. At first John was extremely clumsy with a knife, cutting his fingers and greatly distressing his mother. But in a few days he had become amazingly dextrous. As usual, though he was backward in taking up a new activity, once he had set his mind to it, his progress was fantastically rapid. His next stage was to make use of his sister’s school-set of geometrical instruments. For a week he was enraptured, covering innumerable sheets.</p>
<p>Then suddenly he refused to take any further interest in visual geometry. He preferred to lie back and meditate. One morning he was troubled by some question which he could not formulate. Pax could make nothing of his efforts, but later his father helped him to extend his vocabulary enough to ask, “Why are there only three dimensions? When I grow up shall I find more?”</p>
<p>Some weeks later came a much more startling question. “If you went in a straight line, on and on and on, how far would you have to go to get right back here?”</p>
<p>We laughed, and Pax exclaimed, “Odd John!” This was early in 1915. Then Thomas remembered some talk about a “theory of relativity” that was upsetting all the old ideas of geometry. In time he became so impressed by this odd question of John’s, and others like it, that he insisted on bringing a mathematician from the university to talk to the child.</p>
<p>Pax protested, but not even she guessed that the result would be disastrous.</p>
<p>The visitor was at first patronizing, then enthusiastic, then bewildered; then, with obvious relief, patronizing again; then badly flustered. When Pax tactfully persuaded him to go (for the child’s sake, of course), he asked if he might come again, with a colleague.</p>
<p>A few days later the two of them turned up and remained in conference with the baby for hours. Thomas was unfortunately going the round of his patients. Pax sat beside John’s high chair, silently knitting, and occasionally trying to help her child to express himself. But the conversation was far beyond her depth. During a pause for a cup of tea, one of the visitors said, “It’s the child’s imaginative power that is so amazing. He knows none of the jargon and none of the history, but he has seen it all already for himself. It’s incredible. He seems to visualize what can’t be visualized.”</p>
<p>Later in the afternoon, so Pax reported, the visitors began to grow rather agitated, and even angry; and John’s irritatingly quiet laugh seemed to make matters worse. When at last she insisted on putting a stop to the discussion, as it was John’s bedtime, she noticed that both the guests were definitely “out of control.” “There was a wild look about them both,” she said, “and when I shooed them out of the garden they were still wrangling; and they never said good-bye.”</p>
<p>But it was a shock to learn, a few days later, that two mathematicians on the university staff had been found sitting under a street lamp together at 2 a.m. drawing diagrams on the pavement and disputing about “the curvature of space.”</p>
<p>Thomas regarded his youngest child simply as an exceptionally striking case of the “infant prodigy.” His favourite comment was, “Of course, it will all fizzle out when he gets older.” But Pax would say, “I wonder.”</p>
<p>John worried mathematics for another month, then suddenly put it all behind him. When his father asked him why he had given it up, he said, “There’s not much in number really. Of course, it’s marvellously pretty, but when you’ve done it all — well, that’s that. I’ve finished number. I know all there is in that game. I want another. You can’t suck the same piece of sugar for ever.”</p>
<p>During the next twelve months John gave his parents no further surprises. It is true he learned to read and write, and took no more than a week to outstrip his brother and sister. But after his mathematical triumphs this was only a modest achievement. The surprising thing was that the will to read should have developed so late. Pax often read aloud to him out of books belonging to the elder children, and apparently he did not see why she should be relieved of this duty.</p>
<p>But there came a time when Anne, his sister, was ill, and his mother was too occupied to read to him. One day he clamoured for her to start a new book, but she would not. “Well, show me how to read before you go,” he demanded. She smiled, and said, “It’s a long job. When Anne’s better I’ll show you.”</p>
<p>In a few days she began the task, in the orthodox manner. But John had no patience with the orthodox manner. He invented a method of his own. He made Pax read aloud to him and pass her finger along the line as she read, so that he could follow, word by word. Pax could not help laughing at the barbarousness of this method, but with John it worked. He simply remembered the “look” of every “noise” that she made, for his power of retention seemed to be infallible. Presently, without stopping her, he began analysing out the sounds of the different letters, and was soon cursing the illogicality of English spelling. By the end of the lesson John could read, though of course his vocabulary was limited. During the following week he devoured all the children’s books in the house, and even a few “grown-up” books. These, of course, meant almost nothing to him, even though the words were mostly familiar. He soon gave them up in disgust. One day he picked up his sister’s school geometry, but tossed it aside in five minutes with the remark, “Baby book!”</p>
<p>Henceforth John was able to read anything that interested him; but he showed no sign of becoming a book-worm. Reading was an occupation fit only for times of inaction, when his overtaxed hands demanded repose. For he had now entered a phase of almost passionate manual constructiveness, and was making all manner of ingenious models out of cardboard, wire, wood, plasticine, and any other material that came to hand. Drawing, also, occupied, much of his time.</p>
