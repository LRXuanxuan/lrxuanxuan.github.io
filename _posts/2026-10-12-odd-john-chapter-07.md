<h3>第七章　金融冒险</h3>
<p>从此以后，我自己的工作开始受到严重妨碍，因为约翰那项商业事业给我的任务越来越多。我有很大一部分时间都花在全国各地奔波，拜访专利代理人和制造商。约翰也常常跟我一起去。每次都得把他介绍成“我的一个小朋友，他特别想看看工厂里面是什么样子”。这样一来，他学到了许多有关各种机器的能力和局限的知识，也因此更容易设计出适合批量制造的东西。</p>
<p>正是在这些旅行中，我第一次意识到，即便约翰也有自己的缺陷——我称之为他的唯一盲点。我去见那些工业界先生时，总痛苦地意识到，他们想怎么摆布我都行。通常，专利代理人的意见能使我免遭灭顶之灾；他们本质上首先是科学技术人员，所以不仅出于职业责任，也出于同情而站在我们这一边。但制造商往往还是能绕过他们，直接找到我。有几次，我被宰得相当惨。不过久而久之，我总算学会了怎样同商业头脑周旋。约翰却似乎根本无法相信：那些人的兴趣，与其说在于生产巧妙的新东西，不如说在于压过我们，也压过其他所有人。当然，在理智上他知道事实如此。他对智人（Homo sapiens）的道德和智力同样轻蔑。可他就是无法“从骨头里感觉到”，人竟真会“蠢到把单纯挣钱当作一场技巧游戏，而且还如此在乎它”。像任何男孩子一样，他完全能够体会在肉搏中击败对手的快感，也能体会实际发明取得成功的快感；但工业竞争这种战争对他毫无吸引力。他花了好几个月、付出不少惨痛代价，才真正理解这东西对大多数人意味着什么。虽然他本人正处在一场规模不小的商业冒险中心，他却从来没有感受过商业活动本身的魅力。人的许多本能性、原始性激情，他都能兴致勃勃地投入其中；可是这些激情较为人为的表现——尤其是经济个人主义的贪欲——在他身上找不到任何自发的共鸣。后来，他当然学会预料人会表现出这类激情，也掌握了应付它们的技巧；可他始终以一种时而像孩子、时而像哲学家的轻蔑眼光看待整个商业世界。他既低于它，又高于它。</p>
<p>因此，在约翰商业生涯的第一阶段，扮演精明商人的责任落到了我身上。可惜，如前所述，我自己对这角色也极不称职；刚开始时，我们把几项很不错的发明以一个后来才发现低得可笑的价格卖了出去。</p>
<p>不过，尽管早期吃了不少亏，从长远看我们却取得了惊人的成功。我们推出了几十种巧妙装置，其中许多后来都被普遍承认为现代生活不可缺少的用品。公众注意到，战后没过几年，忽然涌现出一大批小型发明；人们说，这显示了人类能力的韧性。</p>
<p>与此同时，我们的银行存款飞快增长，而开支仍然少得可怜。我提议用我的名义在方便的地方办一个像样的工作室，约翰坚决不肯。他列出好几个站不住脚的理由反对这个计划，我便以为他只不过因为少年人爱刺激，执意不肯放弃自己的秘密巢穴。可过了一阵，他终于说出了真正的理由，吓了我一跳。“不，”他说，“我们现在还不能花钱。我们得投机。那笔银行存款必须先乘上一百，再乘上一千。”</p>
<p>我抗议说自己对金融一窍不通，而且我们很可能把现有的一切都赔掉。他向我保证，他已经在研究金融，而且脑子里已经有几个漂亮的小计划。“约翰，”我说，“你绝不能这么干。这种领域里，光有聪明远远不够。你需要半辈子研究股票市场积累的专门知识。再说，这里面几乎全靠运气。”</p>
<p>劝也没用。毕竟，他完全有理由更相信自己的判断，而不是我的判断。而且他确实拿出证据证明自己把这门功课研究得很透：一方面读财经报刊，另一方面在早晚进城的火车上主动结识本地股票经纪人，讨他们喜欢，从他们嘴里套话。这时的他早已不是当年采访马格内特先生的那个天真小孩；而且一如既往，他非常擅长让别人谈自己的本行。</p>
<p>“要么现在，要么永远别干，”他说，“战争结束后必然会出现繁荣，我们已经进入这个阶段；但再过几年，我们会陷进一次可怕的萧条，到时人们会怀疑整个文明是不是要垮掉了。你等着看。”</p>
<p>我笑他那副笃定的样子，结果换来一堂关于经济学和西方社会状况的长课。八年或十年以后，那些观点在比较先进的社会问题研究者中将会成为常识。讲完以后，约翰说：“我们把一半资本投进英国轻工业——汽车、电气之类，因为相对来说，这些产业肯定会发展。剩下一半拿来投机。”</p>
<p>“我看我们会把钱全赔光。”我咕哝道。随后我换了一条进攻路线。“再说，这么拼命挣钱，对‘高级人类’（Homo superior）来说是不是太琐碎了一点？我怀疑你其实已经中了投机的毒。我的意思是，这一切到底为了什么？”</p>
<p>“没事的，菲多（Fido），老伙计，”他回答。（差不多就是在这段时期，他开始用这个绰号叫我。我抗议时，他向我保证，他说的其实是 Phaido；据他说，这个名字同希腊语里‘光辉’一类的意思有关。）“放心，我脑子还正常。我压根不在乎金融本身，可是在智人世界里，这是最快获得力量的办法，而力量在这里就意味着钱。我必须有钱，大笔大笔的钱。别哼哼！我们已经有了一个不错的小开头，可那只是开头。”</p>
<p>“那你以前说的‘推进精神’呢？”</p>
<p>“那当然还是目标。可你好像忘了，我现在只是个孩子；而且在真正重要的事情上，我还落后得很。我得先做我能做的，再做现在还不会做的。眼下我能做的是准备——靠两样东西：（a）经验，（b）独立。明白？”</p>
<p>显然，这件事非做不可。但我怀着强烈的不安答应充当约翰的金融代理人；后来他不顾我的劝阻，坚持进行好几次疯狂投机，我开始告诉自己：把他当作一个不折不扣的天才，而不是一个聪明绝顶的孩子，恐怕是我犯下的蠢事。</p>
<p>约翰并不像迷恋实际发明那样，会自发地被金融操作吸引。而到了这时，无论发明还是金融，都越来越被两类活动挤到次要地位：研究人类社会，以及青春期带来的那些令人着迷的私人关系。买卖股票时，他显得心不在焉，而且拖拖拉拉，这使身为代理人的我恼火得很。虽然我们共同财产的大部分都挂在我的名下，我却始终无法强迫自己在没有他同意的情况下行动。</p>
<p>我们开始投机后的头六个月，赔的钱远远多于赚的钱。约翰终于惊醒，意识到照这样下去，我们很快就会一无所有。听说又一次特别惨烈的失败后，他爆发了一通，我至今记得很清楚。“该死！”他说，“看来我非得认真对待这场无聊透顶的游戏不可。可眼下还有那么多别的事要做，从长远看都重要得多。我明白了：我要在这场游戏里击败智人，也许就像智人要在杂技上击败猿猴一样困难。人的身体并不适合丛林，而我的头脑也许并不适合个人主义金融的丛林。不过我总会想办法绕过去，就像智人在杂技上想办法绕过身体限制一样。”</p>
<p>约翰有一个很典型的特点：一旦自己因为缺乏经验犯了严重错误，他从不企图遮掩。那一次，他以完全超然的口气叙述了事情经过，不责怪自己，也不为自己辩解：他，这个在智力上高于全人类的家伙，怎样被一个普通骗子耍得团团转。他结识的一名金融人士显然猜到，这孩子对投机如此热心，背后一定另有来头——多半是某个手里有钱可投的成年人，把他当作探子。这人于是对约翰特别好，还“拿自己的投资项目跟他絮絮叨叨”，同时郑重其事地要求他严守秘密。结果，“高级人类”就这样被“智人”彻底骗了。约翰坚持要我把大笔钱投进他那位朋友推荐的项目。起初我拒绝；但约翰满怀信心，说自己掌握了“绝对稳赚的内幕消息”，最后我还是让步了。那些灾难性投资的经过不必详述。总之，我们押进去的钱全部赔光，而约翰那位朋友也消失得无影无踪。</p>
<p>这次打击以后，我们有一段时间停止了投机。约翰有很多时间不在家，也不在工作室。我问他在忙什么，他通常只回答“研究金融”，不肯再透露任何消息。就在这一时期，他的健康开始变坏。消化系统一向是他身体最弱的地方，现在又给他添了麻烦，他也开始抱怨头痛。显然，他过着一种很不健康的生活。</p>
<p>他开始有许多夜晚不回家。父亲在伦敦有亲戚，于是约翰越来越频繁地获准去他们那里住。可那些亲戚没多久就受不了他的独立作风。（他每天一早消失，深夜才回来，有时第二天才出现，而且拒绝交代自己都干了什么。）于是这些探亲只好停止。不过在此期间，约翰已经发现：夏天里，即使有警察盯着，他照样可以在伦敦过流浪猫一样的生活。他告诉父母，自己认识“一个有公寓的人，随便哪天晚上都肯让他睡在那里”。事实上，我很久以后才知道，他常常睡在公园和桥底下。</p>
<p>后来我也知道了他白天都在干什么。靠一连串类似“闯门”的把戏，他设法同伦敦好几位大金融家建立了联系；在他们派汽车送他回愤怒的亲戚家，或者替他买好回北方的车票、另寄一封说明信之前，他会先想办法讨他们喜欢、逗他们开心，同时若无其事地把他们脑子里的东西尽量掏出来。</p>
<p>下面是一份样本。医生和帕克斯收到过很多类似文件，每次都会被折腾得心神不宁。</p>
<p>尊敬的先生：</p>
<p>令郎的自行车旅行昨晚不幸提前结束。他在吉尔福德附近同我的汽车发生了碰撞。他完全承认事故责任在他。孩子没有受伤，但自行车已经无法修理。当时天色已晚，我便把他带回家过夜。令郎是个非常不寻常的孩子，他对金融过早萌发的热情，使我的客人们大感有趣；谨此向您表示祝贺。今天上午，我会让司机送他到尤斯顿车站，乘十点二十六分的火车。我也会发电报告知此事。</p>
<p>谨致敬意。</p>
<p>（签名是一位人物的名字，恕我不宜披露。）</p>
<p>约翰的父母和我都知道他骑自行车旅行去了，可我们以为他去了北威尔士。他这么快就在萨里被人撞见，说明他一定把自行车用火车运了过去。不用说，约翰根本没有搭上尤斯顿十点二十六分那趟车。可怜的司机在一次交通堵塞中被他甩掉了——他直接从车里跳了出去。那天晚上，他成了另一位金融家的客人。如果我没记错，他下午较晚时来到那座宅邸门前，声称自己和母亲就住在附近，可他迷了路，也忘了地址。警察查了一圈，没在附近找到他的母亲，于是主人留他住了一夜，然后又留了一夜——实际上，他整个星期六和星期天都待在那里。我毫不怀疑，他充分利用了这段时间。星期一早上，那位大人物去上班以后，他便消失了。</p>
<p>这样冒险了几个月，又认真研读了金融、政治经济学和社会变迁方面的文献之后，约翰觉得自己已经可以重新出手。他知道我多半会怀疑他的计划，于是直接动用自己名下的钱，而且一句都没告诉我。六个月以后，他把结果拿给我看：他账户上的余额已经成了一笔相当可观的数字。</p>
<p>随着时间推移，事情越来越清楚：他已经像当初掌握数学那样，有效地掌握了金融投机。我完全不知道他究竟按什么原则操作，因为从此以后，除了偶尔在必须由成年人亲自出面会谈时替他做代理，我再也没有参与他的商业交易。我只记得有一次我们盘点形势时，他说：“其实，投机这游戏也没多大花头，只要你掌握事实，摸清钱是怎样在世界上到处渗流的。当然，这里面有一大堆纯粹碰运气的成分。你永远不能完全肯定猫会往哪边跳。可是，只要你很了解你那只猫——我是说智人——也很了解地形，从长远看就不太可能出大错。”</p>
<p>凭借这套新技术，在青春期早期的那些年里，约翰逐渐积累起一笔非常惊人的财富，其中很大一部分在法律上归我所有。他一直到真正准备大手笔动用这些钱时，才告诉父母自己已经很富，这也许显得奇怪。“没必要太早打乱他们的生活，”他说，“而且我不想让他们天天担心自己会不会说漏嘴。”另一方面，他允许父母知道我发了一笔大财——据说纯粹靠在证券交易所撞了大运——并且很高兴让我以自己的名义在许多方面帮助他的家庭，例如负担哥哥姐姐的教育费用，或者带他们全家（当然包括约翰）出国度假。顺便说一句，他父母对我的感激让我尴尬得要命。约翰还故意把事情弄得更糟：他一脸虔诚地加入赞美合唱，给我封了个“恩公”（The Benefactor）的称号；不久又故意缩短成“本尼”（The Benny），后来更进一步变成“豆子”（The Bean）。</p>

<h3><span id="a337"></span>CHAPTER VII. FINANCIAL VENTURES</h3>
<p><span class="t28">H</span><span class="t31">ENCEFORTH</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">MY</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">OWN</span><span class="t30"> </span>work was seriously interfered with by my increasing duties in connexion with John’s commercial enterprise. I spent a great deal of my time travelling about the country, visiting patent agents and manufacturers. Quite often John accompanied me. He had always to be introduced as “a young friend of mine who would so like to see the inside of a factory.” In this way he picked up a lot of knowledge of the powers and limitations of different kinds of machines, and was thus helped to produce easily manufacturable designs.</p>
<p>It was on these expeditions that I first came to realize that even John had his disability, his one blind spot, I called it. I approached these industrial gentlemen with painful consciousness that they could do what they liked with me. Generally I was kept from disaster by the advice of the patent agents, who, being primarily scientists, were on our side not only professionally but by sympathy. But quite often the manufacturer managed to get at me direct. On several of these occasions I was pretty badly stung. Nevertheless, I learned in time to be more able to hold my own with the commercial mind. John, on the other hand, seemed incapable of believing that these people were actually less interested in producing ingenious articles than in getting the better of us, and of every one else. Of course, he knew intellectually that it was so. He was as contemptuous of the morality as of the intelligence of Homo sapiens. But he could not “feel it in his bones” that men could really “be such fools as to care so much about sheer money-making as a game of skill.” Like any other boy, he could well appreciate the thrill of beating a rival in personal combat, and the thrill of triumph in practical invention. But the battle of industrial competition made no appeal whatever to him, and it took him many months of bitter experience to realize how much it meant to most men. Though he was himself in the thick of a great commercial adventure, he never felt the fascination of business undertakings as such. Though he could enter zestfully into most of man’s instinctive and primitive passions, the more artificial manifestations of those passions, and in particular the lust of economic individualism, found no spontaneous echo in him. In time, of course, he learned to expect men to manifest such passions, and he acquired the technique of dealing with them. But he regarded the whole commercial world with a contempt which suggested now the child, and now the philosopher. He was at once below it and above it.</p>
<p>Thus it was that in the first phase of John’s commercial life it fell to me to play the part of hard-headed business man. Unfortunately, as was said, I myself was extremely ill-equipped for the task, and at the outset we parted with several good inventions for a price which we subsequently discovered to be ludicrously inadequate.</p>
<p>But in spite of early disasters we were in the long run amazingly successful. We launched scores of ingenious contrivances which have since become universally recognized as necessary adjuncts of modern life. The public remarked on the spate of minor inventions which (it was said) showed the resilience of human capacity a few years after the war.</p>
<p>Meanwhile our bank balance increased by leaps and bounds, while our expenses remained minute. When I suggested setting up a decent workshop in my name in a convenient place, John would not hear of it. He produced a number of poor arguments against the plan, and I concluded that he was determined to cling to his lair for no reason but boyish love of sensationalism. But presently he divulged his real reason, and it horrified me. “No,” he said, “we mustn’t spend yet. We must speculate. That bank balance must be multiplied by a hundred, and then by a thousand.”</p>
<p>I protested that I knew nothing about finance, and that we might easily lose all we had. He assured me that he had been studying finance, and that he already had a few neat little plans in mind. “John,” I said, “you simply mustn’t do it. That’s the sort of field where sheer intelligence is not enough. You want half a lifetime’s special knowledge of the stock market. And anyhow it’s nearly all luck.”</p>
<p>It was no use talking. After all, he had good reason to trust his own judgement rather than mine. And he gave evidence that he had gone into the subject thoroughly, both by reading the financial journals and by ingratiating himself with local stockbrokers on the morning and evening trains to town. He had by now passed far beyond the naïve child that had interviewed Mr. Magnate, and he was, as ever, an adept at making people talk about their own work.</p>
<p>“It’s now or never,” he said. “We’re entering a boom, inevitable after the war; but in a few years we shall be in the midst of such a slump that people will wonder if civilization is going smash. You’ll see.”</p>
<p>I laughed at his assurance, and was treated to a lecture on economics and the state of Western society, the sort of thing that in eight or ten years was to be generally accepted among the more advanced students of social problems. At the end of this discourse John said, “We’ll put half our capital into British light industry — motors, electricity and so on, because that sort of thing is bound to go ahead, comparatively. The rest we’ll use for speculation.”</p>
<p>“We’ll lose the whole lot, I expect,” I grumbled. Then I tried a new line of attack. “Anyhow isn’t all this money-making a bit too trivial for Homo superior? I believe you’re bitten by the speculation bug after all. I mean, what is the object of it all?”</p>
<p>“It’s all right, Fido, old thing,” he answered. (It was about this time that he began to use this nickname for me. When I protested, he assured me that it was meant to be φαίδω, which name, he said, was connected with the Greek for “brilliant.”) “It’s all right. I’m quite sane still. I don’t care a damn about finance for its own sake, but in the world of Hom. sap. it’s the quickest way to get power, which means money. And I must have money, big money. Now don’t snort! We’ve made a good little start, but it’s only a start.”</p>
<p>“What about ‘advancing the spirit,’ as you called it?”</p>
<p>“That’s the goal, all right; but you seem to forget I’m only a child, and very backward too, in all that really matters. I must do the things I can do before the things I can’t yet do. And what I can do is to prepare — by getting (a) experience, (b) independence. See?”</p>
<p>Evidently the thing had to be. But it was with grave misgiving that I agreed to act as John’s financial agent; and when he insisted on indulging in various wild speculations against my advice, I began to tell myself that I had been a fool to treat him as anything but a brilliant child.</p>
<p>John’s financial operations did not spontaneously hold his attention as his practical inventions had done. And by now both kinds of activity were being subordinated increasingly to the study of human society and the absorbing personal contacts which came to him with adolescence. There was a certain absent-mindedness and dilatoriness about his buying and selling of stock, very exasperating to me, his agent. For though most of our common fortune was in my name, I could never bring myself to act without his consent.</p>
<p>During the first six months of our speculative ventures we lost far more than we gained. John at last woke up to the fact that if we went on this way we should lose everything. After hearing of a particularly devastating disaster he indulged in a memorable outburst. “Blast!” he said. “It means I must take this damned dull game much more seriously. And there’s so much else to be done just now, far more important in the long run. I see it may be as difficult for me to beat Hom. sap. at this game as it is for Hom. sap. to beat apes at acrobatics. The human body is not equipped for the jungle, and my mind may not be equipped for the jungle of individualistic finance. But I’ll get round it somehow, just as Hom. sap. got round acrobatics.”</p>
<p>It was characteristic of John that when he had made a serious mistake through lack of experience he never tried to conceal the fact. On this occasion he recounted with complete detachment, neither blaming nor excusing himself, how he, the intellectual superior of all men, had been tricked by a common swindler. One of his financial acquaintances had evidently guessed that the boy’s interest in speculation had ulterior sources, in fact that some adult with money to invest was using him as a spy. This individual proceeded to treat John extremely well, and to “prattle to him about his ventures,” anxiously pledging him to secrecy. In this way Homo superior was completely diddled by Homo sapiens. John insisted that I should put large sums of money into concerns which his friend had advocated. At first I refused; but John was blandly confident in his alleged “inside information that it’s an absolute scoop,” and finally I consented. I need not recount the history of these disastrous speculations. Suffice it that we lost everything that we had risked, and that John’s friend disappeared.</p>
<p>For some time after this disaster we refrained from speculation. John spent a good deal of his time away from home and away from his workshop also. When I asked what he had been up to, he usually said, “studying finance,” but refused any further information. During this period his health began to deteriorate. His digestion, always his weakest spot, gave him some trouble, and he complained of headaches. Evidently he was leading a rather unwholesome life.</p>
<p>He began to spend many of his nights away from home. His father had relatives in London, and increasingly often John was allowed to visit them. But the relatives did not for long tolerate his independence. (He disappeared every morning and returned late at night or not till the next day, and refused to give an account of his actions.) Consequently these visits had to cease. But John, meanwhile, had learnt that, in summer, he could live the life of a stray cat in the Metropolis, in spite of the police. To his parents he said that he knew “a man who had a flat who would let him sleep there any night.” Actually, as I learned much later, he used to sleep in parks and under bridges.</p>
<p>I learned also how he had occupied himself. By a series of tricks of the “gate-crashing” type he had managed to make contacts with several big financiers in London, and had set about captivating and amusing them, and unobtrusively picking their brains, before they sent him by car with a covering note to his indignant relatives, or paid his railway fare home to the North, and sent the letter by post.</p>
<p>Here is a specimen, one of many such documents which caused much perturbation to Doc and Pax.</p>
<p>Dear Sir,</p>
<p>Your small son’s bicycle tour came to an untimely end yesterday evening owing to a collision with my car near Guildford. He fully admits that the fault was his own. He is unhurt, but the bicycle is past repair. As it was late, I took him to my home for the night. I congratulate you on having a very remarkable boy whose precocious passion for finance was extremely entertaining to my party. My chauffeur will put him on the 10:26 at Euston this morning. I am telegraphing you to that effect.</p>
<p>Yours faithfully,</p>
<p><span class="t24">(Signed by a personage whose name I</span></p>
<p><span class="t24">had better not divulge.)</span></p>
<p>John’s parents, and I also, knew that he was on a bicycling tour, but we supposed him to have gone into North Wales. The fact that he had been so soon encountered in Surrey proved that he must have taken his bicycle by train. Needless to say, John did not turn up by the 10:26 from Euston. He gave the chauffeur the slip, poor man, by jumping out of the car in a traffic jam. That night he spent as the guest of another financier. If I remember rightly, he arrived in the late afternoon at the front door with a story that he and his mother were staying somewhere near, and that he had lost his way and forgotten the address. As police inquiries failed to discover his mother in the neighbourhood, he was kept for the night, and then for the following night, in fact for the Saturday and Sunday. I have no doubt that he made good use of the time. On Monday morning, when the great man had gone to business, he disappeared.</p>
<p>After some months spent partly on such adventures, partly on close study of the literature of finance and political economy and social changes, John felt himself ready to take action once more. He knew that I was likely to be sceptical of his plans, so he operated with money standing in his own name, and told me nothing until, six months later, he was able to show me the results in the shape of a considerable sum standing to his credit.</p>
<p>As time passed it became clear that he had mastered financial speculation as effectively as he had mastered mathematics. I have no knowledge of the principles on which he went to work, for henceforth I played no part in his business deals, save occasionally as his agent when a personal interview was to be undertaken. I remember his saying once when we were reviewing our position, “After all there’s not much in this speculation game, once you know your facts and get the hang of the way money trickles about the world. Of course there’s a frightful lot of mere flukiness about it. You can never be quite sure which way the cat will jump. But if you know your cat well (Hom. sap. I mean), and if you know the ground well, you can’t go far wrong in the long run.”</p>
<p>By the use of his new technique John gradually, throughout the earlier part of his adolescence, amassed a very imposing fortune, much of which was legally owned by me. It may seem strange that he never told his parents about his wealth until the time came for him to use it on a lavish scale. “I don’t want to upset their lives sooner than I need,” he said, “and I don’t want to give them the worry of trying not to blab.” On the other hand he allowed them to know that I had come in for a lot of money (supposed to be entirely due to luck on the Stock Exchange), and he was glad that I should help his parents in a number of ways, such as paying for the education of his brother and sister, and taking them all (John included) on foreign holidays. The parental gratitude, I may say, was extremely embarrassing. John made it more so by joining unctuously in the chorus, and dubbing me “The Benefactor,” which title he soon shortened aggravatingly to “The Benny,” and subsequently changed to “The Bean.”</p>
