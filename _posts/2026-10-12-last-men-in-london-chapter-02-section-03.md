<h2>iii. 影响过去的心灵</h2>
<p>与被动观察过去心灵活动的能力相比，我们真正对过去心灵产生作用的能力要受限得多，而且也是很晚才获得的。在你们看来，这似乎根本不可能；因为你们认为，在先前事件尚未结束以前，未来事件根本不存在。我只能再次说明：未来事件固然只有在前面的事件已经失去其时间性的存在之后，才会获得自己的时间性存在；但与此同时，一切事件也都具有永恒性的存在。这并不意味着时间是不真实的，而只是说，事件不断消逝，并不是事件流逝的全部真相。</p>
<p>像我们这样的某些心灵，能够在一定程度上站到永恒的视角上，并在其他心灵中体验过去事件的永恒面向；因此，我们也能够在一定程度上参与那些过去心灵的经验。于是，当我观察你们的精神活动时，我这项“观察”的活动，从一种意义上说，本身就位于过去。它固然是从我未来经验的立足点展开，却通过你们心灵中过去事件的永恒一面进入过去。当我真正作用于你们的心灵——比如把思想与意象灌输给这本书的作者——我的这项活动便位于你们的时代。只是可以说，它是借着我在未来取得的支点完成的，而且始终带着我全部海王星经验的眼光。</p>
<p>既然未来能够以这种罕见却重要的方式作用于过去，那么便会出现一个结果：过去事件固然会导致未来事件，可它们自己在某种程度上也成了未来事件的产物。这听上去也许难以理解。但事实上，其中的神秘并不比两个彼此认识的心灵相互作用更多。一个心灵的形态部分由另一个决定；与此同时，它本身又成为决定另一个心灵形态的因素之一。</p>
<p>我前面说过，我们影响一个过去心灵的唯一方式，是在其中提示某个观念、欲望，或其他精神事件；而这些内容必须是那个特定层次的心灵能够理解的，也必须能够用它自身经验中的材料表达。不同心灵的接受性差异很大。绝大多数心灵对我们的影响完全密不透风；即使在那些并非毫无感觉的人当中，也有很多人被自身欲望和偏见控制得极其严密，以至我们认为值得提示给他们的任何思想或价值判断，都会立刻遭到猛烈排斥。</p>
<p>这项奇异工作还有许多复杂问题与困难。首先，若我们的影响不够明智，就可能给过去的心灵造成严重伤害；例如，把某些对其本性来说过于扰乱性的观念呈现给它。有些观念与一个心灵实在过分异质，于是会被简单拒绝；但另一些观念虽然与其表层本性格格不入，却可能像火星落进火绒那样，点燃其潜藏在深处的部分。这样，一个在探索者看来直接而无害的思想，可能在过去心灵中同时造成“启示”与“恐怖”两种彼此冲突的经验；由此引发的全心灵冲突，甚至可能走向灾难。又或者，这个观念可能如此俘获、提升那个不幸的人，使他完全丧失比例感，被推动去采取某种荒诞行动，最后与自己的社会发生严重冲突。</p>
<p>另一个困难在于，我们的影响并不总是出于自愿。我们自己某些根本无意传递的思想和欲望，有时也会渗进正在观察的心灵，而后果可能相当惨烈。比如，一个缺乏经验的探索者，看见自己的“寄主”做出格外愚蠢或卑劣的事情时，忽然涌起一阵轻蔑或愤怒；这种情绪可能一下灌满一个此前一直自鸣得意的心灵，使它突然陷入莫名其妙的自我鄙弃。由于这种全新经验毫无准备地降临，它可能摧毁心灵那层由自尊构成的甲壳，连带击碎整个本来就很脆弱的精神结构。又或者，探索者本人那种清醒而狂喜的宇宙观——既看见宇宙的悲剧性，又把它视为值得崇敬——可能灼伤某个原始宗教心灵原本乐观的信仰，却又无力把它提升到更高层次的敬拜。</p>
<p>因此，与探索过去相比，我们影响过去的能力要脆弱得多，也狭窄得多。我们能够做到的，通常只是给极少数心灵一点模糊暗示：某种原本会被错过的真理或美，某条适合其具体处境的特殊准则，或者一颗能够萌发的观念种子。实现这种影响，可以是长期把心灵轻轻“倾斜”向某个大致方向；也可以——这种情况少得多——偶尔把一个精确观念或价值判断直接植入其中。</p>
<p>还有极其例外的情况，比例大约只有几十亿分之一：我们能够让某个个体持续接受一连串细致提示，而他本人则能把这些内容转化为一份或多或少忠实的报告。但在这里，我们立刻陷入两难。要承受如此细致的提示，个体必须具有极强的接受性；可是通常来说，接受性极强的人又缺少原创力。于是，他们即使能精确接收许多细节，也往往不是完全错过通讯真正的精神，就是无法把它表达成足以打动同类的形式。反过来，那些真正高度原创的心灵，即使同时也很敏感，却往往会有意识地忽略提示的具体细节。于是，他们创造出来的作品虽然忠实于提示的精神，却会采用完全体现其自身天才的形式。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" class="stapledon-body" lang="en" hidden>
<h2>iii. INFLUENCING PAST MINDS</h2>
<p>OUR POWER OF taking effect on past minds is much more restricted than our power of passively observing their processes. It is also a much more recent acquisition. To you it seems impossible; for future events, you suppose, have no being whatever until their predecessors have already ceased to exist. I can only repeat that, though future events have indeed no temporal being until their predecessors have ceased to exist with temporal being, all events have also eternal being. This does not mean that time is unreal, but that evanescence is not the whole truth about the passing of events. Now some minds, such as ours, which are to some extent capable of taking up the point of view of eternity, and of experiencing the eternal aspects of past events in other minds, can also to some extent contribute to the experience of those minds in the past. Thus when I am observing your mental processes, my activity of observing is, in one sense, located in the past. Although it is carried out from the point of view of my own experience in the future, it enters the past through the eternal side of past events in your minds. When I act upon your minds, as for instance in inculcating thoughts and images in the writer of this book, that activity of mine is located in your age. Yet it is done, so to speak, from my purchase in the future, and with all my Neptunian experience in view.</p>
<p>From this rare but important action of the future on the past it follows that past events, which themselves cause future events, are in part the product of future events. This may seem unintelligible. But in fact there is no more mystery in it than in the reciprocal interaction of two minds that know one another. The one, which owes its form partly to the other, is itself one factor determining the other’s form.</p>
<p>As I said before, the only way in which we can influence a past mind is by suggesting in that mind some idea or desire, or other mental event, which is intelligible to a mind of that particular order, and is capable of being formulated in terms of its own experience. Some minds are much more receptive than others. The great majority are wholly impervious to our influence; and, even among those who are not insensitive, very many are so strictly dominated by their own desires and prejudices that any thought or valuation which we consider worth suggesting to them is at once violently rejected.</p>
<p>There are many complications and difficulties in this strange work. In the first place we may chance to do serious damage to the past mind by unwise influence, for instance by presenting it with ideas which are too disturbing to its nature. For though some ideas are so foreign to a mind that they are simply rejected, others, though alien to the superficial part of its nature, may be incendiary to the submerged part, as sparks falling upon tinder. Thus an idea which to the explorer seems straightforward and harmless may produce in the past mind a self-discrepant experience both of revelation and of horror; and the resulting conflict throughout the mind may lead to catastrophe. Or the idea may so captivate and exalt the unfortunate person that he loses all sense of proportion, is instigated to some fantastic course of action, and finally comes into serious collision with his society.</p>
<p>Another difficulty lies in the fact that our influence is not always voluntary. Thoughts and desires of our own, which we have no intention of transmitting, may sometimes find their way into the mind under observation; and their effect may be disastrous. For instance, a sudden surge of contempt or indignation on the part of an inexperienced explorer, when he observes his ‘host’ commit some egregious folly or meanness, may flood a hitherto complacent mind with bewildering self-contempt. And this novel experience, coming thus without preparation, may shatter the whole flimsy structure of the mind by destroying its carapace of self-pride. Or again, the explorer’s own clear-eyed and ecstatic view of the cosmos, as both tragic and worshipful, may sear the optimistic faith of a primitive religious mind, without being able to raise it to the loftier worship.</p>
<p>Our influence, then, upon the past is a very much more precarious and restricted power than our past-exploration. All that we can do is to offer to a very small minority of minds some vague hint of a truth or of a beauty which would otherwise be missed, or some special precept, or seminal idea, relevant to the mind’s particular circumstances. This may be done either by a constant ‘tilting’ of the mind in a certain general direction, or, much more rarely, by occasional impregnation of the mind with some precise idea or valuation. In very exceptional cases, which number no more than one in many thousands of millions, we can subject the individual to a constant stream of detailed suggestion which he himself can embody in a more or less faithful report. But here we find ourselves on the horns of a dilemma. To undergo this detailed suggestion, the individual must be extremely receptive; but in the main those that are extremely receptive lack originality, so that, in spite of their detailed receptivity, they either entirely miss the true spirit of the communication, or fail to embody it in a manner capable of stirring their fellows. On the other hand the highly original minds, even when they are also very receptive, tend deliberately to ignore the detail of the suggestion. Thus they produce works which, though true to the spirit of the suggestion, are embodied in manners expressive of their own genius.</p>
</section>
