<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="zh" lang="zh-CN" aria-label="中文译文">
<p>当我这样凝视自己的母星时，我仍在太空中不断上升。地球明显在远处缩小；而当我向东疾驰时，它仿佛正在我下方自转。所有地表特征都向西摆去，不久，日落与大西洋中部出现在它的东缘，随后便是夜。对我来说似乎只过了几分钟，行星就已经变成一轮巨大的半月。很快，它又成了一弯朦胧而不断缩小的月牙，旁边则是它的卫星那锋利而微小的月牙。</p>
<p>我惊愕地意识到，自己必定正以一种荒诞、完全不可能的速度前进。速度如此之快，我仿佛穿行在持续不断的流星冰雹中。它们直到几乎与我并排时才看得见；因为它们只反射太阳光，所以只在一瞬间出现，化作一道道光痕，像从特快列车上看见的灯。有许多迎面与我相撞，却丝毫不能影响我。有一块巨大而不规则的岩石，足有房屋大小，着实把我吓坏了。那被照亮的庞然物在眼前迅速膨胀，在一刹那间显露出粗糙、疙疙瘩瘩的表面，接着便将我吞没。或者更准确地说，我推断它必然吞没了我；只是我穿过它的速度快得惊人，刚在中距离看见它，下一刻就已经发现自己正在把它甩到身后。</p>
<p>很快，地球便只剩下一颗星。我说“很快”，可这时我对时间流逝的感觉已经极其混乱。几分钟、几小时，也许甚至几天、几星期，都再也分不出来。</p>
<p>我还在努力让自己镇定下来，却发现自己已经越过火星轨道，正飞穿小行星带这条繁忙通道。有些微小行星离我如此之近，看起来竟像巨大的星辰，从星座背景前掠过。其中一两颗先显出凸月状、继而显出月牙状轮廓，最后才在我身后消失。</p>
<p>前方遥远处，木星已经越来越明亮，并在恒星背景间明显移动。那巨大的球体如今呈现为一个圆盘，不久竟比正在缩小的太阳还大。它的四颗主要卫星像小珍珠一样漂浮在一旁。由于云带横贯其表面，这颗行星看起来像一条条带纹的培根。云雾把它整个边缘都包裹住。我追上它，又从旁掠过。因为木星大气层极深，昼与夜彼此融汇，没有可以明确指出的分界。我看到它东侧未被太阳照亮的半球上零星分布着模糊的暗红光区；也许那是火山剧烈活动发出的辉光穿过浓厚云层向上透出。</p>
<p>几分钟之后——也许其实是几年——木星又重新缩成一颗星，随后消失在已经变小、却仍炽烈辉煌的太阳光中。外侧其他行星没有一颗靠近我的路线，但我很快意识到，自己必定已经远远越过了甚至冥王星轨道的范围。此刻的太阳不过是群星中最亮的一颗，正在我身后渐渐暗去。</p>
<p>我终于有时间感到痛苦了。如今可见的只剩布满星辰的天空。北斗、仙后座、猎户座、昴宿星团，以它们既熟悉又遥远的样子嘲弄着我。太阳现在不过是其他亮星中的一颗。什么都没有变化。难道我注定要永远这样悬在太空中，只做一个没有身体的观察点？我已经死了吗？这是不是对我那格外无效的一生的惩罚？是不是因为我顽固地想与人类事务、激情和偏见保持距离，而必须付出的代价？</p>
<p>在想象中，我挣扎着回到郊区那座山顶。我看见我们的家。门开了，一个身影走进花园，被门厅的灯照亮。她站了一会儿，沿道路两边张望，随后又走回屋里。然而这一切都只是想象。现实中，什么也没有，只有群星。</p>
<p>过了一阵，我注意到太阳以及它附近的所有星都泛着红色；而天空另一极的群星则呈冰冷的蓝。我猛然明白了这种奇怪现象的原因。我仍在运动，而且速度快到连光本身也无法完全不受我的行程影响。那些从后方追赶我的光波，要花更长时间才能赶上，因此对我而言振动频率显得比正常更低，于是我看见它们偏红；而迎面撞上我这场狂奔的光波则被挤压、缩短，所以呈现为蓝色。</p>
<p>很快，天空变成一副非同寻常的景象：正后方的所有星此刻都成了深红色，正前方的则呈紫色。我身后尽是红宝石，前方尽是紫水晶。红宝石般的星座周围铺开一圈黄玉色星辰，紫水晶般的星座周围则环绕着蓝宝石色星辰。在我航线两侧的各个方向上，这些颜色渐渐褪去，恢复为熟悉天空中钻石般的正常白色。由于我几乎沿着银河平面前进，所以银河光环在左右两侧仍呈白色，前方变成紫色，后方则变成红色。不久，正前方和正后方的星开始变暗，继而完全消失，在天空中留下两个无星洞穴，每个洞穴周围都围着一圈彩色星辰。显然，我还在继续加速。此时从前方与后方群星传来的光，已经以超出人类视觉范围的形式抵达我这里。</p>
<p>随着速度继续增加，前后两个没有星辰的区域连同各自的彩色边缘，不断蚕食横在我四周那一圈正常星空。我开始在这些正常星辰中察觉运动。由于我自己的前进，较近的恒星仿佛在更遥远的恒星背景上横向漂移。这种漂移越来越快，直到某一瞬间，整个可见天空都布满飞掠星辰的光痕。随后，一切全都消失了。大概我的速度相对于群星已经快到一种程度，以至于任何恒星发出的光都无法再以正常方式作用于我。</p>
<p>虽然此时我的速度也许已经超过光本身，我却仿佛漂浮在一口深而停滞的井底。毫无特征的黑暗，以及一切感觉的彻底缺失，使我恐惧——如果还可以把这种厌恶与不祥预感称作“恐惧”的话，因为这时它已经没有恐惧通常伴随的任何身体反应：没有发抖，没有出汗，没有喘息，也没有心悸。我凄凉地、自怜地渴望回家，渴望再次看见那张我最熟悉的脸。我用心眼看见她正坐在火边缝东西，眉间有一道细小的忧虑皱纹。我不禁想：我的身体是否正死在石南丛中？他们会不会明早在那里发现它？她将怎样面对人生中这场巨大变化？她当然会勇敢面对；可是她会受苦。</p>
<p>然而，就在我绝望地抗拒我们那珍贵的共同体原子被拆散时，我也意识到，自己内部的某种东西——那最本质的精神——极其明确地意欲继续前进，而不是退缩。这并不是说，对熟悉人类世界的思念能够被区区冒险欲抵消。我天性太恋家，不会为了危险与不适本身去主动追求它们。但命运给了我一个机会：不只是探索物质宇宙的深处，更是去发现生命与心灵在群星之间究竟扮演什么角色。正是这种机会压过了胆怯。此刻，一种强烈的饥渴占据我——不是对冒险的饥渴，而是想洞见人类，或宇宙中任何类人存在，究竟有何意义。我们这个朴素的珍宝，这朵开在现代生活干旱道路旁、坦率而带来春意的雏菊，推动我欣然接受这场奇异冒险；因为我难道不可能发现，整个宇宙并非只是尘埃与灰烬之地，其中偶尔生出一点发育不良的生命，而是在地球这片焦枯荒原之外，实际上还有一个繁花世界？</p>
<p>人类是否真如自己有时所希望的那样，至少在时间意义上，是宇宙精神正在生长的尖端？还是说，他只是数百万个生长点中的一个？又或者，从宇宙整体看，人类的重要性并不比大教堂里的老鼠高多少？再说，人类真正的职能究竟是力量、智慧、爱、崇拜，还是这一切？还是说，一旦放到宇宙尺度上，“职能”“目的”这些概念本身就毫无意义？这些严肃问题，我要去回答。我还必须学会看得更清楚一些，也更恰当地面对那种东西——只要我们偶尔真正瞥见它，它便会迫使我们敬拜。至少当时的我，是这样对自己说的。</p>
<p>在那个自视甚高的我看来，我现在已经不再是一个渴望扩张自身的孤立个体，而更像是人类的一名使者——不，是一个探索器官，一根由活着的人类世界伸向太空、试图触及同类的触须。无论代价如何，我都必须前进，即使我那微不足道的地球生活必须过早终止，即使妻子和孩子必须从此没有我。我必须前进；而且无论如何，总有一天，即使要经过几个世纪的星际旅行，我也必须回来。</p>
<p>如今我确实已经在经历最令人迷惑的冒险之后回到地球，再回首那一阶段的昂扬，我却为一件事感到沮丧：我当时渴望带给同胞的精神宝藏，与我实际能够献出的那一点东西相比，差距实在太大。也许这种失败在于，尽管我确实接受了冒险的挑战，却始终暗中保留着退路。如今我明白，恐惧与对舒适的渴望削弱了我的意志之光。那时如此大胆立下的决心，终究十分脆弱。我那不稳定的勇气经常让位于对母星的思念。在整个旅途中，我一次次产生这样的感觉：因为自己胆怯、拘泥而平庸的天性，我错过了事件中最有意义的方面。</p>
<p>旅途中我经历的一切，即使在当时，也只有一小部分能被我清楚理解；而且正如以后将要说到的，我自身的能力还曾得到一些超越人类发展程度的存在帮助。如今我重新回到母星，这种帮助已经无法获得，于是连当初达到的那一点较深洞见，我也无法完整重现。因此，我这份本来记述着人类最深远探索的记录，到头来也未必比任何一个心智被超出理解能力的经历冲击得失常的人所说的胡话更可靠。</p>
<p>现在回到故事本身。我不知道自己同自己争辩了多久；但在我作出决定后不久，绝对的黑暗再次被群星刺破。我显然已经静止，因为各个方向都能看见恒星，而且它们的颜色恢复了正常。</p>
<p>但我身上已经发生了一种神秘变化。我很快发现，只要意欲接近某一颗星，我便能够让自己朝它运动，而且速度必定远远超过正常的光。这一点，我非常清楚，在物理上是不可能的。科学家早已告诉我，超过光速的运动没有意义。因此我推断，我的运动一定在某种意义上属于精神现象，而不是物理现象；我能够连续占据一个又一个观察位置，却并不需要任何物理移动手段。我还觉得显而易见的是，如今使群星向我显现的光，也不是正常的物理光；因为我注意到，新获得的这种高效旅行方式丝毫不会改变恒星可见的颜色。无论我移动得多快，它们都保持钻石般的色调，只是与正常视觉相比，所有星都略显更亮，颜色也更浓。</p>
<p>我刚确认自己有了这种新移动能力，便开始近乎狂热地使用它。我告诉自己，这是一次天文学与形而上学研究之旅；可其实，我对地球的渴望已经在扭曲自己的目的。它使我的注意力过度集中到寻找行星上，尤其是寻找地球类型的行星。</p>
<p>我随意选择了附近一颗较明亮的恒星作为目标。我的前进如此迅速，一些较小而距离更近的光点像流星一样从旁飞掠。我贴近那颗巨大的太阳绕行，却完全感受不到它的热。在那斑驳的表面上，尽管光辉无处不在，凭借这种奇迹般的视觉，我仍看见一群巨大的黑色太阳黑子，每一个都像一个深坑，足以塞下十几个地球。沿恒星边缘，色球层的突起像火焰形成的树木、羽饰 和史前怪兽，有的踮脚站立，有的振翅欲飞，仿佛那个球体小得根本容不下它们。再向外，苍白的日冕把薄纱般的光膜铺进黑暗。我沿双曲线轨迹绕过这颗恒星，焦急寻找行星，却一颗也没有发现。我又细致地搜索一遍，近处远处反复折返。在较宽阔的轨道上，像地球这么小的东西很容易被漏掉。但我最后只找到流星和几颗稀薄得几乎没有实体的彗星。这更让我失望，因为这颗恒星看起来与熟悉的太阳颇为相似。其实我暗中希望发现的不仅是行星，甚至就是地球本身。</p>
<p>我再次冲进太空的海洋，朝另一颗附近恒星而去。又一次失望。我接近另一座孤独的熔炉；它身旁同样没有那些能够承载生命的微小尘粒。</p>
<p>现在我像一条走失、寻找主人的狗，从一颗星匆忙奔向另一颗星。我在各处乱冲，一心只想找到一颗拥有行星的太阳，并在那些行星中找到自己的家。一颗又一颗恒星被我搜索；但更多恒星则被我不耐烦地直接略过，因为我一眼便认出，它们太庞大、太稀薄、太年轻，不可能是照耀地球的那一类恒星。有些是朦胧发红的巨星，直径甚至大过木星轨道；另一些体积较小、轮廓更明确，却具有一千个太阳的亮度，颜色呈蓝色。我一直被告知，我们的太阳属于平均类型，可眼下我发现，这些庞大的年轻恒星远比那些收缩、发黄的中年恒星常见。看来，我必定误入了一个恒星形成发生得较晚的区域。</p>
<p>我也注意到巨大尘埃云——大得如同星座，会遮蔽恒星流；还注意到一些微微发光的气体区域，有时靠自身发光，有时反射恒星之光。但我注意它们只是为了避开它们。那些带着珍珠母光泽的云之大陆内部，常常暗藏着几颗朦胧的光珠，那是未来恒星的胚胎。</p>
<p>我漫不经心地掠过许多双星、三星与四星系统，其中质量相近的伴星彼此紧密结合，如同共舞华尔兹。只有一次，我遇见那种极罕见的双星：其中一颗伴星体积不过区区地球大小，质量却与一整颗巨大恒星相当，而且极其明亮。在银河这片区域上下游荡时，我偶尔发现一颗正在死亡、阴沉燃烧的恒星；也偶尔遇到已经结壳、熄灭的死星。直到我几乎撞上它们时才能看见，而且只能借整个天穹反射来的微光勉强辨认。我总尽量不去靠近，因为在那种疯狂的思乡渴望中，它们对我毫无吸引力。更何况，它们让我的心发冷，仿佛预告整个宇宙终有一死。不过，发现这样的恒星目前还很少，多少给了我一点安慰。</p>
<p>我还是没有找到行星。我很清楚，行星的诞生源于两颗或更多恒星彼此近距离掠过，而这种意外必定极为罕见。我提醒自己，在银河里，拥有行星的恒星大概就像海滩沙粒中的宝石一样稀少。我碰上一颗的机会能有多大？我开始灰心。黑暗与贫瘠烈火组成的可怕荒漠，那巨大空无中如此稀疏地点着几点闪光，整个宇宙那种庞大得令人窒息的徒劳感，恶狠狠地压在我身上。此时又添了一重痛苦：我的移动能力开始衰退。只有付出极大努力，我才能在群星间移动一点点，而且越来越慢。很快，我就会像收藏盒里的苍蝇一样被钉死在空间中；但我是孤独的，永远孤独。是的，这无疑就是为我特制的地狱。</p>
<p>我强迫自己振作起来。我提醒自己，即便命运真要如此，也没有什么大不了。没有我，地球照样可以好好存在。即使整个宇宙再没有任何别的生命世界，至少地球本身拥有生命，而且也许还能醒来，获得远比现在充实的生命。即使我已经失去了自己的母星，那个心爱的世界终究是真实存在的。更何况，我整个冒险本身就是一个奇迹；那么靠着奇迹继续发生，我难道不能偶然撞见另一个地球吗？我想起自己曾立志进行一次崇高的朝圣，想起自己是人类派往群星的使者。</p>
<p>勇气恢复之后，我的移动能力也随之恢复。显然，它依赖于一种旺盛而能够摆脱自我纠缠的精神状态。我刚才那种自怜与思乡情绪把它拖住了。</p>
<p>我决定探索银河另一片区域，也许那里有更多年老恒星，找到行星的希望也更大。于是我朝一个遥远而星辰稠密的星团前进。那个朦胧斑点状光球里的单颗恒星如此暗淡，我猜它一定远得惊人。</p>
<p>我在黑暗中不停前进。由于始终没有偏离航线去搜索，我穿过太空之海的路线从未靠近任何恒星到足以让它显成圆盘。天上的灯火在远处从我身边流过，像遥远船只的灯。经过一段让我彻底失去时间尺度的航行后，我发现自己来到一片巨大荒漠，那里没有恒星，是两条恒星流之间的空隙，是银河中的一道裂谷。银河环绕着我，各个方向仍铺着正常的遥远星尘；可这里没有任何显著光源，只有远处那个作为我目标的星团，像一团蓟草绒毛。</p>
<p>这片陌生天空令我不安，因为它提醒我，自己与家园的联系正在越来越淡。奇怪的是，看到银河最远处恒星之外那些微小污迹般的异星系，反而几乎成了一种安慰。它们比银河最幽深处还要遥远得不可比拟，而它们的外观丝毫没有受到我所有旅行的影响；这提醒我：尽管我进行了这样疯狂而奇迹般的远行，我仍然待在自己的银河系里，仍在宇宙的同一个小小细胞中，而她——我一生的伴侣——也仍生活在其中。顺带一提，我很惊讶竟有这么多外部星系能以肉眼看见，其中最大的一个呈苍白的云状斑痕，在地球天空中看起来甚至比月亮还大。</p>
<p>和那些遥远星系相比，我的旅行无论如何都不能改变它们的外观；而前方的星团却已经明显在膨胀。越过两条恒星流之间那片巨大空无后不久，星团就以一团庞大的钻石云迎面而来。很快，我进入了一个恒星更加稠密的区域；再后来，星团本身在前方彻底展开，用拥挤的光点覆盖整个前方天空。就像一艘驶近港口的船会不断遇见别的船只，我也接连靠近、掠过一颗又一颗恒星。当我深入星团核心时，已经来到一个比先前探索过的任何地方都稠密得多的区域。四面八方的天空都被太阳照得炽亮，其中许多比地球天空中的金星还明亮得多。我感到一种远洋旅行者夜间进入港湾、突然发现自己被大都会灯火包围时的兴奋。我告诉自己，在这样拥挤的区域里，恒星近距离接近的事件一定发生过许多次，因此也一定形成过许多行星系统。</p>
<p>我再次寻找太阳那样的中年恒星。此前从我身边经过的几乎全是年轻巨星，有的甚至大如整个太阳系。继续搜寻之后，我找到几颗看似有希望的恒星，却都没有行星。我还发现许多双星和三星，沿着人力无法计算的轨道运行；也有大片气体大陆，新的恒星正在其中凝结。</p>
<p>终于，终于，我找到一个行星系统。我带着几乎无法承受的希望在这些世界之间盘旋；可它们全都比木星还大，而且全部处于熔融状态。我又匆忙奔向一颗又一颗恒星。我一定访问了成千上万颗，却全都徒劳。病态而孤独的我逃出星团。它在身后缩成一团绒球，上面闪着露珠般的光点。前方，一大片黑暗遮掉银河的一部分以及附近星空，只剩少数位于我与遮蔽物之间的近星还能看见。这一定是一片巨大的气体或尘埃云，它起伏的边缘被后方亮星擦过来的光线勾勒出来。这景象让我自怜起来；在家乡多少个夜晚，我也曾看到黑云边缘被月光镀成同样的银色。可眼前挡住我的这片云，能够吞下的不只是整个世界，不只是无数行星系统，而是整个又整个星座。</p>
<p>我的勇气再一次崩溃。我痛苦地想闭上眼，把这些巨大尺度排除在外。可我既没有眼睛，也没有眼睑。我只是一个失去身体、四处漂泊的观察点。我竭力在想象中召回家里那个小小的室内空间：窗帘拉着，炉火跳动。我努力说服自己，这一切黑暗、距离与贫瘠白炽的恐怖只是一场梦；我其实正坐在火边打盹；任何时刻都可能醒来；她会从手里的针线活上抬起身，伸手碰我一下，对我微笑。然而群星仍把我牢牢囚禁。</p>
<p>我再一次开始寻找，虽然力气已经衰弱。我从一颗星游荡到另一颗星，时间也许是几天，也许是几年，也许是亿万年；终于，幸运——或者某种守护灵——把我引向一颗类似太阳的恒星。从这个中心向外望时，我忽然发现一个小小光点，它随着我的移动，在有图案的星空背景上改变位置。我猛地朝它跃去，又看见第二个、第三个。这里确实存在一个与我的太阳系颇为相似的行星系统。由于我仍被人类标准牢牢支配，我立刻寻找其中最像地球的世界。随着它的圆盘在我眼前——或者说在我下方——不断膨胀，它竟惊人地像地球。它的大气显然比我们的稀薄，因为陌生大陆与海洋的轮廓都极其清楚。和地球一样，深色海面强烈反射太阳的影像。白色云带零散铺在海洋与陆地上，而陆地也像我的母星一样，呈现绿色与棕色的斑驳色彩。不过，即使从这个高度看，我也能发现，那里的绿色比地球植被更加鲜艳，而且明显更偏蓝。我还注意到，这颗行星的海洋面积少于陆地，而几块巨大大陆的中央主要由耀眼的乳白色沙漠占据。</p>
</section>
<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="en" lang="en" aria-label="English original" hidden>
<h3>II. INTERSTELLAR TRAVEL</h3>
<p>WHILE I WAS thus contemplating my native planet, I continued to soar through space. The Earth was visibly shrinking into the distance, and as I raced eastwards, it seemed to be rotating beneath me. All its features swung westwards, till presently sunset and the Mid-Atlantic appeared upon its eastern limb, and then the night. Within a few minutes, as it seemed to me, the planet had become an immense half-moon. Soon it was a misty, dwindling crescent, beside the sharp and minute crescent of its satellite.</p>
<p>With amazement I realized that I must be travelling at a fantastic, a quite impossible rate. So rapid was my progress that I seemed to be passing through a constant hail of meteors. They were invisible till they were almost abreast of me; for they shone only by reflected sunlight, appearing for an instant only, as streaks of light, like lamps seen from an express train. Many of them I met in head-on collision, but they made no impression on me. One huge irregular bulk of rock, the size of a house, thoroughly terrified me. The illuminated mass swelled before my gaze, displayed for a fraction of a second a rough and lumpy surface, and then engulfed me. Or rather, I infer that it must have engulfed me; but so swift was my passage that I had no sooner seen it in the middle distance than I found myself already leaving it behind.</p>
<p>Very soon the Earth was a mere star. I say soon, but my sense of the passage of time was now very confused. Minutes and hours, and perhaps even days, even weeks, were now indistinguishable.</p>
<p>While I was still trying to collect myself, I found that I was already beyond the orbit of Mars, and rushing across the thoroughfare of the asteroids. Some of these tiny planets were now so near that they appeared as great stars streaming across the constellations. One or two revealed gibbous, then crescent forms before they faded behind me.</p>
<p>Already Jupiter, far ahead of me, grew increasingly bright and shifted its position among the fixed stars. The great globe now appeared as a disc, which soon was larger than the shrinking sun. Its four major satellites were little pearls floating beside it. The planet’s surface now appeared like streaky bacon, by reason of its cloud-zones. Clouds fogged its whole circumference. Now I drew abreast of it and passed it. Owing to the immense depth of its atmosphere, night and day merged into one another without assignable boundary. I noted here and there on its eastern and unilluminated hemisphere vague areas of ruddy light, which were perhaps the glow cast upwards through dense cloud by volcanic upheavals.</p>
<p>In a few minutes, or perhaps years, Jupiter had become once more a star, and then was lost in the splendour of the diminished but still blazing sun. No other of the outer planets lay near my course, but I soon realized that I must be far beyond the limits of even Pluto’s orbit. The sun was now merely the brightest of the stars, fading behind me.</p>
<p>At last I had time for distress. Nothing now was visible but the starry sky. The Plough, Cassiopeia, Orion, the Pleiades, mocked me with their familiarity and their remoteness. The sun was now but one among the other bright stars. Nothing changed. Was I doomed to hang thus for ever out in space, a bodiless view-point? Had I died? Was this my punishment for a singularly ineffectual life? Was this the penalty of an inveterate will to remain detached from human affairs and passions and prejudices?</p>
<p>In imagination I struggled back to my suburban hill-top. I saw our home. The door opened. A figure came out into the garden, lit by the hall light. She stood for a moment looking up and down the road, then went back into the house. But all this was imagination only. In actuality, there was nothing but the stars.</p>
<p>After a while I noticed that the sun and all the stars in his neighbourhood were ruddy. Those at the opposite pole of the heaven were of an icy blue. The explanation of this strange phenomenon flashed upon me. I was still travelling, and travelling so fast that light itself was not wholly indifferent to my passage. The overtaking undulations took long to catch me. They therefore affected me as slower pulsations than they normally were, and I saw them therefore as red. Those that met me on my headlong flight were congested and shortened, and were seen as blue.</p>
<p>Very soon the heavens presented an extraordinary appearance, for all the stars directly behind me were now deep red, while those directly ahead were violet. Rubies lay behind me, amethysts ahead of me. Surrounding the ruby constellations there spread an area of topaz stars, and round the amethyst constellations an area of sapphires. Beside my course, on every side, the colours faded into the normal white of the sky’s familiar diamonds. Since I was travelling almost in the plane of the galaxy, the hoop of the Milky Way, white on either hand, was violet ahead of me, red behind. Presently the stars immediately before and behind grew dim, then vanished, leaving two starless holes in the heaven, each hole surrounded by a zone of coloured stars. Evidently I was still gathering speed. Light from the forward and the hinder stars now reached me in forms beyond the range of my human vision.</p>
<p>As my speed increased, the two starless patches, before and behind, each with its coloured fringe, continued to encroach upon the intervening zone of normal stars which lay abreast of me on every side. Amongst these I now detected movement. Through the effect of my own passage the nearer stars appeared to drift across the background of the stars at greater distance. This drifting accelerated, till, for an instant, the whole visible sky was streaked with flying stars. Then everything vanished. Presumably my speed was so great in relation to the stars that light from none of them could take normal effect on me.</p>
<p>Though I was now perhaps travelling faster than light itself, I seemed to be floating at the bottom of a deep and stagnant well. The featureless darkness, the complete lack of all sensation, terrified me, if I may call ‘terror’ the repugnance and foreboding which I now experienced without any of the bodily accompaniments of terror, without any sensation of trembling, sweating, gasping or palpitation. Forlornly, and with self-pity, I longed for home, longed to see once more the face that I knew best. With the mind’s eye I could see her now, sitting by the fire sewing, a little furrow of anxiety between her brows. Was my body, I wondered, lying dead on the heather? Would they find it there in the morning? How would she confront this great change in her life? Certainly with a brave face; but she would suffer.</p>
<p>But even while I was desperately rebelling against the dissolution of our treasured atom of community, I was aware that something within me, the essential spirit within me, willed very emphatically not to retreat but to press on with this amazing voyage. Not that my longing for the familiar human world could for a moment be counterbalanced by the mere craving for adventure. I was of too home-keeping a kind to seek serious danger and discomfort for their own sake. But timidity was overcome by a sense of the opportunity that fate was giving me, not only to explore the depths of the physical universe, but to discover what part life and mind were actually playing among the stars. A keen hunger now took possession of me, a hunger not for adventure but for insight into the significance of man, or of any manlike beings in the cosmos. This homely treasure of ours, this frank and spring-making daisy beside the arid track of modern life, impelled me to accept gladly my strange adventure; for might I not discover that the whole universe was no mere place of dust and ashes with here and there a stunted life, but actually, beyond the parched terrestrial waste land, a world of flowers?</p>
<p>Was man indeed, as he sometimes desired to be, the growing point of the cosmical spirit, in its temporal aspect at least? Or was he one of many million growing points? Or was mankind of no more importance in the universal view than rats in a cathedral? And again, was man’s true function power, or wisdom, or love, or worship, or all of these? Or was the idea of function, of purpose, meaningless in relation to the cosmos? These grave questions I would answer. Also I must learn to see a little more clearly and confront a little more rightly (so I put it to myself) that which, when we glimpse it at all, compels our worship.</p>
<p>I now seemed to my self-important self to be no isolated individual, craving aggrandizement, but rather an emissary of mankind, no, an organ of exploration, a feeler, projected by the living human world to make contact with its fellows in space. At all cost I must go forward, even if my trivial earthly life must come to an untimely end, and my wife and children be left without me. I must go forward; and somehow, some day, even if after centuries of interstellar travel, I must return.</p>
<p>When I look back on that phase of exaltation, now that I have indeed returned to earth after the most bewildering adventures, I am dismayed at the contrast between the spiritual treasure which I aspired to hand over to my fellow men and the paucity of my actual tribute. This failure was perhaps due to the fact that, though I did indeed accept the challenge of the adventure, I accepted it only with secret reservations. Fear and the longing for comfort, I now recognize, dimmed the brightness of my will. My resolution, so boldly formed, proved after all frail. My unsteady courage often gave place to yearnings for my native planet. Over and over again in the course of my travels I had a sense that, owing to my timid and pedestrian nature, I missed the most significant aspects of events.</p>
<p>Of all that I experienced on my travels, only a fraction was clearly intelligible to me even at the time; and then, as I shall tell, my native powers were aided by beings of superhuman development. Now that I am once more on my native planet, and this aid is no longer available, I cannot recapture even so much of the deeper insight as I formerly attained. And so my record, which tells of the most far-reaching of all human explorations, turns out to be after all no more reliable than the rigmarole of any mind unhinged by the impact of experience beyond its comprehension.</p>
<p>To return to my story. How long I spent in debate with myself I do not know, but soon after I had made my decision, the absolute darkness was pierced once more by the stars. I was apparently at rest, for stars were visible in every direction, and their colour was normal.</p>
<p>But a mysterious change had come over me. I soon discovered that, by merely willing to approach a star, I could set myself in motion toward it, and at such a speed that I must have travelled much faster than normal light. This, as I knew very well, was physically impossible. Scientists had assured me that motion faster than the speed of light was meaningless. I inferred that my motion must therefore be in some manner a mental, not a physical phenomenon, that I was enabled to take up successive view-points without physical means of locomotion. It seemed to me evident, too, that the light with which the stars were now revealed to me was not normal, physical light; for I noticed that my new and expeditious means of travel took no effect upon the visible colours of the stars. However fast I moved, they retained their diamond hues, though all were somewhat brighter and more tinted than in normal vision.</p>
<p>No sooner had I made sure of my new power of locomotion than I began feverishly to use it. I told myself that I was embarking on a voyage of astronomical and metaphysical research; but already my craving for the Earth was distorting my purpose. It turned my attention unduly toward the search for planets, and especially for planets of the terrestrial type.</p>
<p>At random I directed my course toward one of the brighter of the near stars. So rapid was my advance that certain lesser and still nearer luminaries streamed past me like meteors. I swung close to the great sun, insensitive to its heat. On its mottled surface, in spite of the pervading brilliance, I could see, with my miraculous vision, a group of huge dark sun-spots, each one a pit into which a dozen Earths could have been dropped. Round the star’s limb the excrescences of the chromosphere looked like fiery trees and plumes and prehistoric monsters, atiptoe or awing, all on a globe too small for them. Beyond these the pale corona spread its films into the darkness. As I rounded the star in hyperbolic flight I searched anxiously for planets, but found none. I searched again, meticulously, tacking and veering near and far. In the wider orbits a small object like the earth might easily be overlooked. I found nothing but meteors and a few insubstantial comets. This was the more disappointing because the star seemed to be of much the same type as the familiar sun. Secretly I had hoped to discover not merely planets but actually the Earth.</p>
<p>Once more I struck out into the ocean of space, heading for another near star. Once more I was disappointed. I approached yet another lonely furnace. This too was unattended by the minute grains that harbour life.</p>
<p>I now hurried from star to star, a lost dog looking for its master. I rushed hither and thither, intent on finding a sun with planets, and among those planets my home. Star after star I searched, but far more I passed impatiently, recognizing at once that they were too large and tenuous and young to be Earth’s luminary. Some were vague ruddy giants broader than the orbit of Jupiter; some, smaller and more definite, had the brilliance of a thousand suns, and their colour was blue. I had been told that our Sun was of average type, but I now discovered many more of the great youngsters than of the shrunken, yellowish middle-aged. Seemingly I must have strayed into a region of late stellar condensation.</p>
<p>I noticed, but only to avoid them, great clouds of dust, huge as constellations, eclipsing the star-streams; and tracts of palely glowing gas, shining sometimes by their own light, sometimes by the reflected light of stars. Often these nacrous cloud-continents had secreted within them a number of vague pearls of light, the embryos of future stars.</p>
<p>I glanced heedlessly at many star-couples, trios, and quartets, in which more or less equal partners waltz in close union. Once, and once only, I came on one of those rare couples in which one partner is no bigger than a mere Earth, but massive as a whole great star, and very brilliant. Up and down this region of the galaxy I found here and there a dying star, sombrely smouldering; and here and there the encrusted and extinguished dead. These I could not see till I was almost upon them, and then only dimly, by the reflected light of the whole heaven. I never approached nearer to them than I could help, for they were of no interest to me in my crazy yearning for the Earth. Moreover, they struck a chill into my mind, prophesying the universal death. I was comforted, however, to find that as yet there were so few of them.</p>
<p>I found no planets. I knew well that the birth of planets was due to the close approach of two or more stars, and that such accidents must be very uncommon. I reminded myself that stars with planets must be as rare in the galaxy as gems among the grains of sand on the sea-shore. What chance had I of coming upon one? I began to lose heart. The appalling desert of darkness and barren fire, the huge emptiness so sparsely pricked with scintillations, the colossal futility of the whole universe, hideously oppressed me. And now, an added distress, my power of locomotion began to fail. Only with a great effort could I move at all among the stars, and then but slowly, and ever more slowly. Soon I should find myself pinned fast in space like a fly in a collection; but lonely, eternally alone. Yes, surely this was my special Hell.</p>
<p>I pulled myself together. I reminded myself that even if this was to be my fate, it was no great matter. The Earth could very well do without me. And even if there was no other living world anywhere in the cosmos, still, the Earth itself had life, and might wake to far fuller life. And even though I had lost my native planet, still, that beloved world was real. Besides, my whole adventure was a miracle, and by continued miracle might I not stumble on some other Earth? I remembered that I had undertaken a high pilgrimage, and that I was man’s emissary to the stars.</p>
<p>With returning courage my power of locomotion returned. Evidently it depended on a vigorous and self-detached mentality. My recent mood of self-pity and earthward-yearning had hampered it.</p>
<p>Resolving to explore a new region of the galaxy, where perhaps there would be more of the older stars and a greater hope of planets, I headed in the direction of a remote and populous cluster. From the faintness of the individual members of this vaguely speckled ball of light I guessed that it must be very far afield.</p>
<p>On and on I travelled in the darkness. As I never turned aside to search, my course through the ocean of space never took me near enough to any star to reveal it as a disc. The lights of heaven streamed remotely past me like the lights of distant ships. After a voyage during which I lost all measure of time I found myself in a great desert, empty of stars, a gap between two star-streams, a cleft in the galaxy. The Milky Way surrounded me, and in all directions lay the normal dust of distant stars; but there were no considerable lights, save the thistle-down of the remote cluster which was my goal.</p>
<p>This unfamiliar sky disturbed me with a sense of my increasing dissociation from my home. It was almost a comfort to note, beyond the furthest stars of our galaxy, the minute smudges that were alien galaxies, incomparably more distant than the deepest recesses of the Milky Way; and to be reminded that, in spite of all my headlong and miraculous travelling, I was still within my native galaxy, within the same little cell of the cosmos where she, my life’s friend, still lived. I was surprised, by the way, that so many of the alien galaxies appeared to the naked eye, and that the largest was a pale, cloudy mark bigger than the moon in the terrestrial sky.</p>
<p>By contrast with the remote galaxies, on whose appearance all my voyaging failed to make impression, the star-cluster ahead of me was now visibly expanding. Soon after I had crossed the great emptiness between the star-streams, my cluster confronted me as a huge cloud of brilliants. Presently I was passing through a more populous area, and then the cluster itself opened out ahead of me, covering the whole forward sky with its congested lights. As a ship approaching port encounters other craft, so I came upon and passed star after star. When I had penetrated into the heart of the cluster, I was in a region far more populous than any that I had explored. On every side the sky blazed with suns, many of which appeared far brighter than Venus in the Earth’s sky. I felt the exhilaration of a traveller who, after an ocean crossing, enters harbour by night and finds himself surrounded by the lights of a metropolis. In this congested region, I told myself, many close approaches must have occurred, many planetary systems must have been formed.</p>
<p>Once more I looked for middle-aged stars of the sun’s type. All that I had passed hitherto were young giants, great as the whole solar system. After further searching I found a few likely stars, but none had planets. I found also many double and triple stars, describing their incalculable orbits; and great continents of gas, in which new stars were condensing.</p>
<p>At last, at last I found a planetary system. With almost insupportable hope I circled among these worlds; but all were greater than Jupiter, and all were molten. Again I hurried from star to star. I must have visited thousands, but all in vain. Sick and lonely I fled out of the cluster. It dwindled behind me into a ball of down, sparkling with dew-drops. In front of me a great tract of darkness blotted out a section of the Milky Way and the neighbouring area of stars, save for a few near lights which lay between me and the obscuring opacity. The billowy edges of this huge cloud of gas or dust were revealed by the glancing rays of bright stars beyond it. The sight moved me with self-pity; on so many nights at home had I seen the edges of dark clouds silvered just so by moonlight. But the cloud which now opposed me could have swallowed not merely whole worlds, not merely countless planetary systems, but whole constellations.</p>
<p>Once more my courage failed me. Miserably I tried to shut out the immensities by closing my eyes. But I had neither eyes nor eyelids. I was a disembodied, wandering view-point. I tried to conjure up the little interior of my home, with the curtains drawn and the fire dancing. I tried to persuade myself that all this horror of darkness and distance and barren incandescence was a dream, that I was dozing by the fire, that at any moment I might wake, that she would reach over from her sewing and touch me and smile. But the stars still held me prisoner.</p>
<p>Again, though with failing strength, I set about my search. And after I had wandered from star to star for a period that might have been days or years or aeons, luck or some guardian spirit directed me to a certain sun-like star; and looking outwards from this centre, I caught sight of a little point of light, moving, with my movement, against the patterned sky. As I leapt toward it, I saw another, and another. Here was indeed a planetary system much like my own. So obsessed was I with human standards that I sought out at once the most earth-like of these worlds. And amazingly earth-like it appeared, as its disc swelled before me, or below me. Its atmosphere was evidently less dense than ours, for the outlines of unfamiliar continents and oceans were very plainly visible. As on the earth, the dark sea brilliantly reflected the sun’s image. White cloud-tracts lay here and there over the seas and the lands, which, as on my own planet, were mottled green and brown. But even from this height I saw that the greens were more vivid and far more blue than terrestrial vegetation. I noted, also, that on this planet there was less ocean than land, and that the centres of the great continents were chiefly occupied by dazzling creamy-white deserts.</p>
