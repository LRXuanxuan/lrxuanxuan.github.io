<h3>第十五章　雅克利娜</h3>
<p>等到这些工程问题解决之后，约翰终于又能把注意力转回他的心灵感应研究。由于他看上去仍旧太年轻，不适合独自一人在欧洲大陆上游荡，他坚持要我陪他去巴黎。快到目的地时，他显得颇为急切。这也难怪，因为他以为自己将找到一个真正可以同他平等相见的人，一个能给他带来远胜于此前任何人的满足与陪伴的同类。然而，我们在贝尔托莱街（克洛德·贝尔纳大街旁）一家小旅馆安顿下来以后，他反倒几乎泄了气。我问他怎么回事，他有点尴尬地笑了，说：“我有了一种新鲜感受。我居然害羞了！她好像并不特别盼着我来。她也不肯帮我找到她。我知道她就在拉丁区某个地方，而且常常从这条街口经过。我知道她知道有人在寻找她，可她偏偏不肯帮忙。再说，她显然已经非常老，也非常有智慧。她还记得普法战争。我一直在试着看清她照镜子时看见的东西，好弄到她自己的相貌，可总是赶不上时候。”</p>
<p>就在这时，他的头猛地一动，话却没有停顿：“我刚才一边同你说话，真正的我却一直在和她接触。她现在在一家咖啡馆里，而且还会在那里待一阵。我们去找她。”</p>
<p>他隐约感觉那家咖啡馆就在奥德翁附近，于是我们急忙赶了过去。犹豫片刻以后，他选中了一家店。刚一进门，他便兴奋地低声说：“没错，就是这里。这正是她此刻正在看见的房间。”他站了几秒钟，像个古怪的小外国人，被来回穿梭的侍者和客人挤来撞去。随后他穿过人群，走向房间另一头的一张空桌。</p>
<p>“她就在那儿。”约翰说。他的声音里带着惊讶，几乎还有几分敬畏。我顺着他的目光望去，看见近旁一张桌边坐着两个女人。一个背对着我们，不过从她纤细的身形和几乎还带着少女气息的脸颊轮廓来看，我估计她不到三十岁。另一个则老得夸张。她那张脸简直像一幅地形起伏图，满是山脊和沟谷。我失望地打量着她，因为她的神情既迟钝又乖戾，而且正带着一种令人不快的好奇盯着约翰。</p>
<p>但就在这时，另一个女人转过头来，朝屋里看了一圈。那双大眼睛绝不会认错。那是约翰的眼睛，只是眼皮垂得更低。她的目光先在我身上停了一瞬，又移向约翰。低垂的眼睑随即抬起，露出两个黝黑而高深的洞窟，仿佛比约翰的眼睛还要深不可测。整张脸顿时被聪慧和戏谑照亮。她站起身，朝约翰走来；约翰也站了起来。两个人一言不发地面对面站着。随后那女人说道：“Alors c'est toi qui me cherches toujours!”——“原来一直在找我的就是你！”</p>
<p>她并不是我原先想象的样子。除了那双巨大的眼睛之外，她几乎可以冒充一个正常女人——正常人类中稍微古怪一点的标本。她的头虽然很大，却没有大得同身体明显失衡，因为她个子很高，而且紧贴头部的帽子下面几乎看不见黑发，也就没有再给头围增添什么体积。她那张宽阔的嘴，我猜，多半还借化妆技巧刻意缩小过。</p>
<p>然而，尽管按照智人的标准她还算勉强“像个人”，她依旧极其奇异。假如我是个富于想象力的作家，而不只是个记者，也许还能用某种象征性的方式暗示她给我造成的那种几乎“令人发毛”的感觉——某种遥远、沉睡而强大的力量。可我只能记录一些明显的特征，以及她身上那种奇特的结合：婴儿般、甚至胎儿般的形态，同成熟经验并存。突出的额头、短而宽的鼻子、两只大眼之间异常宽阔的距离、整张脸惊人的宽度、从鼻翼一直延伸到嘴唇的明显沟纹——这些特征无不带着鲜明的胎儿意味；可那线条精确得像雕刻出来的嘴唇，以及眼睑细腻的造型，又构成一种饱经经验的微妙表情，让人联想到不受年龄支配的神祇。至少对我而言——当然，我已经被约翰本人的怪异预先训练过了——这张奇异的脸似乎同时兼具极端的个性与某种普遍性。它虽然带着一种隐约令人反感的粗野，却又像一个活生生的“女性”象征；与此同时，它又分明属于一个同任何其他人都截然不同的存在，一个独一无二、不可替代的个体。我从她转头去看房间里最漂亮的姑娘，竟震惊地发现，真正令我感到反感的反倒是那种正常的美。带着几乎眩晕的感觉，我又把目光转回这个可爱的怪物。</p>
<p>我观察她时，她和约翰一直完全沉默地彼此端详。过了一会儿，这位“新女性”——我已经出于私下的刻薄趣味这样称呼她了——请我们移到她那张桌子去，我们便照办了。她自称雅克利娜·卡斯塔涅。那位老妇人被介绍为勒迈特夫人。她对我们的到来明显抱有敌意，却也只好忍受。她本人平庸得彻底；然而我还是注意到，她同雅克利娜之间有某些相似之处，一些很难描述的表情和声音上的共同特征。我猜这两个女人是母女。后来事实证明，我猜对了；但同时又错得离谱。</p>
<p>接下来是一阵漫无目的的寒暄，随后雅克利娜突然用一种我完全不懂的语言说起话来。约翰先是愣了一下，随即笑了起来，也用同样的语言回答。此后大约半个小时，他们一直这样交谈，而我则用极其糟糕的法语，费尽力气同勒迈特夫人维持谈话。</p>
<p>过了一会儿，老妇人提醒雅克利娜，她们两人还有别的约会。两个女人离开以后，我和约翰仍旧在桌边坐了一阵。他沉默不语，完全陷入自己的思绪。我问他们刚才说的是什么语言。“英语。”他说，“她想告诉我很多关于她自己的事，又不想让那个老太太知道，于是就开始说倒着来的英语。我以前从没试过，不过对我们来说挺容易。”他说“我们”这个词时，语气微微加重。约翰显然知道我觉得自己被排除在外，于是继续说：“我最好把她说的要点告诉你。那个老太太是她女儿，可自己并不知道。八十三年前，雅克利娜嫁给一个叫卡泽的人，但孩子四岁时她就跑掉了。几天前她偶然碰见这个老太婆，认出她就是自己当年的小女儿，于是同她交上了朋友。勒迈特夫人还给她看了一张照片，说：‘这是我母亲，她在我很小的时候就死了——真奇怪，亲爱的，你跟她长得太像了。也许你是我的某个远房侄孙女。’雅克利娜本人出生于1765年。”</p>
<p>约翰后来向我讲述了雅克利娜那段惊人的一生，我在这里只能作个概要。她的经历完全值得写成一大厚卷，不过我关心的毕竟是约翰。</p>
<p>她的父母是农民，住在沙隆—叙尔—马恩与阿尔贡森林之间那片荒凉的地区，当地被称作“贫瘠香槟”。他们节俭得近乎吝啬。雅克利娜有着超常的智力、感受力和对生命近乎饕餮般的渴求，却是在一种极端束缚人的环境里长大的。这大概正是她早年生涯中那种强烈的享乐欲与权力欲的来源之一。她和约翰一样，成长得慢得不合常理。这令她父母很不满意：他们急着让她帮忙做家务、干农活；后来眼见别的姑娘到了可以出嫁的年纪，她却还是个胸部尚未发育的孩子，他们更是恼火。她被迫过的生活在身体上倒很健康，对精神却具有毁灭性。她很快便意识到，自己具备种种细腻经验的能力，而这些能力远远超出周围凡人的范围；理智的做法本该是把整个人生投入这些能力的锻炼与实现。可是她那单调而阴沉的生活，使她始终无法从自身较低层次的欲求中抽离出来，那就是日益增长的奢华欲和权力欲。她身处一个由半傻子组成的世界，这个事实最明显地体现在她的一个观察上：附近农家的女儿们虽然在普通的性吸引力上远胜于她，却蠢得根本不会充分利用这一优势去支配别人。</p>
<p>她甚至还没有真正进入青春期，年仅十九岁时，就已经下定决心要在她们自己的游戏里把所有人都打败，并且要成为女人中的女王。她偶尔会在邻近的圣默讷乌尔德小城看见衣着华丽的贵妇坐着马车经过，前往巴黎，或在当地旅店停留休息。她以科学研究般的仔细态度观察她们，为自己日后的手段打下基础。</p>
<p>她刚刚站在成年女性的门槛上，父母便把她许配给附近一个农场主。她逃了。凭借自己仅有的两件武器——性与智力——她一路挣扎，从最卑微、最粗暴的卖淫生活中脱身，最后成为一个富有巴黎商人的情妇。她依附他生活了好几年。到后期，她每星期只在晚餐时陪他一次，除此之外什么也不给；而仅仅她那令人无法摆脱的可怕魅力，就已经足够。</p>
<p>三十五岁那年，她有生以来第一次爱上了一个年轻画家。那人属于一批正在为巴黎绘画界即将到来的蓬勃而胜利的运动开辟道路的艺术家。这种全新的经验把长期折磨她的巨大冲突推到了顶点。过去她从事“最古老的职业”时毫无厌恶，如今却突然开始对自己感到恐惧。因为那个年轻人唤醒了她体内那些一直沉睡的能力，而她此前的生活不得不把这些能力压抑下去。她运用自己的技巧捕获了他，而且轻而易举地成功了。两人同居起来。最初几个月，他们都很幸福。</p>
<p>然而渐渐地，她终于明白，归根到底，自己所结合的这个东西，从她的角度看仍然只比猿猴好上那么一点。她当然一直知道，过去那些农民客人、巴黎客人，以及那个和善而富有的供养者，都属于“次人类”；可她曾经说服自己相信，这个艺术家是例外。即便如此，她还是依恋着这个男人。她觉得，若是同这个自己曾经误把灵魂交付出去的存在断绝关系，即使那交付本就是错误，也足以杀死她。何况，她仍真诚地——尽管毫无理性地——爱着他。他是她那只几乎像人的动物。她对他的感情，就像一个女猎人或独身女人会爱惜自己的马。他不是人，永远不可能成为她精神上的伴侣；可他是一头高贵的动物，她也以他在动物层面取得的成就为傲——也就是他在“次人类”艺术领域里的胜利。她热情地投入他的创作。她并不只是他的灵感来源；随着时间过去，她越来越实际地接管了他的艺术才能。她占有他越深，这个不幸的男人就越清楚地意识到，自己原有的天赋正被她那汹涌而丰饶的想象力压倒、窒息。他的悲剧很复杂。他似乎承认，在她影响下画出的作品，比自己独立创作的一切都更大胆，在审美上也更成功；可他同时也发现，自己的名声正在毁掉，甚至那些感觉最敏锐的同行也无法欣赏这些作品。于是他奋起争取独立，逐渐重新找回自尊和声望。事情发展到这一步，反过来唤醒了雅克利娜压抑已久的全部厌恶。两个人都拼命想摆脱对方，却又都渴求着对方。他们终于爆发争吵。争吵中，她把自己摆在一位降临凡间、要把他提升到自己高度却遭到拒绝的神祇位置上。第二天，他开枪自杀。</p>
<p>这场悲剧显然深刻影响了她那仍旧年轻的心灵。死亡这种不可挽回的终局，在她心中滋生出一种新的温柔，也让她开始尊重那些环绕着她的次人类存在。不知怎么，这次死亡缩短了她同他们之间的距离。她表达自我的激情很快便重新出现，而且有时仍会毫不留情地放纵它；可是，从那以后，这种激情始终受到一段记忆的节制：她曾经杀死过这个世界上唯一一个曾在整整一个月里让她觉得比自己更优秀的存在。</p>
<p>艺术家死后几年，雅克利娜靠着与那位商人相处时攒下的一点积蓄，在极端贫困中生活。她用男性笔名试图以写作为自己闯出名声；可是她写出来的东西离普通人的理解太远，根本无人欣赏，而她又不肯强迫自己换一种调子。四十多岁时，她仍处于成熟期最初的旺盛阶段，那种对奢华和权力的强迫性渴求突然以如此猛烈的方式卷土重来，以至于她在惊慌之下进了修道院。她并不相信教会任何明确的教义；但她决定口头上承认它的一切迷信，以换取教会生活中那一点真正存在的、共同体式的宗教体验。她觉得自己需要这种体验来加强自身较高贵的一面。然而，她一进入修道院，很快就把里面搅得天翻地覆，最后整个机构竟被迫解散。雅克利娜心中带着苦涩的笑，再度回到自己原先的职业。</p>
<p>但令她自己也吃惊的是，卖淫此时带给她的东西已经不再只是财富与权力。她在修道院里的经历并非全无所得。她对于次人类那一类存在的精神渴求了解了很多，而现在，她把这种知识充分运用起来。最初她重操旧业的动机纯粹出于自我；可是很快她就发现，在较有人性的客人中，许多人无意识里需要的远远不只是肉体满足。她开始从满足这种本质上属于精神的需求中获得一种升华感。至于肉体上的满足，她也毫不吝啬地给予。过去，同较低层次的存在发生性关系曾使她本能地厌恶；现在这种厌恶渐渐让位于对自己新职责的喜悦。许多男人真正需要的不只是交媾，而是同一个敏感却无所畏惧的女人建立亲密；还有许多人，需要有人帮助他们完成那项看似绝望的任务——“同宇宙讲和”。这些人都在雅克利娜身上找到了一口力量的深井。随着名声传播开来，人们对她提出的要求越来越多。为了避免自己彻底崩溃，她挑选了一些弟子，一些年轻女子，愿意过她这样的生活，像她一样把自己给予别人。有些人取得了一部分成功，却没有任何人能做到她所做到的程度。负担不断加重，最后她终于病倒，而且病得很重。</p>
<p>康复以后，她旧日那种自我追求的激情再次占了上风。她动用全部本领，一路向欧洲社会阶梯的上层攀登。五十七岁时，她正站在完全成熟的门槛上，嫁给了一位俄国亲王。她结婚时非常清楚，这个男人即使按普通人的标准也是个毫无价值的半傻子。此后十五年，她把手中的牌打得如此漂亮，以至于一度很有希望把丈夫送上王位。然而，日益增长的厌恶和恐惧最终又一次把她抛进精神失常。她从那场病中出来时，再次恢复成真正的自己。她断绝一切关系，乔装改扮，逃回巴黎，重新从事旧业。偶尔，她会遇见从前的某个客人，而那些人此时都已经年纪很大。可她自己仍旧保持着年轻的外表，甚至看起来还没有真正进入女性生命最盛放的阶段，所以她很容易就能让他们相信，自己只是当年那个雅克利娜的年轻侄女。</p>
<p>在这整个过程中，她始终没有生过孩子，甚至从未怀孕。年轻时，她会采取预防措施避免这种灾难；到了成熟以后，虽然她从不渴望做母亲，却已经不那么害怕冒险，谨慎也少了许多。几十年过去，剩余的谨慎也一点点消失，她开始怀疑自己天生不育，最后索性完全不再采取任何措施。从俄国回来以后，一种朦胧的感觉渐渐形成：没有经历母职，也许使她错过了一种很有价值的人生经验。到后来，这种感觉变成了明确的渴望——她想拥有一个自己的婴儿。</p>
<p>她的客人中，不止一个曾试图劝她接受婚姻。过去，她总拿这些求婚者当笑话；可是过了八十岁以后，她开始真心被一段安静的婚姻生活所吸引。她的客人里有个年轻的巴黎律师，叫让·卡泽。她并不知道他究竟是不是后来那个孩子的父亲；但当她惊讶地发现自己已经怀孕时，便选中了他作为合适的丈夫。巧的是，他此前从没想过要娶她；可等她把这个念头悄悄塞进他脑子里以后，他立刻热烈地追求起来，克服她假装出来的迟疑，最后得意洋洋地把她娶走。怀孕十一个月以后，她生下一个女儿，并且在生产中险些死去。四年的母职，加上同忠诚的卡泽共同生活，对她来说已经足够。她知道，让会珍爱这个婴儿；而他的确如此，甚至宠得孩子一辈子都受了影响。雅克利娜不仅逃离巴黎，也逃离法国，在德累斯顿重新开始了自己的人生。</p>
<p>十九世纪最后三分之二的岁月里，雅克利娜似乎一直在两种生活之间交替：一段时间从事被她提升到近乎宗教境界的娼业，另一段时间则进入婚姻。她说，她的丈夫中有过一位英国大使、一位著名作家，还有一个西非黑人——当时是法国殖民军里的一名普通士兵。此后她再也没有受孕。约翰大概猜得没错：她可能拥有一种仅凭意志就阻止受孕的能力，只是连她自己也完全不知道究竟怎样施展这种能力。</p>
<p>十九世纪结束以后，雅克利娜便不再结婚了。她更愿意继续自己的职业，因为她“非常喜欢那些亲爱的孩子们”——她所谓的“孩子”，指的是客人。她的一生必定奇异得难以想象。当然，她像任何一个娼业成员、也像任何其他行业成员一样，是为了钱而提供自己的服务。可她确实把心放在工作里，而且选择客人时考虑的并不是他们付得起多少钱，而是他们真正需要什么，以及他们是否有能力从她的帮助中获益。她似乎把妓女、精神分析师和祭司这三种职能集中到了一个人身上。</p>
<p>1914至1918年的大战期间，她又一次把自己逼得过了头。来到她身边的悲惨个案实在太多。战后，由于她完全没有民族偏见，便搬去了德国，因为那里的需要更大。1925年，她在德国再次崩溃，被迫在一家“精神疗养院”里住了一年。我们遇见她时，她已经重新在巴黎安顿下来，也重新开始工作。</p>
<p>我们在咖啡馆见面的第二天，约翰把我丢下，让我自己想办法消磨时间，他则去拜访雅克利娜。他四天没有回来。回来时，他面容憔悴，显然陷入了极大的痛苦。很久以后，他才勉强愿意告诉我自己为什么那么难过，而且也只说了几句话：“她太了不起了，可她受了伤。我帮不了她，她也不肯帮我。她对我非常非常温柔。她说她从没遇见过我这样的人，希望我们一百年前就能认识。她说我的工作会很伟大。可其实在她看来，那不过是男学生式的冒险，仅此而已。”</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" lang="en" hidden>
<h3><span id="a345"></span>CHAPTER XV. JACQUELINE</h3>
<p><span class="t28">W</span><span class="t31">HEN</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">THESE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">PROBLEMS</span><span class="t30"> </span>of engineering had been solved, John was able to turn his attention once more to his telepathic researches. As he still looked too young to be wandering about the Continent by himself, he insisted on taking me with him to Paris. When we were approaching our destination he showed signs of eagerness. Well might he, for he expected to find a being who could meet him as an equal and afford him a far more satisfying companionship than any he had yet known. But when we had lodged ourselves in a little hotel in the rue Bertholet (off the avenue de Claude Bernard) he became almost disheartened. When I questioned him he laughed awkwardly, and said, “I’m having a new sensation. I’m feeling shy! She doesn’t seem particularly keen on my coming. She won’t help me to find her. I know she’s somewhere in the Quartier Latin. She passes the end of this street quite often. I know she knows some one is looking for her, and yet she won’t help. Also, she’s evidently very old and wise. She remembers the Franco-Prussian War. I’ve been trying to see what she sees when she looks in a mirror, so as to get her face; but I can’t catch her at the right time.”</p>
<p>At that moment his head jerked, and he said without any pause, “While I was talking to you, I, the real I, was in touch with her. She’s in a certain café. She’ll be there for some time. Let’s find her.”</p>
<p>He had an obscure feeling that the café was near the Odéon, so thither we hastened. After some hesitation he selected a certain establishment, and we entered. As soon as he had passed through the door, he whispered excitedly, “This is it all right. This is the room she is seeing at the moment.” He stood for a second or two, a queer little foreigner, jostled by waiters and a stream of guests. Then he made his way to an empty table at the far side of the room.</p>
<p>“There she is,” said John, with surprise in his voice, almost with awe. Following his gaze I saw at a near table two women. One had her back to us, but I judged that she was under thirty, for her figure was slim and the curve of her cheek almost juvenile. The other was extravagantly old. Her face was a relief map; all ridges and valleys. I studied her with disappointment, for she had a dull and peevish face, and she was looking at John with offensive curiosity.</p>
<p>But now the other woman turned her head and looked about the room. There was no mistaking those large eyes. They were John’s, though heavy-lidded. For a moment they rested on me, then on John. The drooping lids were lifted to reveal two black and lofty caverns more abysmal even than John’s. The whole face lit up with intelligence and amusement. She rose, and advanced toward John, who also rose. They faced one another in silence. Then the woman said, “Alors c’est toi qui me cherches toujours!”</p>
<p>She was not what I had expected. In spite of the great eyes, she might almost have passed for a normal woman, an eccentric specimen of the normal species. Her head, though large, did not look noticeably out of proportion to her body, for she was tall, and the black hair which scarcely showed under her close-fitting hat added little to its size. Her ample mouth, I guessed, had been skilfully reduced by painting.</p>
<p>But though passably “human,” according to the standards of Homo sapiens, she was strange. Were I an imaginative writer, and not merely a journalist, I might be able to suggest symbolically something of the almost “creepy” effect she had on me, something of its remote and sleepy power. As it is I can only record certain obvious features, and in general that curious combination of the infantile or even the foetal, with the mature. The protruding brow, the short broad nose, the great distance between the great eyes, the surprising breadth of the whole face, the marked furrow from nose to lips — all these characters were definitely foetal; and yet the precisely chiselled lips themselves and the delicate moulding of the eyelids produced an expression of subtle experience suggestive of an ageless divinity. To me, at least, prepared of course by familiarity with John’s own strangeness, this strange face seemed to combine idiosyncrasy and universality. Here, in spite of a vaguely repulsive uncouthness, was a living symbol of womanhood. Yet here also was a being utterly different from any other, something unique and individual. When I looked from her to the most attractive girl in the room I was shocked to find that it was the normal beauty that was repulsive. With something like vertigo I looked once more at the adorable grotesque.</p>
<p>While I was watching her, she and John stood regarding one another in complete silence. Presently the New Woman, as I had already cynically named her for my private amusement, asked us to move to her table, which we accordingly did. Her real name was given as Jacqueline Castagnet. The old lady, introduced as Mme. Lemaître, regarded us with hostility, but had to put up with us. She was thoroughly commonplace; yet I was struck with certain points of likeness with Jacqueline, certain indescribable traits of expression and of voice. I guessed that the two women were mother and daughter. Later it turned out that I was right; and yet also quite wrong.</p>
<p>There followed a few aimless remarks, and then Jacqueline began speaking in a language quite unknown to me. For a second John looked surprised, then laughed, and answered, apparently in the same tongue. For half an hour or so they continued speaking, while I laboured to maintain conversation with Mme. Lemaître in very bad French.</p>
<p>Presently the old lady reminded Jacqueline that they were both due elsewhere. When the two women had left us, John and I remained at the table for a while. He was silent and absorbed. I asked what language they had been talking. “English,” he said. “She wanted to tell me a lot about herself, and didn’t want the old one to know about it, so she started in on English-back-to-front. I’ve never tried that before, but it’s quite easy, for us.” There was a faint stress on the “us.” John evidently knew that I felt “left out,” for he continued: “I had better tell you the gist of what she said. The old lady is her daughter, but doesn’t know it. Jacqueline was married to a man called Cazé eighty-three years ago, but she cleared out when the child was four. A few days ago she came across this old thing, and recognized her as her baby daughter, and made friends with her. Mme. Lemaître showed her a photograph ‘of my mother who died when I was quite little — strangely like you, my dear. Perhaps you are some sort of great niece of mine.’ Jacqueline herself was born in 1765.”</p>
<p>John’s account of the amazing life story of Jacqueline I can only summarize. It deserves to be recorded in a fat volume, but my concern is with John.</p>
<p>Her parents were peasants of that bleak country called “Lousy Champagne,” between Châlons-sur-Marne and the Forest of Argonne. They were thrifty even to miserliness. Jacqueline, with her supernormal intelligence and sensibility and her ravenous capacity for life, was brought up in very cramping circumstances. This was probably a cause of the passion for pleasure and power which played so great a part in the earlier phases of her career. Like John, she took an unconscionable time a-growing up. This was a grievance to her parents, who were impatient for her to help in the house and on the land, and later were indignant that at an age when other girls were ready for marriage she was still a breastless child. The life which she was compelled to lead was physically healthy, but devastating to her spirit. She soon realized that she had capacities for all manner of subtle experiences beyond the reach of her fellow mortals, and that the sane course was to devote herself to the exercise of these capacities; but her monotonous and dour existence made it impossible for her to detach herself from the less developed cravings of her nature, the increasing hunger for luxury and power. The fact that she inhabited a world of half-wits was borne in on her most obviously in the perception that the neighbouring peasants’ daughters, though they eclipsed her in normal sexual appeal, were too stupid to make full use of this asset as a means to dominance.</p>
<p>Before adolescence had properly begun, when she was only nineteen, she had already determined to beat them all at their own game, and indeed to become a queen among women. In the neighbouring town of Ste. Menehould she sometimes saw fine ladies passing through in their coaches on their way to Paris, or breaking their journey at the local inn. She observed them with scientific care, and laid the foundations of her future technique.</p>
<p>When she was on the threshold of womanhood, her parents betrothed her to a neighbouring farmer. She ran away. Making full use of her only two weapons, sex and intelligence, she struggled through from the humblest and most brutish sort of prostitution to become the mistress of a wealthy Parisian merchant. For some years she lived upon him, latterly giving him nothing in return but the terrible charm of her society once a week at dinner.</p>
<p>When she had reached the age of thirty-five she fell in love, for the first time in her life, with a young artist, one of those who were preparing the way for the vital and triumphant movement of Parisian painting. This novel experience brought to a climax the great conflict which tormented her. She who had followed the most ancient profession without repugnance was now horrified at herself. For the young man had wakened in her those dormant capacities which had perforce been thwarted by her career. She used her technique to capture him, and easily succeeded. They lived together. For a few months both were happy.</p>
<p>Gradually, however, she came to realize that, after all, she was mated to something which, from her point of view, was little better than an ape. She had known, of course, that her peasant clients, her Parisian clients, and her amiable wealthy patron, were “subhuman”; the artist, she had persuaded herself, was an exception. Yet she still clung to her man. To break with the being to whom she had given her soul, even though in error, would, she felt, have killed her. Moreover, she still genuinely, though irrationally, loved him. He was her almost-human animal. She cared for him as a huntswoman and a spinster might care for her horse. He was not human, and could never be the mate of her spirit; but he was a noble animal and she was proud of his animal attainments, namely of his triumphs in the sphere of “subhuman” art. She entered into his work with enthusiasm. She was not merely his source of inspiration; increasingly she took command of his artistic faculty. The more she possessed him, the more clearly the unhappy man realized that his native genius was being overborne and suffocated by the flood of her fertile imagination. His was a complex tragedy. He seems to have recognized that the pictures which he produced under her influence were more daring and aesthetically more triumphant than anything he could produce without her; but he realized also that he was losing his reputation, that even the most sensitive of his fellow-artists could not appreciate them. He made a stand for independence, and began to regain his self-respect and reputation. On her this turn of affairs had the effect of rousing all her suppressed disgust. Each was striving to be rid of the other, yet each craved the other. There was a quarrel, in which she played the part of the divinity who had come down to raise him to her own level, and was rejected. Next day he shot himself.</p>
<p>This tragedy evidently had a profound effect on her still juvenile mind. The finality of the deed bred in her a new tenderness and respect for the subhuman beings who surrounded her. Somehow this death lessened the distance between her and them. Though her passion for self-expression soon returned, and though she sometimes indulged it ruthlessly, it was tempered by the recollection that she had killed the one being in the world who for a whole month had seemed to her superior to herself.</p>
<p>For a few years after the death of the artist Jacqueline lived in great poverty on savings which she had accumulated in her association with the merchant. She tried to make a name for herself as a writer, under a masculine pseudonym; but the stuff which she produced was too remote to be appreciated, and she could not bring herself to write in a different vein. When she was in her forties, and still in the first flush of maturity, her obsessive craving for luxury and power returned with such insistence that, in a panic, she became a nun. She did not believe any of the explicit doctrines of the Church; but she made up her mind to pay lip service to all its superstitions for the sake of its bicker of genuine and corporate religious experience, which, she felt, she needed for the strengthening of her better nature. Her presence in the nunnery, however, very soon caused such an upheaval that the institution was finally disbanded, and Jacqueline, with bitter laughter in her heart, returned to her original calling.</p>
<p>But to her own surprise prostitution now afforded her something more than the means to wealth and power. Her experience in the nunnery had not been wholly barren. She had learned a good deal about the spiritual cravings of the subhuman kind; and this knowledge she now put to good use. Her motive in returning to prostitution had been purely self-regarding, but she soon discovered that the more human of her clients were suffering from an unconscious need for something more than carnal satisfaction. And she found exaltation in ministering to this essentially spiritual need. Carnal satisfaction she gave ungrudgingly. Her own initial distaste at intercourse with beings of a lowly order gave way to delight in her new office. Many a man whose real need was not merely copulation but intimacy with a sensitive yet fearless woman, many who needed moreover help in the seemingly hopeless task of “coming to terms with the universe,” found in Jacqueline a well of strength. As her reputation grew, ever greater demands were made on her. Hoping to save herself from a breakdown, she chose disciples, young women who were ready to live her life and give themselves as she gave herself. Some of them were partially successful, but none could do as she had done. The strain increased until at last she fell seriously ill.</p>
<p>When she recovered, her old self-seeking passion was once more uppermost. Using all her prowess she fought her way up the social ladder of Europe, till, at fifty-seven and on the threshold of full maturity, she married a Russian prince. She did so knowing that he was a worthless creature and a half-wit, even by normal standards. So skilfully did she play her cards during the next fifteen years that she had a good prospect of setting him on the throne. Increasing disgust and horror, however, flung her into another mental disorder. From this she emerged once more her true self. She cut adrift, disguised herself, and fled back to Paris to carry on her old profession. Occasionally she met one or other of her former clients, now well advanced in years. But as she herself had retained her youthful appearance, and indeed seemed to have the full flower of womanhood still ahead of her, she easily persuaded them that she was the former Jacqueline’s young niece.</p>
<p>All this while she had never had a child, never conceived. In her early years she had taken precaution to avoid such a disaster; but in maturity, though she had felt no craving for motherhood, she had been less reluctant to risk it, and less cautious. As the decades passed and her remaining caution dwindled out, she came to suspect that she was sterile, and in the end she ceased to take any precautions at all. On her return from Russia an obscure sense that in missing motherhood she had missed a valuable experience developed into a definite hunger to have a baby of her own.</p>
<p>Not a few of her clients had tried to persuade her to accept marriage. Hitherto she had laughed at these suitors, but when she had passed her eightieth year, she began to be seriously attracted by the prospect of a spell of quiet married life. Among her clients was a young Parisian lawyer, Jean Cazé. Whether he was in fact the father of her child she did not know; but when, to her amazement, she found that she had conceived, she singled him out as a suitable husband. He, it so happened, had never thought of marrying her; but when she had slipped the idea into his mind, he pressed her ardently, overcame her feigned reluctance and carried her off in pride. After eleven months of pregnancy she bore her daughter, and very nearly died in the ordeal. Four years of maternal duties and of companionship with the faithful Cazé were enough for her. Jean, she knew, would treasure the infant; and indeed he did, to the extent of spoiling her for life. Jacqueline fled not only from Paris but from France, and started all over again in Dresden.</p>
<p>Throughout the last two-thirds of the nineteenth century Jacqueline appears to have had alternating spells of exalted prostitution and marriage. She counted among her husbands, she said, a British ambassador, a famous writer, and a West African Negro who was a private in the French colonial army. Never again did she conceive. Probably John was right in surmising that she had the power of preventing conception by an act of volition, though she had no idea how she exercised this power.</p>
<p>Since the close of the nineteenth century Jacqueline had not indulged in marriage. She had preferred to carry on her profession, because of her “great affection for the dear children,” by which she meant her clients. Hers must have been a strange life. Of course she gave herself for money, like any member of her profession, or of any other profession. Nevertheless, her heart was in her work, and she chose her clients, not according to their power to pay, but according to their needs and their capacity to benefit by her ministrations. She seems to have combined in her person the functions of harlot, psycho-analyst and priest.</p>
<p>During the war of 1914-18 she was drawn into overstraining herself once more. So many tragic cases came her way. And after the war, being wholly without national prejudices, she moved to Germany, where the need was greater. It was in Germany, in 1925, that she had once more collapsed, and was forced to spend a year in a “mental home.” When we met her she was again established in Paris, and again at work.</p>
<p>On the day after our meeting in the café John had left me to amuse myself as best I could while he visited Jacqueline. He stayed away four days, and when he returned he was haggard and obviously in great distress. Not till long afterwards could he bring himself to tell the cause of his misery, and then he said only, “She’s glorious, and hurt, and I can’t help her, and she won’t help me. She was terribly kind and sweet to me. Said she had never met any one like me, wished we’d met a hundred years ago. She says my work is going to be great. But really she thinks it’s just schoolboy adventure, no more.”</p>
