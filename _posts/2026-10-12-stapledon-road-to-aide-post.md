<p>凌晨两点，在比利时，一名救护车司机走下车，打了个呵欠。从前一夜起雨就一直下，整个世界都湿透了。不过西风终于占了上风，追逐着云层，把那一队队溃散的云赶得层层叠叠。忽然，月亮露了出来。街道一边是白色的残屋，像一群挤在一起的羊，朝着东方和那里的照明弹望去；另一边是黑黢黢的废墟，断墙和残梁剪在天空上。司机站了一会儿，看着这一切。他本来叹了口气，却很成功地把它改成了一个呵欠，然后转身去准备车辆，好装两个担架伤员。接着他走进那片从前是儿童操场的地方，朝救护站走去——救护站原来是学校的地下室。今晚他们把伤员从学校地下室抬出来，怎么这么慢？他看了看建筑物上一个新洞，那是一发炮弹刚打穿的。他站在一堆瓦砾旁看月亮。一团巨大的、洁白而竖直的云正从头顶飞过。他仰望那云的侧面，仿佛自己站在一座荒诞的空中比萨斜塔脚下；它在深得仿佛直通宇宙的天空里，永远向他倾倒下来。月亮用她那意味深长的方式望着他，仿佛正拼命想把什么好消息告诉他。有那么一刻，他被这突如其来的美迷住了。随后他想起自己此刻身在何处，便郑重其事地对着月亮又打了一个呵欠。</p>
<p>伤员终于被抬了出来。一个呻吟着，另一个一声不响；前一个人的脸半埋在毯子下面，痛苦地扭动着，另一个人的整张脸都裹在白色绷带里。两副担架很快安置上车，司机和担架员坐好，那辆老旧的车沿街慢慢爬去。</p>
<p>呻吟的那个人很有规律地呻吟着，只有汽车颠簸得让他突然叫出声时才会中断。另一个一直躺着不动。司机想：我可真是个躲在后方的懒汉！这些老伙计会怎么看我？那个呻吟的人是个上了年纪的庄稼汉，对他来说，战争像是在耕作、成家、生儿育女的一生之后，硬生生接上了一个极不相称的末章。它确实不相称，可他没有抱怨。他并不是那种以英勇见长的人，但别人要求他做的事，他一样也没有逃。如今他完全被疼痛吞没，只求这段路赶快结束；又或者，他会迷失在庄稼、牲畜和炸裂炮弹组成的荒诞幻象里，转眼却又发现自己重新落进疼痛的火炉。另一个仍然不动；谁也猜不出他的灵魂此刻游荡在大地上，还是游荡在空洞的天穹中。司机想，这真是场可怜的把戏。我为什么不早早参军？他并没有什么和平主义原则，而且不喜欢那些自称和平主义者的人。战争也许是一场可怕的错误，可他那些在加里波利和佛兰德作战的朋友，正在堂堂正正地死去。他们都超越了自己。与其独自做一个自以为是的清高鬼，不如和同伴们一起犯下一个可怕的错误、一起承担苦难。在他看来，战争并不是有条理的仇恨，而是发了疯的爱。英格兰在召唤他，而英格兰比上帝更切近。再说，谁说战斗就是错的？最好的东西往往是战斗争来的；上帝也曾与撒旦交战。倘若上帝是个和平主义者，那《失乐园》可真要失去多少东西！</p>
<p>司机就这样想着，驶过月光照亮的林荫道。到了医院，伤员被卸下车，他看着这两个被打碎的人被抬进那扇门——那扇门已经接纳过太多与他们一样的人。</p>
<p>黎明初起时，司机匆匆往回赶。东方泛着玫瑰色的光，仿佛灾祸从未从那个方向来过，仿佛这世界上从来没有仇恨。他把车开进这片仙境，清晨的喜悦也开始在他心里苏醒。然而万物温柔的模样并没有动摇他的决心。他当然必须去参军，必须和朋友们一道献出生命。对他这样的人来说，红十字还不是一副足够沉重的十字架。日出吞没了残余的夜色，整片天空都燃烧起来。他要去，他一定要去。既然那么多比他更优秀的人毫不迟疑地投入战斗，他又算什么，凭什么来审判？他的贵格会父母一定会非常痛苦，可他必须这么做。一想到父母的悲伤，他自己也难过起来。说到底，战争的确是件丑恶至极的事。事实上，就在他下定决心去打仗的同时，幻灭已经开始侵蚀这个决心。一个隐秘的声音说：你去战斗，只因为你羞于不战斗。你是为了让自己心安才去战斗，不是为了胜利，也不是为了事业。你并没有把自己忘进这项事业里。你甚至得不到你所寻找的心安。太阳从东方的云堤后猛地闪出来，树木、田野和闪光的运河骤然亮得仿佛在笑。司机让汽车轰鸣着向前，忍不住高声喊道：“上帝啊，这是怎样一个世界！”既然他终于肯听，那太阳和乡野无疑都在替那个隐秘的声音作证。</p>
<p>他曾听人说过，正如私人之间的杀人终有一天会过时，战争也终有一天会退出历史；而这场大战，不过是一个新时代血红的黎明，在那个时代，许多幽暗之处都将得到照明。倘若“和平与善意”还不能成为今日的理念，那么它们总会成为明日的理念。那些已经隐约看见明日伟大理念的人，若为了今日最高尚的理想而背弃它，也要有祸了。命运使他比那些正在作战的朋友们更早瞥见了一线曙光；倘若他闭上眼睛，那便有祸了。</p>
<p>他回来时并不快乐，不满足，甚至也谈不上确信；可有一点他非常确定：他不会去打仗。他对那个新理念——其实也是一个极古老的理念——看得还很朦胧；但那终究是一种异象，而异象要求他的忠诚。也许归根结底，他仍然犯了一个错误；可那至少会是一个高贵的错误。哪怕要拿灵魂的生命去冒险，他也必须追随这个异象。</p>
<p>司机把车倒回原位，咚咚地走进营地，把自己最要好的冤家从床上拽起来，又哄那只小狗去舔熟睡厨子的脸，然后开始洗漱。在那片怀疑的荒野中，他后来还受过许多次诱惑。可每一次，那个异象都比先前清楚一点。</p>
<p>他算是一种典型，不是吗？</p>

  <section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="en" lang="en" aria-label="English original" hidden>
<p>IN BELGIUM AT two o’clock in the morning, an ambulance driver stepped out of his car and yawned. It had rained since the previous night and the world was very wet. But at last the west wind was victoriously pursuing the clouds, piling their disordered companies one upon another. Suddenly the moon shone. White ruined houses on one side of the street, huddled like sheep, looked towards the East and the star shells. Dark ruined houses on the other side held their broken walls and rafters against the sky. The driver stood for a moment watching: he began a sigh, but successfully turned it into a yawn, and moved away to prepare his car for two stretcher cases. Then he walked into the place that was once a children’s playground, toward the Aide post, once the school cellar. How slow they were to-night in bringing out the school cellar. How slow they were to-night in bringing out the wounded? He examined a new hole where a shell had gone through the building. He stood by a heap of debris and watched the moon. A mighty white upright cloud was flying overhead. He looked up the sides of it as if he were standing at the foot of some extravagant aerial leaning tower of Pisa, for ever falling upon him through a sky visibly deep as the universe. The moon looked at him in that significant way of hers, as if she were desperately trying to tell him some good news. For a moment he stood fascinated by this sudden beauty. Then he remembered himself, and carefully yawned in the face of the moon.</p>
<p>They brought out the wounded; one moaning, the other silent; the one face half hidden under rugs and miserably moving; the other face wholly hidden under white bandages. The stretchers were soon stowed on board, driver and brancardier took their seats, and the old bus crept down the street.</p>
<p>The moaning man moaned with regularity, save when the car bumped him into a cry. The other lay still. What an embusqué slacker I am! thought the driver What must these old fellows think of me? The moaning man was a vieux papa for whom war was an incongruous, last chapter to a life of tilling and begetting. It was incongruous, but he had not complained. Gallantry was not his line, but he had not shirked anything that he was expected to do. Now he lay absorbed in his pain, praying for the end of the journey, or losing himself among grotesque visions of crops and beast and bursting shells, only to find himself once more in a furnace of pain. The other lay still; no one can guess where his spirit wandered, upon the earth or in the hollow sky. It’s a miserable game, thought the driver, Why didn’t I enlist long ago? He had no peace principles, and he disliked people, who said they were pacifists. War might be a horrible mistake, but his soldier friends in Gallipoli and Flanders were dying well. They had excelled themselves. Better make a hideous mistake and suffer with one’s fellows than be a lone prig. For him, war was not scientific hate; it was love gone mad. England demanded him, and England was a nearer thing than God. Besides, who said it was wrong to fight? The best things were won by fighting; and God fought Satan. What a Paradise Lost if God had been a pacifist!</p>
<p>So thought the driver, as he drove down moonlit avenues. At the hospital, the car was unloaded, and he saw the two broken men carried through the door that had received so many like them.</p>
<p>Now in the early dawn that driver came hurrying back. There was a rose pink glow in the East, as if no ill had ever come out of that quarter; as if hate were never in this world. Into this fairy land he drove, and the joy of morning began in him. But the gentle appearance of things did not shake his resolution. Surely, surely, he must enlist, and give his life with his friends. The Red Cross was not a heavy enough cross for such as he. The sunrise swallowed aid that was left of the night; the whole sky was on fire. He would go, he would go. What was he that he should judge, when so many finer men had not hesitated to fight? His Quaker parents would be very grieved, but he must do it. He himself was unhappy thinking of his parents’ grief. After all war was indeed a hideous thing. In fact his determination to fight began already his disillusionment. A secret voice saying You will fight only because you are ashamed not to fight. You will fight for you own peace of mind, not for victory, not for the cause. You have not forgotten yourself in the cause. You will not even find the peace of mind you seek. The sun flashed from behind the Eastern cloudbank and the trees and fields and sparkling canal seemed suddenly to laugh, so bright they grew. Oh God, what a world! cried the driver aloud while the car roared along. The sun and the countryside undoubtedly confirmed that secret voice now that he allowed himself to attend to them.</p>
<p>He had heard someone say that just as private killing went out of date so will war someday go also, and that this War is but the red dawn of a new age wherein many obscurities will be enlightened. Surely if Peace and Goodwill could not be the idea of to-day they would be the idea of to-morrow. Woe unto those who, having any inkling of that great idea of to-morrow, desert it even for the highest of to-day’s ideals. The Fates had made him to have some glimpse of the dawn, before his fighting friends: Woe to him if he closed his eyes.</p>
<p>Not happy, nor content, nor even positive, was he on his return; but very sure that he would not fight. His vision of the new idea (which is also so old an idea) was very faint; but it was a vision, and commanded his allegiance. Perhaps after all he was making a mistake; but it was a noble mistake. The vision must be followed even at the risk of his soul’s life.</p>
<p>The driver backed his car into its place; stumped into the camp, pulled his best enemy out of bed, persuaded the puppy to lick the cook’s slumbering face; and began his morning toilet. Many times again he was tempted in that wilderness of doubt. Each time the vision was a little clearer than before.</p>
<p>He is a type, is he not?</p>
