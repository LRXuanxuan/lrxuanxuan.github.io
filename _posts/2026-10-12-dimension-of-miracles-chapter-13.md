<h2>第十三章</h2>
<p>讲完自己的故事以后，莫兹利沉默了很长时间。他显得闷闷不乐，神情封闭，仿佛陷入了一大堆不愉快的念头。过了一会儿，他总算振作起来，说道：“卡莫迪，像我这种地位的人，总会受到各种慈善机构的请求纠缠。我每年都会慷慨捐助‘贫困碳基生命氧气基金’，也会给‘星际再开发基金会’、‘宇宙安置之家’和‘拯救未成熟生命计划’捐款。依我看，这已经足够满足我的人道主义本能了，而且这些捐款还能抵税。”</p>
<p>“好吧。”卡莫迪突然生出一阵自尊。“反正我也不要你的施舍。”</p>
<p>“请不要打断我。”莫兹利说。“我刚才说，我的这些慈善捐助已经完全足以满足我的人道主义本能。我不喜欢处理个别案例，因为那会把事情搞得又麻烦、又私人化。”</p>
<p>“我完全理解。”卡莫迪说。“我想我最好现在就走。”他补了一句，尽管他压根不知道自己该去哪里，也不知道该怎么去。</p>
<p>“我已经请你别打断我了。”莫兹利说。“正如我所说，我不喜欢接手私人个案。不过这一次，我准备破个例，帮你回到你的行星。”</p>
<p>“为什么？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“一时兴起。”莫兹利说。“纯粹心血来潮，也许还掺了一丁点利他主义。还有——”</p>
<p>“还有什么？”</p>
<p>“这个嘛，如果你最后真的能回到家——即使有我的帮助，这一点仍然很值得怀疑——我希望你替我捎句话。”</p>
<p>“没问题。”卡莫迪说。“给谁？”</p>
<p>“还能给谁？当然是当初委托我建造那颗行星的那个大胡子老头。我想他现在还管事吧？”</p>
<p>“我不知道。”卡莫迪说。“这个问题已经争论很久了。有些人说他还在那里，一直都在；另一些人说他已经死了——不过我想他们说的是一种比喻意义上的死亡；还有些人坚持认为，他从一开始就根本不存在。”</p>
<p>“他还在那里。”莫兹利斩钉截铁地说。“那种家伙，你拿撬棍都打不死。至于他现在看起来好像缺席了，这倒很符合他的性格。他这人情绪化得很，而且满脑子崇高道德，还指望别人都照着他的道德生活。他会闹脾气；事情一旦发展得不合他意，他完全可能暂时消失一阵。况且他很懂分寸：他知道任何东西都不能给人太多，不管是烤牛肉、漂亮女人，还是上帝。真要是他的话，他很可能故意把自己从菜单上撤下来，等大家重新对他产生胃口再回来。”</p>
<p>“您好像很了解他。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“这个嘛，我有很长时间可以琢磨他。”</p>
<p>“而且我觉得我应该指出，”卡莫迪果然指出道，“您对他的这种理解，和我听说过的任何神学观点都不一致。说上帝会情绪化、会闹脾气——”</p>
<p>“可他当然必须会！”莫兹利说。“而且还远远不止这些！他一定是个情感极端丰富的生物！毕竟，你就是这样；我想你的其他人类同胞也差不多。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪点了点头。</p>
<p>“那不就得了！他明确说过，要按照自己的形象进行创造。很明显，他确实这么做了。你一来到这里，我就认出了你身上的家族相貌。卡莫迪，你身体里有一个小小的上帝，不过千万别因此冲昏头脑。”</p>
<p>“我从来没和他有过任何接触。”卡莫迪说。“我不知道该怎么把口信给他。”</p>
<p>“这还不明显吗！”莫兹利露出不耐烦的样子。“你回到家以后，只要用坚定、清楚的声音直接说出来就行。”</p>
<p>“您凭什么觉得他会听见？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“他想不听都不行！”莫兹利说。“那毕竟是他的行星，而且他已经表现出对自己那些房客的深切兴趣。如果他希望你们用其他方式和他联系，他自然早就表示出来了。”</p>
<p>“好吧，我会这么做。”卡莫迪说。“您想让我告诉他什么？”</p>
<p>“其实也没什么大事。”莫兹利突然显得有些不自在。“不过说真的，那位老先生相当有品格，我对当年给他造的那颗行星一直有点过意不去。倒不是说那颗行星真有什么问题；认真讲，它完全能用，各方面也都凑合。不过那个老头是位真正的绅士。我是说，他有格调；而这种东西你怎么都嫌见得太少。所以，我有点想给他的行星做一次翻修——完全免费，你明白吧，gratis，一分钱都不要他的。如果他愿意，我能把那颗行星改造成一个样板工程，真正的天堂。跟你说，我真的是个该死的好工程师；为了混口饭吃，我平时不得不做出来的那些硼砂货色，根本不能拿来评价我的真实水平。”</p>
<p>“我会转告他。”卡莫迪说。“不过坦白讲，我觉得他不会接受。”</p>
<p>“我也觉得他不会。”莫兹利闷闷不乐地说。“他是个固执的老头，从来不肯欠别人情。不过我还是想把这个提议正式说出来，而且我是真心的。”莫兹利犹豫了一下，又说，“你也可以顺便问问他，什么时候愿不愿意过来聊聊天。”</p>
<p>“您为什么不去看他？”</p>
<p>“我试过几次，他都不肯见我。你们那老头报复心还挺重！不过也许哪天他会消气。”</p>
<p>“也许吧。”卡莫迪将信将疑地说。“总之，我会转告。不过莫兹利先生，如果您想和一个上帝聊天，为什么不找梅利克隆？”</p>
<p>莫兹利仰起头大笑。“梅——利——克隆！那个白痴？他是个自命不凡、自我中心的蠢货，性格里没有任何值得讨论的东西。我宁愿找条狗谈形而上学！严格从技术角度讲，神性不过是力量与控制的问题，你知道；里面没有任何魔法，也不是包治百病的灵药。没有两个上帝是一样的。你知道这一点吗？”</p>
<p>“不知道。”</p>
<p>“记住它。谁也说不准这种知识什么时候会派上用场。”</p>
<p>“谢谢。”卡莫迪说。“你知道，在这之前，我根本不相信任何上帝存在。”</p>
<p>莫兹利若有所思地说：“按我的理解，上帝——一个或者多个——的存在既明显又不可避免；相信上帝，就像相信一个苹果一样轻松、自然，意义也不比相信苹果更多或更少。真要说起来，只有一件事妨碍人们形成这种信念。”</p>
<p>“什么？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“商业原则。它比重力定律还根本。你走遍银河，到处都能找到食品生意、造房子的生意、战争生意、和平生意、治理生意，等等。当然，也少不了‘上帝生意’，人们把它叫作‘宗教’，而且这是一门尤其令人反感的买卖。我可以花整整一年谈宗教兜售的那些扭曲而恶心的观念，不过我想你以前已经听够了。我只提其中一点。这一点似乎埋在一切宗教说教的底下，而在我看来，它几乎称得上精致绝伦的变态。”</p>
<p>“是什么？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“就是那块深埋在宗教地基最底层的、最根本的虚伪基岩。你想想：一个生物如果没有自由意志，就不能说它是在崇拜。可自由意志，顾名思义，是自由的。也正因为自由，它必然难以驾驭、无法计算；那是一份真正近乎神性的礼物，是使自由状态成为可能的能力。处在自由状态本来就是件狂野而奇异的事，而且显然就是故意被设计成这样的。可宗教拿这件事怎么办？它们说：‘很好，你拥有自由意志；现在，你必须运用你的自由意志，把自己奴役给上帝，也奴役给我们。’何等厚颜无耻！一个连苍蝇都不愿强迫的上帝，竟被描绘成至高无上的奴隶主！面对这种东西，任何有骨气的生物都必须反抗；他要么完全按照自己的意愿和选择去侍奉上帝，要么就根本不侍奉。只有这样，他才既忠于自己，也忠于上帝赐给他的那些能力。”</p>
<p>“我想我明白你的意思了。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“我讲得太复杂了。”莫兹利说。“其实还有一个简单得多的理由可以让人避开宗教。”</p>
<p>“什么理由？”</p>
<p>“你只要看看它的风格——浮夸、训诫味十足、甜得发腻、居高临下、矫揉造作、不合时宜、无聊透顶，不是塞满阴沉乏味的意象，就是充斥精神抖擞的标语——这种东西拿去哄老糊涂的老太太和还没断奶的婴儿正合适，除此以外谁也不该受这个罪。我不相信当初在这里见过的那个上帝会走进教堂；他的品味太好，性情太凶猛，愤怒和骄傲都太强。我不信，所以对我来说，事情到这里就结束了。我为什么要去一个连上帝自己都不会进去的地方？”</p>
</section>
<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="en" lang="en" aria-label="English original" hidden>
<p><b>CHAPTER 13</b></p>
<p>After finishing his story, Maudsley was silent for a long time. He seemed morose and withdrawn, and filled with unhappy thoughts. But after a while he roused himself and said, ’Carmody, a person in my position is always beset by requests from various charities. I give generously every year to the Oxygen Fund for Indigent Carbon-Forms. I also contribute to the Interstellar Redevelopment Foundation, the Cosmic Settlement Home, and the Save-the-Immature programme. This seems to me quite sufficient, and is also tax-deductible.’</p>
<p>‘All right,’ Carmody said, with a sudden flash of pride. ‘I don’t want your charity anyhow.’</p>
<p>‘Please do not interrupt me,’ Maudsley said. ‘I was saying that my charities are quite sufficient to fulfil my humanitarian instincts. I do not like to take up individual cases because it gets messy and personal.’</p>
<p>‘I quite understand,’ Carmody said. ‘I think I had better go now,’ he added, though he didn’t have the slightest idea of where he was going or how he would get there.</p>
<p>‘I asked you not to interrupt me,’ Maudsley said. ‘Now, I don’t like to take personal cases, as I said. But I am going to make an exception this time and help you get back to your planet.’</p>
<p>‘Why?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘A whim,’ Maudsley said. ‘The merest fancy, with perhaps a touch of altruism thrown in. Also –’</p>
<p>‘Yes?’</p>
<p>‘Well, if you ever get home – which is dubious in spite of my help – I would appreciate your delivering a message.’</p>
<p>‘Sure,’ Carmody said. ‘Who’s the message to?’</p>
<p>‘Why, obviously, to the bearded old man for whom I built the planet. I suppose he’s still in charge?’</p>
<p>‘I don’t know,’ Carmody said. ‘There’s been a great deal of discussion on that point. Some people say he’s there just as he always has been. But others say he’s dead (though I think that’s meant metaphorically), and still others maintain that he never existed in the first place.’</p>
<p>‘He’s still there,’ Maudsley said with conviction. ‘You couldn’t kill a fellow like that with a crowbar. As for his apparent absence, that’s very like him. He’s moody, you know, and filled with high morals which he expects people to live by. He can be peevish, he can just drop out of sight for a while if he doesn’t like how things are going. And he can be subtle; he knows that people don’t like too much of anything, no matter if it’s roast beef, lovely women, or God. So it would be just like him to remove himself from the bill of fare, so to speak, until an appetite has been built up for him again.’</p>
<p>‘You seem to know a lot about him,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Well, I’ve had a lot of time to think about him.’</p>
<p>‘And I think that I should point out,’ Carmody pointed out, ‘that the way you see him is not in accord with any theological view that I’ve ever heard. The idea that God can be moody, peevish –’</p>
<p>‘But he must be those things,’ Maudsley said. ‘And much more besides! He must be a creature of extreme emotionality! After all, that’s how <i>you</i> are and, I presume, how your fellow humans are.’</p>
<p>Carmody nodded.</p>
<p>‘Well, there you are! He stated plainly that he was going to create in his own image. And obviously, he did so. The moment you came here, I recognized the family resemblance. There is a little God in you, Carmody, though you shouldn’t let that go to your head.’</p>
<p>‘I’ve never had any contact with him,’ Carmody said. ‘I don’t know how to give him a message.’</p>
<p>‘It’s so plain!’ Maudsley said, with an air of exasperation. ‘When you get home, you must simply speak up in a firm, clear voice.’</p>
<p>‘What makes you think he’ll hear me?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘He can’t help but hear you!’ Maudsley said. ‘It <i>is</i> his planet, you know, and he has shown his deep interest in his tenants. If he had wanted you to communicate in any other way, he would have shown it.’</p>
<p>‘All right, I’ll do it,’ Carmody said. ‘What do you want me to tell him?’</p>
<p>‘Well, it isn’t anything much, really,’ Maudsley said, suddenly ill at ease. ‘But he was quite a worthy old gentleman, really, and I’ve felt a bit bad about the planet I built him. Not that there’s anything <i>wrong</i> with the planet, when you come right down to it. It’s quite serviceable and all that. But this old guy was a gentleman. I mean, he had class, which is something you never see too much of. So I’d kind of like to do a renovation on that planet of his, entirely free you understand, gratis, it wouldn’t cost him a cent. If he’d go for it, I could turn that planet into a showplace, a real paradise. I’m really a hell of a good engineer, let me tell you; it’s quite unfair to judge me by the borax I have to turn out to earn a buck.’</p>
<p>‘I’ll tell him,’ Carmody said. ‘But very frankly, I don’t think he’ll take you up on the offer.’</p>
<p>‘I don’t think he will, either,’ Maudsley said morosely. ‘He’s a stubborn old man and he doesn’t want favours from anyone. Still, I do want to make the offer, and I mean it in all sincerity.’ Maudsley hesitated, then said, ‘You might also ask him if he’d care to drop around for a chat sometime.’</p>
<p>‘Why don’t you go to see him?’</p>
<p>‘I tried that a couple of times, but he wouldn’t see me. He’s got quite a vindictive streak, that old man of yours! Still, maybe he’ll relent.’</p>
<p>‘Maybe,’ Carmody said doubtfully. ‘Anyhow, I’ll tell him. But if you want to talk to a God, Mr Maudsley, why don’t you talk to Melichrone?’</p>
<p>Maudsley threw back his head and laughed. ‘Meli-chrone! That imbecile? He’s a pompous, self-centred ass, and he has no character worth considering. I’d rather talk metaphysics with a dog! Technically speaking, Godhead is a matter of power and control, you know; there’s nothing magical about it, and it’s not a cure-all for what ails you. No two Gods are alike. Did you know that?’</p>
<p>‘No, I didn’t.’</p>
<p>‘Bear it in mind. You can never tell when a piece of information like that will prove useful.’</p>
<p>‘Thank you,’ Carmody said. ‘You know, before this, I didn’t believe in any God at all.’</p>
<p>Maudsley looked thoughtful and said, ‘To my way of thinking, the existence of a God or Gods is obvious and inevitable; and belief in God is as easy and natural as belief in an apple, and of no more or less significance. When you come right down to it, there’s only one thing that stands in the way of this belief.’</p>
<p>‘What’s that?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘It is the Principle of Business, which is more fundamental than the law of gravity. Wherever you go in the galaxy, you can find a food business, a house-building business, a war business, a peace business, a governing business, and so forth. And, of course, a God business, which is called “religion,” and which is a particularly reprehensible line of endeavour. I could talk for a year on the perverse and nasty notions that the religions sell, but I’m sure you’ve heard it all before. But I’ll just mention one matter, which seems to underlie everything the religions preach, and which seems to me almost exquisitely perverse.’</p>
<p>‘What’s that?’Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘It’s the deep, fundamental bedrock of hypocrisy upon which religion is founded. Consider: no creature can be said to worship if it does not possess free will. Free will, however, is <i>free.</i> And just by virtue of being free, is intractable and incalculable, a truly Godlike gift, the faculty that makes a state of freedom possible. To exist in a state of freedom is a wild, strange thing, and was clearly intended as such. But what do the religions do with this? They say, “Very well, you possess free will; but now you must use your free will to enslave yourself to God and to us.” The effrontery of it! God, who would not coerce a fly, is painted as a supreme slavemaster! In the face of this, any creature with spirit must rebel, must serve God entirely of his own will and volition, or must not serve him at all, thus remaining true to himself and to the faculties God has given him.’</p>
<p>‘I think I see what you mean,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I’ve made it too complicated,’ Maudsley said. ‘There’s a much simpler reason for avoiding religion.’</p>
<p>‘What’s that?’</p>
<p>‘Just consider its style – bombastic, hortatory, sickly-sweet, patronizing, artificial, inapropos, boring, filled with dreary images or peppy slogans – fit subject matter for senile old women and unweaned babies, but for no one else. I cannot believe that the God I met here would ever enter a church; he had too much taste and ferocity, too much anger and pride. I can’t believe it, and for me that ends the matter. Why should I go to a place that a God would not enter?’</p>
</section>
