<h3>第五章　思想与行动</h3>
<p>这件事之后的六个月里，约翰越来越不依赖长辈。父母知道他完全有能力照顾自己，因此几乎彻底放任他自行其是。他们很少盘问他每天都在干些什么，因为两个人都厌恶窥探别人隐私；而且约翰的行动看起来也并没有什么神秘之处。他仍在继续研究人类以及人类的世界。有时，他会主动讲起当天冒险中的某一段经历；有时，在讨论某个问题时，他又会从自己积累的大量材料中抽出一个例子来说明观点。</p>
<p>虽然他的兴趣在某些方面仍很孩子气，可从谈话中已经很明显地看出，在另一些方面，他正在飞速成熟。他仍会连续几天埋头制作机械玩具，比如电动船。他的电气铁路系统不断扩张，线路、隧道、高架桥和玻璃顶车站像迷宫一样铺满花园。他自制模型飞机，也在许多飞行比赛里获胜。做这些事情时，他骨子里似乎就是个典型的学生，只不过技巧和创造性都异常惊人。但实际上，他真正花在这些活动上的时间并不很多。唯一占据他相当大比例时间的少年爱好，是航海。他给自己造了一只极小却真正适航的独木舟，既装帆，又装了一台旧摩托车发动机。他常常驾驶它在河口和海岸一带探索，一待就是许多个小时，同时观察海鸟——他对海鸟有一种令人吃惊的热情，有时甚至近乎痴迷。对此，他略带歉意地解释：“它们做那些简单工作时，比人类做复杂工作有格调多了。看看塘鹅怎么飞，再看看杓鹬怎么把嘴插进泥里找食。依我看，人类在自己的发展道路上，大概也就像最早学会飞的鸟那么聪明。他是一种精神上的始祖鸟。”</p>
<p>就连那些偶尔完全抓住约翰的最幼稚活动，也常常会被他性格中更成熟的那一面这样照亮。比如他喜欢《漫画剪报》，一半是自然而然地觉得好玩，另一半则是在欣赏“自己居然会喜欢这种东西”这件事本身有多傻。</p>
<p>约翰一生中从未真正摆脱童年兴趣。即便到了最后阶段，他仍然随时可能表现出纯粹的学生式恶作剧和幻想游戏。不过，这一面此时已经开始从属于成熟的一面。比如我们知道，他已经在形成自己关于个人应有目标、社会政策和国际事务的意见。我们也知道，他正在大量阅读物理学、生物学、心理学、天文学，而且哲学问题已经开始严肃地占据他的头脑。他对哲学的反应，与普通的哲学型成年人大不相同。每当某个经典哲学难题第一次吸引他的注意，他就会一头扎进相关文献，连续扎扎实实读上一个星期；随后又会把哲学整个扔到一边，直到下一个难题自己找上门来。</p>
<p>这样几次突袭哲学领地以后，他终于发动了一次真正的长期战役。将近三个月，哲学似乎成了他最主要的智力兴趣。那时正值夏天，他喜欢在户外学习。每天早晨，他都会骑自行车出发，后架上绑着一箱书和食物。到了构成河口海岸线的黏土悬崖顶上，他把车留下，爬到岸边，在那里安顿一整天。脱掉衣服，换上那件少得可怜的“泳装”以后，他就躺在烈日下阅读或思考。有时，他会停下来游个泳，或者在潮间泥滩上游荡，看那些鸟。附近有一座废弃石灰窑，他从那里捡石头垒了两堵矮墙，再横铺两块锈蚀的波纹铁皮，便成了避雨处。有时涨潮，他会划独木舟从海上过去。风平浪静的日子里，人们甚至会在离岸一两英里的海面看见他，一边随水漂荡，一边读书。</p>
<p>有一次，我问约翰，他的哲学研究进展如何。他的回答值得记下来。“哲学，”他说，“对于正在成长的头脑其实非常有帮助，可也特别让人失望。起初，我还以为自己终于碰见了真正成熟的人类智力在工作。读柏拉图、斯宾诺莎、康德，还有一些现代实在论者时，我几乎觉得终于遇到了自己的同类。我能和他们并肩前进，玩他们的游戏，而且感觉这游戏调动了我过去从未使用过的能力。有时我跟不上他们，好像漏掉了某个关键步骤。那种对关键点苦思不解，同时又觉得自己终于遇到真正大师级头脑的兴奋感，真是不得了！可随着我一个哲学家接一个哲学家读过去，又到处随便翻看，我开始意识到一个骇人的事实：那些关键点根本不是我原先以为的深奥之处，而只是离谱的低级错误。我原本觉得，这些明明发展得很好的头脑怎么可能犯如此简单的错误，于是出于尊敬，我把这种可能性排除掉，一心去寻找藏在后面的深刻真理。可天哪，我错了！一个低级错误接着一个低级错误！有时，一个哲学家的对手会抓到他的错误，于是为自己的聪明得意得不得了。但据我所能发现的，大多数错误根本从来没人发现。哲学是一张惊人的织物：真正高明的思考，同不可思议的幼稚错误缠在一起。它就像给狗磨牙的那种橡胶‘骨头’，拿来练脑子的牙齿好得很；可真当食物——屁用没有。”</p>
<p>我试着提醒他，也许他其实还没有资格评判那些哲学家。“不管怎么说，”我说，“你年纪小得荒唐，根本不该这么早啃哲学。有些经验领域，你压根还没碰过。”</p>
<p>“当然有。”他说，“不过——比如说，我现在几乎没有什么性经验。可即便如此，要是有人说性——在正确定义的前提下——才是一切农业活动背后真正的动机，我还是一眼就能看出他在胡说八道。再举一个例子，我现在没有宗教经验。也许将来会有，也许根本就不存在这种东西。但我完全看得出来，宗教经验——同样，在正确定义的前提下——既不能证明太阳绕着地球转，也不能证明宇宙有某个目的，比如所谓人格的圆满实现。哲学家的错误大多没有这么明显，但性质是一样的。”</p>
<p>我现在说的这段时期，约翰还不到九岁。那时我根本不知道，他其实过着双重生活，而隐藏的一面还颇具通俗惊险剧色彩。只有一次，我的怀疑短暂地被唤起；可当时一闪而过的那个可能性实在太荒诞、太可怕，我根本没法认真相信。</p>
<p>一天早晨，我碰巧去温赖特家借托马斯的一本医学书。大概是十一点半左右。约翰最近养成了深夜读书、上午晚起的习惯，此时正被愤怒的母亲从床上赶出来。“先来吃早饭，再穿衣服。”她说，“我可不再给你留着了。”</p>
<p>帕克斯请我喝“上午茶”，于是我们俩坐到了早餐桌旁。没过多久，约翰眯着眼、皱着眉出现了，睡衣外面套着晨袍。我和帕克斯东一句西一句闲聊。说着说着，她忽然道：“玛蒂尔达今天带来一个真够吓人的故事。”玛蒂尔达是洗衣妇。“她讲得兴高采烈。她说今天早上有人在马格内特先生家的花园里发现一个被杀的警察，据说是被刀捅死的。”约翰一句话也没说，继续吃早饭。我们又谈了一阵，随后发生了一件让我骤然警觉的事。约翰伸手越过桌面去拿黄油，晨袍袖口往上缩，露出一截手臂。手腕内侧有一道颇难看的擦伤，里面还残留着一些脏东西。我相当肯定，前一天晚上见他时，那里还没有伤口。这件事本身当然毫不稀奇，真正让我不安的是：约翰自己也看见了那道擦伤，随后立刻朝我瞥了一眼。他的目光同我的目光相接，只有一瞬间；然后他若无其事地拿起黄油碟。就在那一瞬间，我仿佛看见午夜里的约翰顺着落水管爬回自己卧室，在攀爬时擦伤了手臂；而且我莫名觉得，他正是从马格内特家回来。我立刻让自己清醒过来，提醒自己那不过是一道再普通不过的擦伤；约翰正深深沉浸在自己的智力冒险里，不可能有兴致半夜胡闹；而且无论如何，他也聪明得不至于让自己冒上谋杀罪名的风险。可是——刚才那一眼又算什么？</p>
<p>那起谋杀给整个郊区提供了好几个星期的谈资。最近附近已经发生了一连串手段极其高明的入室盗窃，警方正在全力寻找罪犯。死者被发现仰面躺在花坛里，胸口有一道干净利落的刀伤。他的心脏被刺穿，所以肯定“当场”死亡。房子里丢了一条钻石项链和其他几件贵重首饰。窗台和落水管上留下的轻微痕迹表明，窃贼很可能是从楼上攀进攀出。如果真是这样，他就必须先爬上落水管，然后只靠双手——说得更准确些，几乎只靠指尖——横向攀过那座仿伊丽莎白式房屋外墙的一根装饰木梁。这几乎是不可能完成的动作。</p>
<p>警方先后逮捕了好几个人，但始终没有查出真正的凶手。不过，那阵入室盗窃潮倒就此停止；随着时间过去，整件事也渐渐被人遗忘。</p>
<p>说到这里，最好引用一些约翰在多年以后亲口告诉我的资料。那实际上已是他生命最后一年；殖民地已经顺利建立，却还没有被“文明”世界发现。那时我已经在考虑为他作传，并养成一个习惯：每当发生什么特别的事件或谈话，就尽快记下笔记。因此，对于这起谋杀，我现在可以大致用约翰自己的话来叙述。</p>
<p>“那阵子我的精神状态糟透了，”约翰说。“我知道自己跟所有见过的人都不一样，可还没有意识到究竟不一样到什么程度。我不知道自己这一生到底要拿来做什么，但我知道，很快总会找到某件非常大、非常绝望的事情，而我必须先为它做好准备。还有，别忘了，我毕竟还是个孩子；我有孩子对通俗惊险戏码的爱好，却同时又有成年人的狡猾和决心。</p>
<p>“我不可能让你真正理解自己当时陷在怎样可怕的一团混乱里，因为你的头脑毕竟不是沿着和我相同的路线运转。不过你可以这样想。我发现自己被扔进了一个彻底令人困惑的世界。里面的人建立起一套庞大的思想和知识体系，而我非常清楚地看出，那里面到处都是错误。从我的角度看，这体系在有限范围内当然足够可靠，也能应付实际用途；可若说它是在描述世界本身，那简直疯得离谱。然而正确的描述究竟是什么，我又找不到。我太年轻，掌握的数据不够，大片经验领域仍在我之外。所以我就像一个人身处陌生黑屋，只能伸手摸索一个又一个不认识的物体。可与此同时，我全身又痒得发疯，只想赶紧开始自己的工作——要是我能先弄明白那工作究竟是什么就好了。</p>
<p>“还得再加上一点：随着年纪增长，我越来越孤独，因为能够同我真正碰面的、能够走到半路来接我的人越来越少。帕克斯算一个。祝福她，她确实能帮上忙，因为有时候她真的会从我的角度看见东西。即使她看不见，她也有足够的见识猜到，我所看见的是真实的什么，而不只是幻想。不过从根本上说，她终究明确属于你们那一边，不属于我。然后还有你；你比帕克斯盲得多，不过对我行动的一面更有同情心。”</p>
<p>我半认真半恶作剧地插嘴：“至少算一条可靠的猎犬。”约翰笑了。我又补充：“而且偶尔还会因为纯粹的忠诚，突破犬类能力的上限，升华到理解你的程度。”他看着我笑了笑，却没有像我所期待的那样表示同意。</p>
<p>“总之，”他继续说，“我那时孤独得该死。我仿佛住在一个由幻影或者活动面具组成的世界里。没有谁看起来真正活着。我甚至有过一个很怪的念头：如果拿针扎你们中的任何一个，流出来的不会是血，只会‘噗’地喷出一股风。我也弄不明白，你们为什么会是这种样子，我究竟在你们身上缺少了什么。真正的问题是，当时我自己也还没看清：在我身上，到底是什么东西使我同你们不同。</p>
<p>“我的困惑中渐渐浮现出两个明确的结论。第一，也是最简单的一点：我必须让自己独立，必须获得力量。在我发现自己身处的这个疯狂世界里，这意味着得弄到很多钱。第二，我必须尽快尝遍各种经验，而且必须准确体验自己面对各种经验时会产生怎样的反应。</p>
<p>“以我当时孩子气的想法，至少可以靠干几起入室盗窃，同时开始满足这两项需要。我能拿到钱，也能获得经验，还能非常仔细地观察自己的反应。良心一点也没有刺痛我。我觉得马格内特先生以及他那一类人，本来就是合适的猎物。</p>
<p>“我先开始研究技术。一部分靠读书，一部分则靠同一个警察朋友讨论——后来我不得不杀掉的正是这个警察。我还在邻居家做过一连串实验性质、完全无害的入室行动。我夜里一家接一家进去，找到他们藏着的小财物，却什么也不拿，然后回家睡觉，对自己的进步十分满意。</p>
<p>“终于，我觉得自己已经能开始真正干活。第一户人家里，我只拿了几件老式首饰，因为我推测他们很长时间都不会发现。后来我开始拿现代首饰、现金和银器。真正把东西弄到手，困难少得惊人；麻烦得多的是怎么把它们脱手。我最后同一艘远洋船的事务长达成了安排。他每隔几周会回到我们郊区的家里一次，然后买下我的赃物。我毫不怀疑，他到了外国港口再转手，至少能卖到给我价钱的十倍。现在回头看，我才意识到自己的运气有多好——这项生意的‘出口环节’竟然从没给我招来灾难。那个事务长太容易被警方盯上了。当然，我当时对社会仍然了解得太少，根本意识不到危险。我再聪明，也缺数据。</p>
<p>“就这样，几个月里一切顺利得不得了。我进过几十户人家，从事务长那里收了几百英镑。不过，这场入室盗窃传染病自然已经把整个郊区弄得彻底沸腾。实际上，为了分散警察注意，我不得不把行动扩展到其他地区。显然，如果无限期继续下去，我迟早会被抓。可我已经被这种游戏狠狠咬住了。它给我一种独立和力量的感觉，尤其是独立——从你们那个疯狂世界中独立出来。</p>
<p>“我答应自己再干三票。第一票，也是唯一真正做成的一票，就是马格内特家。我事先相当仔细地勘察过地点，也把警察的行动规律摸得相当清楚。真正动手那晚，一切都按计划进行，直到我口袋里塞得鼓鼓囊囊，装满马格内特太太的珍珠和钻石——她要是把全部行头都戴上，准像伊丽莎白女王——开始沿那段指尖横攀路线返回。突然，一束手电光从下面照到我身上，一个平静而愉快的声音说：‘这回逮住你了，小伙子。’我没出声，因为我认出了那个声音，不想让对方也认出我的声音。那名警员正是我最熟的朋友史密森；此前他在完全不知情的情况下教给我太多东西。</p>
<p>“我只靠指尖吊在那里，一动不动，飞快思考，同时把脸贴向墙面。可隐瞒身份已经毫无用处，因为他说：‘快点，约翰，孩子，下来吧，不然你会掉下来摔断腿。你挺有种，不过这次你输了。’</p>
<p>“我静止在那里最多不过三秒，可就在那三秒里，我第一次以从未有过的方式看见了自己和自己的世界。一个我此前已经摸索了很久、却始终不敢确定的念头，突然以完全清晰、完全确定的形式显现在我面前。早在那以前一段时间，我就已经开始认为，自己明确属于一个不同于智人（Homo sapiens）的生物物种——而手电后面那条和善的猎犬就属于智人这个物种。但直到那一刻，我才第一次意识到，这种差异还意味着一种我现在会称作极其深远的精神差异：我的人生目的、我对生命的态度，将会不同于普通物种所能想象的一切；我仿佛正站在一个新世界的门槛上，而那个世界远远超出当时统治这个星球的十六亿粗糙动物所能触及的范围。这个发现几乎让我生平第一次真正感到害怕——一种畏惧。我也同时明白，盗窃这场游戏根本不值得冒险；此前我的行为其实非常像低等物种的成员，为了一种廉价的自我表现，竟拿自己的未来——以及比个人成败更重要得多的东西——去下注。如果那条和善的猎犬抓住我，我就会失去独立。从此以后，我会被人知道、被做上标记，落入法律掌握之中。这绝不能发生。此前所有孩子气的冒险，其实都只是为一项终于开始在我面前显出大致轮廓的终身事业所作的、盲目而笨拙的准备。既然我是这样一个独一无二的存在，我的任务就是在这个星球上‘推进精神’。当时我的脑中突然闪出这句话。虽然在那个早期阶段，我对于‘精神’是什么、它怎样‘推进’，还只有极其模糊的概念，可我已经非常清楚地看见：在更实际的一面，我必须着手完成自己的任务——要么接管普通物种，教会它发挥自身最好的一面；要么，如果这条路证明不可能，就由我自己建立一种更优秀的人类类型。</p>
<p>“这些念头，就是我在可怜的史密森手电光里，只靠指尖吊着的头两秒钟里突然闪过的一切。将来如果你真去写那本威胁已久的传记，你根本不可能让读者相信，一个九岁的孩子在那种处境下会有这样的思想。当然，你也不可能真正写出我那种新态度的实际性质，因为其中包含一种超出你理解范围的经验形式。</p>
<p>“接下来大约两秒，我拼命思考有没有什么办法能够不杀这只忠诚的生物。我的手指快撑不住了。我用最后一点力量够到落水管，开始往下爬。爬到一半，我停住，问：‘史密森太太怎么样？’他说：‘不好。快点，我还想早点回家。’这反而让事情更糟。我怎么下得了手？可就是必须下手，根本没有别的办法。我想过杀掉自己，就这样从整个烂摊子里退出。但我不能这么做。那会彻底背叛我必须为之活下去的东西。我也想过干脆接受史密森、接受法律；可不行，我知道，那同样是背叛。杀人就是非做不可。是我自己的幼稚把我弄进了这场麻烦，可到了现在——杀人就是非做不可。即便如此，我仍然痛恨这件事。当时我还没发展到能够喜欢上一切‘必须做的事’。许多年前，我不得不杀一只老鼠时，那种令我意外的强烈反感又重新出现，而且这次远远更痛苦。你还记得那只老鼠吧，我把它驯熟了，可女佣们受不了它在房子里到处跑。</p>
<p>“总之，史密森必须死。他就站在落水管脚下。我假装脚下一滑，朝他摔下去，同时借墙面一蹬，把他整个人撞失平衡。我们俩一起重重摔倒。我左手一把夺过手电，右手抽出自己的小童军刀。人体心脏的位置，我当然不会不知道。我把刀狠狠扎进去，同时把全身重量压在上面。史密森在最后一次疯狂抽搐中把我甩开，随后便一动不动。</p>
<p>“这场扭打发出了相当大的动静，我听见屋里有一张床吱呀响了一声。我盯着史密森睁着的眼睛和张开的嘴看了一会儿。然后我把刀拔出来，鲜血一下喷了出来。”</p>
<p>约翰对这件怪事的叙述，使我第一次意识到，当年的我对他真正的性格了解得何等之少。</p>
<p>“你回家的路上一定难受得厉害吧。”我说。</p>
<p>“事实上，”他回答，“并没有。我一旦作出决定，那种难受就结束了。而且我没有直接回家。我去了史密森家，本来打算把他妻子也杀掉。我知道她得了癌症，之后还要承受大量痛苦；丈夫一死，她又一定会悲痛欲绝。所以我决定再冒一次险，让她早点脱离苦海。可我用自己那套秘密办法到了那里以后，却发现屋里灯火通明，所有人都醒着。显然她那晚病得很厉害。因此我只好把她留下，可怜的女人。即使这样，其实也没有真正把我弄得很难过。你或许会说，是童年的麻木救了我。也许有一部分确实如此；不过我对‘如果帕克斯失去丈夫会怎样痛苦’这件事，其实有相当鲜明的想象。真正救我的，是一种宿命感。必须发生的，就让它发生。我对自己过去的愚蠢没有悔恨。犯下那些蠢事的那个‘我’，根本没有能力认识到自己有多蠢。突然苏醒的这个新的‘我’，已经看得极其清楚，也急于尽可能补救；但悔恨或羞耻，它一点都没有。”</p>
<p>面对这番坦白，我能作出的回应只有一句：“怪约翰！”</p>
<p>接着我问他，之后是否一直被“会不会被抓”的恐惧折磨。“没有，”他说，“我已经把能做的都做了。要是他们抓到我，那就是抓到了。但那件事我处理得和任何人能处理的一样彻底。我戴了橡胶手套，还故意留下几枚假指纹——用的是我自己做的一个很巧妙的小装置。我唯一真正担心的是那个事务长。我把赃物拆成小批，在几个月里一点点卖给他。”</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a335"></span>CHAPTER V. THOUGHT AND ACTION</h3>
<p><span class="t28">D</span><span class="t31">URING</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">THE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">SIX</span><span class="t30"> </span>months which followed this incident, John became increasingly independent of his elders. The parents knew that he was well able to look after himself, so they left him almost entirely to his own devices. They seldom questioned him about his doings, for anything like prying was repugnant to them both; and there seemed to be no mystery about John’s movements. He was continuing his study of man and man’s world. Sometimes he would volunteer an account of some incident in his day’s adventure; sometimes he would draw upon his store of data to illustrate a point in discussion.</p>
<p>Though his tastes remained in some respects puerile, it was clear from his conversation that in other respects he was very rapidly developing. He would still spend days at a stretch in making mechanical toys, such as electric boats. His electric railway system spread its ramifications all over the garden in a maze of lines, tunnels, viaducts, glass-roofed stations. He won many a competition in flying home-made model aeroplanes. In all these activities he seemed at heart a typical schoolboy, though abnormally skilful and original. But the actual time spent in this way was really not great. The only boyish occupation which seemed to fill a large proportion of his time was sailing. He had made himself a minute but seaworthy canoe, fitted both with sail and an old motor-bicycle engine. In this he spent many hours exploring the estuary and the sea-coast, and studying the sea-birds, for which he had a surprising passion. This interest, which at times seemed almost obsessive, he explained apologetically by saying, “They do their simple jobs with so much more style than man shows in his complicated job. Watch a gannet in flight, or a curlew probing the mud for food. Man, I suppose, is about as clever along his own line as the earliest birds were at flight. He’s a sort of archaeopteryx of the spirit.”</p>
<p>Even the most childish activities which sometimes gripped John were apt to be illuminated in this manner by the more mature side of his nature. His delight in Comic Cuts, for instance, was half spontaneous, half a relish of his own silliness in liking the stuff.</p>
<p>At no time of his life did John outgrow his childhood interests. Even in his last phase he was always capable of sheer schoolboy mischief and make-believe. But already this side of his nature was being subordinated to the mature side. We knew, for instance, that he was already forming opinions about the proper aims of the individual, about social policy, about international affairs. We knew also that he was reading a great deal of physics, biology, psychology, astronomy; and that philosophical problems were now seriously occupying him. His reaction to philosophy was curiously unlike that of the normal philosophically minded adult human being. When one of the great classical philosophical puzzles attracted his attention for the first time, he plunged into the literature of the subject, read solidly for a week, and then gave up philosophy entirely till the next puzzle occurred to him.</p>
<p>After several of these raids upon the territory of philosophy he undertook a serious campaign. For nearly three months philosophy appeared to be his main intellectual interest. It was summer-time, and he liked to study out of doors. Every morning he would set off on his push-bike with a box of books and food strapped on the carrier. Leaving his bicycle at the top of the clay cliffs which formed the coastline of the estuary, he would climb down to the shore, and settle himself for the day. Having undressed and put on his scanty “bathers,” he would lie in the full sunshine reading, or thinking. Sometimes he broke off to bathe or wander about the mud flats watching the birds. Shelter from rain was provided by two rusty pieces of corrugated iron sheeting laid across two low walls, which he built of stones from a ruined lime-kiln near at hand. Sometimes, when the tide was up, he went by the sea route in his canoe. On calm days he might be seen a mile or two from the coast, drifting and reading.</p>
<p>I once asked John how his philosophical researches were progressing. His answer is worth recording. “Philosophy,” he said, “is really very helpful to the growing mind, but it’s terribly disappointing too. At first I thought I’d found the mature human intelligence at work at last. Reading Plato, and Spinoza, and Kant, and some of the modern realists too, I almost felt I had come across people of my own kind. I walked in step with them. I played their game with a sense that it called out powers that I had never exercised before. Sometimes I couldn’t follow them. I seemed to miss some vital move. The exhilaration of puzzling over these critical points, and feeling one had met a real master mind at last! But as I went on from philosopher to philosopher and browsed around all over the place, I began to realize the shocking truth that these critical points were not what I thought they were, but just outrageous howlers. It had seemed incredible that these obviously well-developed minds could make simple mistakes; and so I had respectfully dismissed the possibility, and looked for some profound truth. But oh my God, I was wrong! Howler after howler! Sometimes a philosopher’s opponents spot his howlers, and are frightfully set up with their own cleverness. But most of them never get spotted at all, so far as I can discover. Philosophy is an amazing tissue of really fine thinking and incredible, puerile mistakes. It’s like one of those rubber ‘bones’ they give dogs to chew, damned good for the mind’s teeth, but as food — no bloody good at all.”</p>
<p>I ventured to suggest that perhaps he was not really in a position to judge the philosophers. “After all,” I said, “you’re ridiculously young to tackle philosophy. There are spheres of experience that you have not touched yet.”</p>
<p>“Of course there are,” he said. “But — well, for instance, I have little sexual experience, yet. But even now I can see that a man is blathering if he says that sex (properly defined) is the real motive behind all agricultural activities. Take another case. I have no religious experience, yet. Maybe I shall have it, some day. Maybe there’s really no such thing. But I can see quite well that religious experience (properly defined) is no evidence that the sun goes round the earth, and no evidence that the universe has a purpose, such as the fulfilment of personality. The howlers of philosophers are mostly less obvious than these, but of the same kind.”</p>
<p>At the time of which I am speaking, when John was nearly nine, I had no idea that he was leading a double life, and that the hidden part of it was melodramatic. On one single occasion my suspicion was roused for a few moments, but the possibility that flashed upon me was too fantastic and horrible to be seriously entertained.</p>
<p>One morning I happened to go round to the Wainwrights to borrow one of Thomas’s medical books. It must have been about 11:30. John, who had recently developed the habit of reading late at night and rising late in the morning, was being turned out of bed by his indignant mother. “Come and get your breakfast before you dress,” she said. “I’ll keep it no longer.”</p>
<p>Pax offered me “morning tea,” so we both sat down at the breakfast-table. Presently a blinking and scowling John appeared, wearing a dressing-gown over his pyjamas. Pax and I talked about one thing and another. In the course of conversation she said, “Matilda has come with a really lurid story to-day.” (Matilda was the washerwoman.) “She’s as pleased as Punch about it. She says a policeman was found murdered in Mr. Magnate’s garden this morning, stabbed, she says.” John said nothing, and went on with his breakfast. We continued talking for a while, and then the thing happened that startled me. John reached across the table for the butter, exposing part of his arm beyond the end of the dressing-gown sleeve. On the inner side of the wrist was a rather nasty-looking scrape with a certain amount of dirt still in it. I felt pretty sure that there had been no scrape there when I saw him on the previous evening. Nothing very remarkable in that, but what disturbed me was this: John himself saw that scrape, and then glanced quickly at me. For a fraction of a second his eyes held mine; then he took up the butter-dish. In that moment I seemed to see John, in the middle of the night, scraping his arm as he climbed up the drain-pipe to his bedroom. And it seemed to me that he was returning from Mr. Magnate’s. I pulled myself together at once, reminding myself that what I had seen was a very ordinary abrasion, that John was far too deeply engrossed in his intellectual adventures to indulge in nocturnal pranks, and anyhow far too sensible to risk a murder charge. But that sudden look?</p>
<p>The murder gave the suburb matter for gossip for many weeks. There had recently been a number of extremely clever burglaries in the neighbourhood, and the police were making vigorous efforts to discover the culprit. The murdered man had been found lying on his back in a flower-bed with a neat knife-wound in his chest. He must have died “instantaneously,” for his heart was pierced. A diamond necklace and other valuable pieces of jewellery had disappeared from the house. Slight marks on a window-sill and a drain-pipe suggested that the burglar had climbed in and out by an upper storey. If so, he must have ascended the drain-pipe and then accomplished an almost impossible hand-traverse, or rather finger-tip-traverse, up and along one of the ornamental timbers of the pseudo-Elizabethan house.</p>
<p>Sundry arrests were made, but the perpetrator of the crime was never detected. The epidemic of burglaries, however, ceased, and in time the whole matter was forgotten.</p>
<p>At this point it seems well to draw upon information given me by John himself at a much later stage, in fact during the last year of his life, when the colony had been successfully founded, and had not yet been discovered by the “civilized” world. I was already contemplating writing his biography, and had formed a habit of jotting down notes of any striking incident or conversation as soon as possible after the event. I can, therefore, give the account of the murder approximately in John’s own words.</p>
<p>“I was in a bad mess, mentally, in those days,” said John. “I knew I was different from all other human beings whom I had ever met, but I didn’t realize how different. I didn’t know what I was going to do with my life, but I knew I should soon find something pretty big and desperate to do, and that I must make myself ready for it. Also, remember, I was a child; and I had a child’s taste for the melodramatic, combined with an adult’s cunning and resolution.</p>
<p>“I can’t possibly make you really understand the horrible muddle I was in, because after all your mind doesn’t work along the same lines as mine. But think of it this way, if you like. I found myself in a thoroughly bewildering world. The people in it had built up a huge system of thought and knowledge, and I could see quite well that it was shot through and through with error. From my point of view, although so far as it went it was sound enough for practical purposes, as a description of the world it was simply crazy. But what the right description was I could not discover. I was too young. I had insufficient data. Huge fields of experience were still beyond me. So there I was, like some one in the dark in a strange room, just feeling about among unknown objects. And all the while I had a frantic itch to be getting on with my work, if only I could find out what it was.</p>
<p>“Add to all this that as I grew older I grew more and more lonely, because fewer and fewer people were able to meet me half-way. There was Pax. She really could help, bless her, because she really did see things from my angle — sometimes. And even when she didn’t she had the sense to guess I was seeing something actual, and not merely fantasies. But at bottom she definitely belonged with the rest of you, not with me. Then there was you, much blinder than Pax, but more sympathetic with the active side of me.”</p>
<p>Here I interposed half seriously, half mischievously, “At least a trusty hound.” John laughed, and I added, “And sometimes rising to an understanding beyond my canine capacity, through sheer devotion.” He looked at me and smiled, but did not, as I had hoped he would, assent.</p>
<p>“Well,” he continued, “I was most damnably lonely. I was living in a world of phantoms, or animated masks. No one seemed really alive. I had a queer notion that if I pricked any of you, there would be no bleeding, but only a gush of wind. And I couldn’t make out why you were like that, what it was that I missed in you. The trouble really was that I didn’t clearly know what it was in myself that made me different from you.</p>
<p>“Two clear points emerged from my perplexity. First and simplest, I must make myself independent, I must acquire power. In the crazy world in which I found myself, this meant getting hold of much money. Second I must make haste to sample all sorts of experience, and I must accurately experience my own reactions to all sorts of experience.</p>
<p>“It seemed to me, in my childishness, that I should at any rate begin to fulfil both these needs by bringing off a few burglaries. I should get money, and I should get experience, and I should watch my reactions very carefully. Conscience did not prick me at all. I felt that Mr. Magnate and his like were fair game.</p>
<p>“I first set about studying the technique, partly by reading, partly by discussing the subject with my friend the policeman whom I was afterwards forced to kill. I also undertook a number of experimental and innocuous burglaries on our neighbours. House after house I entered by night, and after locating but not removing the small treasures which they contained, I retired home to bed, well satisfied with my progress.</p>
<p>“At last I felt ready for serious work. In my first house I took only some old-fashioned jewellery, which, I surmised, would not be missed for some time. Then I began taking modern jewellery, cash, silver plate. I found extraordinarily little difficulty in acquiring the stuff. Getting rid of it was much more ticklish work. I managed to make an arrangement with the purser of a foreign-going vessel. He turned up at his home in our suburb every few weeks and bought my swag. I have no doubt that when he parted with it, in foreign ports, he got ten times what he gave me for it. Looking back, I realize how lucky I was that the export side of my venture never brought me to disaster. My purser might so easily have been spotted by the police. Of course, I was still far too ignorant of society to realize the danger. Bright as I was, I had not the data.</p>
<p>“Well, things went swimmingly for some months. I entered dozens of houses and collected several hundred pounds from my purser. But naturally the suburb had got thoroughly excited by this epidemic of housebreaking. Indeed, I had been forced to extend my operations to other districts so as to dissipate the attention of the police. It was clear that if I went on indefinitely I should be caught. But I had been badly bitten by the game. It gave me a sense of independence and power, especially independence, independence of your crazy world.</p>
<p>“I promised myself three more ventures. The first, and the only one to be accomplished, was the Magnate burglary. I went over the ground pretty carefully, and I ascertained the movements of the police pretty thoroughly too. On the actual night all went according to plan until, with my pockets bulging with Mrs. Magnate’s pearls and diamonds (in her full regalia she must have looked like Queen Elizabeth), I started back along that finger-traverse. Suddenly a torch flashed on me from below, and a quiet cheery voice said, ‘Got you this time, my lad.’ I said nothing, for I recognized the voice, and did not wish mine to be recognized in turn. The constable was my own particular pal, Smithson, who had unwittingly taught me so much.</p>
<p>“I hung motionless by my finger-tips, thinking hard, and keeping my face to the wall. But it was useless to conceal my identity, for he said, ‘Buck up, John, boy, come along down or you’ll drop and break your leg. You’re a sport, but you’re beat this time.’</p>
<p>“I must have hung motionless for three seconds at most, but in that time I saw myself and my world as never before. An idea toward which I had been long but doubtfully groping suddenly displayed itself to me with complete clarity and certainty. I had already, some time before, come to think of myself as definitely of a different biological species from Homo sapiens, the species of that amiable bloodhound behind the torch. But at last I realized for the first time that this difference carried with it what I should now describe as a far-reaching spiritual difference, that my purpose in life, and my attitude to life, were to be different from anything which the normal species could conceive, that I stood, as it were, on the threshold of a world far beyond the reach of those sixteen hundred million crude animals that at present ruled the planet. The discovery made me feel, almost for the first time in my life, fear, dread. I saw, too, that this burglary game was not worth the candle, that I had been behaving very much like a creature of the inferior species, risking my future and much more than my personal success for a cheap kind of self-expression. If that amiable bloodhound got me, I should lose my independence. I should be henceforth known, marked, and in the grip of the law. That simply must not be. All these childish escapades had been a blind, fumbling preparation for a lifework which at last stood out more or less clearly before me. It was my task, unique being that I was, to ‘advance the spirit’ on this planet. That was the phrase which flashed into my mind. And though at that early stage I had only a very dim idea about ‘spirit’ and its ‘advance,’ I saw quite clearly that I must set about the more practical side of my task either by taking charge of the common species and teaching it to bring out the best in itself, or, if that proved impossible, by founding a finer human type of my own.</p>
<p>“Such were the thoughts that flashed on me in the first couple of seconds as I hung by my finger-tips in the blaze of poor Smithson’s torch. If ever you do write that threatened biography, you’ll find it quite impossible to persuade your readers that I, a child of nine, could have had such thoughts in such circumstances. Also, of course, you won’t be able to give anything of the actual character of my new attitude, because it involved a kind of experience beyond your grasp.</p>
<p>“During the next two seconds or so I was desperately considering if there was any way to avoid killing the faithful creature. My fingers were giving out. With their last strength I reached the drain-pipe, and began to descend. Half-way I stopped, ‘How’s Mrs. Smithson?’ I said. ‘Bad,’ he answered. ‘Look sharp, I want to get home.’ That made matters worse. How could I do it? Well, it just had to be done, there was no way out of it. I thought of killing myself, and getting out of the whole mess that way. But I couldn’t do that. It would be sheer betrayal of the thing I must live for. I thought of just accepting Smithson and the law; but no, that, I knew, was betrayal also. The killing just had to be. It was my own childishness that had got me into this scrape, but now — the killing just had to be. All the same, I hated the job. I had not yet reached the stage of liking whatever had to be done. I felt over again, and far more distressingly, the violent repulsion which had surprised me years earlier, when I had to kill a mouse. It was that one I had tamed, you remember, and the maids wouldn’t stand it running about the house.</p>
<p>“Well, Smithson had to die. He was standing at the foot of the pipe. I pretended to slip, and fell on him, overbalancing him by kicking off from the wall. We both went down with a crash. With my left hand I seized the torch, and with my right I whipped out my little scout’s knife. The position of the human heart was not unknown to me. I plunged the knife home, leaning on it with all my weight. Smithson flung me off with one frantic spasm, then lay still.</p>
<p>“The scrimmage had made a considerable noise, and I heard a bed creak in the house. For a moment I looked at Smithson’s open eyes and open mouth. I pulled out the knife, and then there was a spurt of blood.”</p>
<p>John’s account of this strange incident showed me how little I had known of his real character at that time.</p>
<p>“You must have felt pretty bad on the way home,” I said.</p>
<p>“As a matter of fact,” he answered, “I didn’t. The bad feeling ended when I made my decision. And I didn’t go straight home. I went to Smithson’s house, intending to kill his wife. I knew she was down with cancer and in for a lot of pain, and would be broken-hearted over her husband’s death; so I decided to take one more risk and put her out of her misery. But when I got there, by secret ways of my own, I found the house lit up and awake. She was evidently having a bad night. So I had to leave her, poor wretch. Even that didn’t really upset me. You may say I was saved by the insensitivity of childhood. Perhaps to some extent; though I had a pretty vivid notion of what Pax would suffer if she lost her husband. What really saved me was a kind of fatalism. What must be, must be. I felt no remorse for my own past folly. The ‘I’ that had committed that folly was incapable of realizing how foolish it was being. The new ‘I,’ that had suddenly awakened, realized very clearly, and was anxious to make amends so far as possible; but of remorse or shame it felt nothing.”</p>
<p>To this confession I could make only one reply, “Odd John!”</p>
<p>I then asked John if he was preyed on by the dread of being caught. “No,” he said. “I had done all I could. If they caught me, they caught me. But I had done the job as efficiently as it is ever done. I had worn rubber gloves, and left a few false fingerprints, made by an ingenious little instrument of my own. My only serious anxiety was over my purser. I sold him the swag in small instalments over a period of several months.”</p>
