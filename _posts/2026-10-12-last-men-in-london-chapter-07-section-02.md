<h2>ii. 保罗重新拾起生活的线索</h2>
<p>我的任务，是向你们展示处于“战后”阶段的你们这个世界——但不是从海王星人的直接视角，而是通过一个你们自己的同类来观察；这个人已经感染了某种海王星式的心境。不过在此之前，我必须先回顾一下：保罗怎样处理自己私人生活中的“战后问题”，并最终成为一件适合我用途的、近乎完善的工具。</p>
<p>最后一次穿着军服横渡英吉利海峡时，保罗怀着复杂心情回望过去，也望向未来。</p>
<p>身后是死者，是成千上万座木十字架；在保罗的想象中，还有伤员——灰色、蓝色、卡其色的身影，浑身是血，骨头被炸得粉碎。</p>
<p>身后，而且永远无法抹去的，是全部恐怖、全部浪费、全部愚蠢；然而他又不得不向自己承认：身后还存在一种精美得令人无法忍受的美，一种非人的、无法辩护的、不可容忍的美。</p>
<p>前方则是生活，是一种新的希望。</p>
<p>可是，当他望向未来时，无论对自己还是对世界，他都怀有不祥的疑虑。</p>
<p>他为自己担忧，因为自己并不分享同胞普遍的情绪——那种“战争终于光荣地取得了胜利”的感激与欣慰。</p>
<p>他也为世界担忧，因为他越来越强烈地感觉到：人类所处的环境正在迅速超出人类自身的控制。这个时代要求人拥有比他实际具备的更多智慧，也要求更多正直。</p>
<p>因此，在返乡旅途中，保罗无法像同伴那样从心底唤起纯粹的感恩之情。</p>
<p>然而，当他一面回望、一面前瞻，任风在耳边吟唱，任咸涩飞沫打在唇上时，他仍充满兴致地品尝着存在那苦涩而挑衅的滋味。</p>
<p>他把如今的自己同战争期间第一次横渡海峡时的自己相比，想到那时那个半睡半醒的可怜东西，不禁笑了。</p>
<p>“至少，”他说，“我醒过来了。”</p>
<p>接着，他又想到十字架下那成千上万的人。他们永远不会再醒来。</p>
<p>他想起那个自己十分熟悉、又亲眼看着死去的年轻人——那个人原本应该醒得多么灿烂。</p>
<p>随后，他又一次品尝到那种神秘的、无法辩护的美；它竟能从这一切恐怖之中浮现出来。</p>
<p>我选择了这个时刻。保罗此时对自我和世界都具有相对较深的意识，于是我把那个问题以一种新的清晰度强加给他——从此以后，我准备让它一直纠缠他。</p>
<p>航程本身使这项工作变得容易。</p>
<p>海面很不平静。保罗非常喜欢风浪，也喜欢船身上下俯冲的感觉；可是这种快乐又被一点晕船的恶心搅乱。</p>
<p>他努力抵抗恶心，也抵抗恶心带来的精神低落。他想继续享受风、飞沫、船身的运动以及布满白色纹路的大海，可他做不到。</p>
<p>不久以后，他非常痛快地吐了一场。</p>
<p>然后，他又能够享受这一切，甚至重新思考世界。</p>
<p>他的思想同时染上了两种颜色：恶心的阴影，以及咸涩空气带来的兴奋。</p>
<p>他坚决地擤了擤鼻子，若有所思地擦了擦嘴，继续靠在栏杆上，望着那片虽遭亵渎、却仍毫无污损的海浪。</p>
<p>他给自己的问题定下了形式：</p>
<p>“事情怎么能错得如此彻底、如此毫无意义、如此肮脏；可同时又能对得如此彻底、如此压倒性地充满意义、如此精美？”</p>
<p>事情究竟错到了什么程度，又究竟精美到了什么程度，无论保罗还是任何人类都还远远没有完全发现。</p>
<p>但问题已经被明确提出了。</p>
<p>我暗自决定，要以最细致的注意力观察这个经过特殊处理的你们同类，看看他会怎样努力同宇宙达成某种和解。</p>
<p>保罗回到了自己出生的郊区，被家庭重新接纳进温暖怀抱。</p>
<p>回家真好。</p>
<p>有一阵子，他满足于享受那仿佛永远不会结束的休假。他穿着法兰绒长裤和一件旧花呢外套，懒洋洋地过日子。</p>
<p>在家里，没有人要求他解释自己，因此他感到安宁；可到了别处，即使穿得和别人一模一样，他仍觉得自己是个异乡人。</p>
<p>他把这种孤立感归因于自己的和平主义，却不知道自战争开始以来，社会舆论实际上已经移动了多远。</p>
<p>事实上，他的孤独更大程度上来自我在他内部造成的澄清作用：仿佛有一些由另一个物种、另一个世界孕育出的种子般观念，被植入了他的心灵。</p>
<p>他发现自己的思维方式与别人不同，却又说不清差异究竟在哪里。</p>
<p>这令他很痛苦。</p>
<p>他憎恨自己与众不同。他渴望变成羊群里无法辨认的一员——尤其是自己所属社会阶层中一个普通得不能再普通的人。</p>
<p>于是，他开始非常用心地穿得合乎规矩，也刻意使用当下流行的俚语。</p>
<p>他试着打桥牌，可总是无法保持清醒。</p>
<p>他试着打高尔夫，可每到关键时刻，他的肌肉似乎偏偏要跟他作对。</p>
<p>真正的问题在于：他其实根本不想做这些事。</p>
<p>他只想做“大家都该做的事”。</p>
<p>还有另一件麻烦。</p>
<p>回家不到两个星期，保罗便痛苦地意识到：自己与父母之间的关系已经不再像从前。</p>
<p>从前他常说：“我挑父母的运气不错。当然，他们其实并不十分了解我，不过知道得已经够用了。他们两个都善良得没完没了，也都体谅人、肯帮忙，而且聪明到知道什么时候该让我一个人待着。”</p>
<p>如今，他却逐渐看见：过去父母其实比他以为的更理解他；只是后来他变成的这个东西，对他们而言已经无法理解。</p>
<p>早在战争刚开始时，他们便因为他的和平主义而与他失去联系。他们庆幸地接受这种立场，因为它能使儿子免于进入战壕；可他们从来没有真正严肃地看待它。</p>
<p>现在，双方之间的距离比以前更大。</p>
<p>保罗觉得，父母仿佛生活在一种心理上的“平面国”里，从未真正接触过第三维。</p>
<p>他们如此通情达理，如此善良，又如此努力想要宽容。</p>
<p>在许多事情上，他都能同他们意见一致——也就是在“平面国”与“立体国”恰好重合的那些方面。</p>
<p>但时不时地，交流就会突然断裂。</p>
<p>父母会耐心地同他争论一阵，却毫无结果；然后他们便沉默下来，用一种奇怪表情看着他。</p>
<p>在那表情里，保罗似乎同时看见爱、怜悯与恐惧。</p>
<p>这时他便耸耸肩，想：以前那些时代，代际鸿沟也曾经有这么宽吗？</p>
<p>至于他的父母，则会悲伤地彼此说：战争经历——还是他的和平主义？——已经把他的头脑扭曲到了某种程度，以至于他“不是完全……不是绝对地……正常”。</p>
<p>几个星期以后，保罗回到原先那所大型郊区中学任职。</p>
<p>他很惊讶学校竟然还肯要他，毕竟他既是和平主义者，业务能力也不怎么样。</p>
<p>后来他才发现，自己的父亲认识学校管理委员会主席。</p>
<p>他是不是应该辞职？大概应该。</p>
<p>但他没有。</p>
<p>到这时，他已经稳稳地属于教职员中那类“并不出色，但也不至于无法忍受”的成员。工作对他来说依然绝不轻松，却已经不像从前那样是一场折磨。</p>
<p>他年纪大了些，也没那么容易慌乱。</p>
<p>而且他必须有钱。</p>
<p>再说，他发现自己其实还挺喜欢男孩子——只要自己能同他们保持良好关系。</p>
<p>一旦关系弄坏，他们就是地狱；可只要相处得对，他们比大多数成年人好忍受得多。</p>
<p>教师这份工作是正当而有用的。说起来，如果真正做好，它甚至可能是所有工作里最重要的一种。</p>
<p>而且，这也是他唯一会做的工作。</p>
<p>他必须有钱——不需要很多，但至少要足够让自己独立。</p>
<p>此时，保罗的人生目标是过一种轻松、无事发生的生活。</p>
<p>他告诉自己，当然愿意尽自己的那份责任；不过他可不准备把自己绷得太紧。</p>
<p>他必须留出时间享受人生。</p>
<p>他打算用两种方式享受，而且两者同时进行。</p>
<p>第一，他想在那场永无休止的性之比武大会中赢得自己的马刺。自己的处男身份使他对自我产生怀疑，也干扰了他对生活的判断。</p>
<p>与此同时，他还答应自己要进行大量所谓“智力观光”或“精神旅行”。</p>
<p>他会舒舒服服地旅行；即使买不起头等票，至少也要坐二等车，把心灵所有大陆上那些已经开发完善的旅游路线都走上一遍。</p>
<p>偶尔，他甚至也许会闯进没有道路的荒野，但绝不能危险。</p>
<p>只要有可能，他再也不会像和平主义时期那样，与“文明”失去联系——也就是与同伴们那套舒适的精神结构失去联系。</p>
<p>这就是这个厌倦了战争的年轻地球人为自己拟定的计划。</p>
<p>其中相当大一部分非常符合我的目的，但绝不是全部。</p>
<p>于是，保罗安顿下来，开始教授历史、地理、英语和“圣经课”。</p>
<p>学校让他教什么，他就教什么；单单出于怜悯，他会尽量把那些东西弄得容易入口一些。</p>
<p>总体而言，学生们喜欢他。</p>
<p>他看待学校功课的态度似乎和学生自己差不多：这是一种烦人的必要苦工，必须有效率地完成，但完全可以靠笑话和故事缓解。</p>
<p>就连纪律——过去一直令保罗头疼——现在也仿佛自动运转起来。</p>
<p>一部分原因是他不再为纪律担忧，因为他已经摆脱那种“必须维护自身威严”的想法；另一部分原因，则是我在他新教学生活最关键的头几天里影响了他的心境与举止，使他给人的印象同时具有友善和一种漫不经心的坚定。</p>
<p>保罗本人既惊讶又感激，因为课堂竟能靠学生们自己的自我约束运转起来，这替他卸掉了一副可怕重担。</p>
<p>有访客在场时，他常当着全班说：“你看，我们这里没有独裁者，只有主席。”</p>
<p>尽管如此，他私下里仍把这项成就的一部分功劳记到了自己名下。</p>
<p>我通过帮助保罗建立一套尚可忍受的日常生活，使他的心灵能够保持空闲而敏感，把注意力留给其他事情。</p>
<p>学生们自己也知道，他还有另外一面。</p>
<p>有时候，上着上着课，课程会彻底跑偏；他会突然开始以令人吃惊的方式谈论自己感兴趣的东西——完全超出教学大纲，有时甚至非常不合正统。</p>
<p>他会长篇大论地讲恒星的年龄和数量，讲脑内功能定位，讲科学与数学的抽象性，讲不同类型的精神错乱，讲苏维埃俄国的真相，讲戏剧和电影，讲人体的细胞结构，讲所谓原子的电子结构，讲死亡和衰老，讲世界主义，讲日光浴，讲自由泳，讲塞缪尔·巴特勒，讲性交，以及婴儿是怎样出生的。</p>
<p>面对这种语言洪水，男孩子们会按照各自不同的天性作出反应。</p>
<p>愚笨的学生会感激地进入昏睡；勤奋的学生则趁机复习家庭作业。</p>
<p>精明的会从整场演说里嚼出一切似乎具有实际价值的东西，然后把剩下的吐掉。</p>
<p>具有智力好奇心的学生则保持距离，冷静聆听。</p>
<p>大多数人会在觉得好玩、热烈拥护和感到无聊之间来回摆动。</p>
<p>还有少数敏感的学生，简直把保罗当成自己的先知。</p>
<p>每次这样爆发之后，保罗都会变得格外沉默、谨慎。</p>
<p>有时，他甚至会告诉学生：“你们最好把这些全忘了，因为我刚才有点疯。”</p>
<p>显然，他很怕关于自己那些离经叛道言论的传闻传出教室门外。</p>
<p>这种秘密感却正合男孩子们的胃口。</p>
<p>不管大家对那些内容本身意见如何，至少所有人都能享受“共谋”的乐趣。</p>
<p>保罗自己那个班逐渐把本班想象成一种奇妙混合物：既像一群门徒——甚至先知，又像秘密革命社团，还像一伙海盗。</p>
</section>

<h2>ii. PAUL GATHERS UP THE THREADS</h2>
<p>IT IS MY task to show your world in its ‘post-war’ phase as it seemed to one of your own kind who had been infected with something of the Neptunian mood. First, however, I must recall how Paul faced his own private ‘post-war’ problems, and emerged at last as a perfected instrument for my purpose.</p>
<p>Crossing the Channel for the last time in uniform, Paul looked behind and forward with mixed feelings. Behind lay the dead, with their thousands upon thousands of wooden crosses; and also (for Paul’s imagination) the wounded, grey, blue and khaki, bloody, with splintered bones. Behind, but indelible, lay all that horror, that waste, that idiocy; yet also behind lay, as he was forced to admit to himself, an exquisite, an inhuman, an indefensible, an intolerable beauty. Forward lay life, and a new hope. Yet looking toward the future he felt misgivings both for himself and for the world. For himself he feared, because he had no part in the common emotion of his fellow-countrymen, in the thankfulness that the war had been nobly won. For the world he feared, because of a growing sense that man’s circumstances were rapidly passing beyond man’s control. They demanded more intelligence and more integrity than he possessed.</p>
<p>Thus Paul on his homeward journey could not conjure in himself the pure thankfulness of his fellows. Yet, looking behind and forward, with the wind chanting in his ears and the salt spray on his lips, he savoured zestfully the bitter and taunting taste of existence. Comparing his present self with the self of his first war-time crossing, he smiled at the poor half-conscious thing that he had been, and said, at least, I have come awake.’ Then he thought again of those thousands under the crosses, who would not wake ever. He remembered the boy whom he had known so well, and had watched dying; who should have wakened so splendidly. And then once more he savoured that mysterious, indefensible beauty which he had seen emerge in all this horror.</p>
<p>I chose this moment of comparatively deep self-consciousness and world-consciousness to force upon Paul with a new clarity the problem with which I intended henceforth to haunt him. This task was made easier by the circumstances of the voyage. The sea was boisterous. Paul greatly enjoyed a boisterous sea and the plunging of a ship, but his pleasure was complicated by a feeling of sea-sickness. He fought against his nausea and against its depressing influence. He wanted to go on enjoying the wind and the spray and the motion and the white-veined sea, but he could not. Presently, however, he very heartily vomited; and then once more he could enjoy things, and even think about the world. His thinking was coloured both by his nausea and by his zest in the salt air. Resolutely blowing his nose, meditatively wiping his mouth, he continued to lean on the rail and watch the desecrated but all-unsullied waves. He formulated his problem to himself, ‘How can things be so wrong, so meaningless, so filthy; and yet also so right, so overwhelmingly significant, so exquisite?’ How wrong things were, and how exquisite, neither Paul nor any man had yet fully discovered. But the problem was formulated. I promised myself that I should watch with minute attention the efforts of this specially treated member of your kind to come to terms with the universe.</p>
<p>Paul returned to his native suburb, and was welcomed into the bosom of the home. It was good to be at home. For a while he was content to enjoy the prospect of an endless leave, lazing in flannel trousers and an old tweed coat. At home, where he was not expected to explain himself, he was at peace; but elsewhere, even when he was dressed as others, he felt an alien. He attributed his sense of isolation to his pacifism, not knowing how far opinion had moved since the beginning of the war. This loneliness of his was in fact due much more to my clarifying influence in him, to his having been as it were impregnated with seminal ideas gendered in another species and another world. He found his mind working differently from other minds, yet he could not detect where the difference lay. This distressed him. He hated to be different. He longed to become an indistinguishable member of the herd, of his own social class. He began to take great pains to dress correctly, and to use the slang of the moment. He tried to play bridge, but could never keep awake. He tried to play golf, but his muscles seemed to play tricks on him at the critical moment. The truth was that he did not really want to do these things at all. He wanted to do ‘the done thing’.</p>
<p>There was another trouble. He had not been at home more than a fortnight before he realized with distress that his relations with his parents were not what they had been. In the old days he used to say, ‘I have been lucky in my choice of parents. Of course they don’t really know me very well, but they understand as much as is necessary. They are both endlessly kind, sympathetic; helpful, and they have the sense to leave me alone.’ Now, he began to see that in the old days they really had understood him far more than he supposed, but that the thing that he had since become was incomprehensible to them. Even at the beginning of the war they had lost touch with him over his pacifism; accepting it thankfully as a means of saving him from the trenches, but never taking it seriously. Now, they were more out of touch than ever. It seemed to him that they were living in a kind of mental Flat Land, without acquaintance with the third dimension. They were so sensible, so kind, so anxious to be tolerant. He could see eye to eye with them in so many things, in all those respects in which Flat Land was in accord with Solid Land. But every now and then intercourse would break down. They would argue with him patiently for a while in vain; then fall silent, regarding him with an expression in which he seemed to see, blended together, love, pity and horror. He would then shrug his shoulders, and wonder whether in earlier times the gulf between the generations had ever been so wide. They, for their part, sadly told one another that his war experience (or was it his pacifism?) had so twisted his mind that he was ‘not quite, not absolutely, sane’.</p>
<p>After a few weeks Paul went back to his old post in the big suburban secondary school. He was surprised that they took him, in spite of his pacifism and his incompetence. Later he discovered that his father knew the Chairman of the Council. Ought he to resign? Probably, but he would not. He was by now well established as one of the less brilliant but not impossible members of the staff; and though the work never came easily to him, it was no longer the torture that it had formerly been. He was older, and less easily flustered. And he must have money. After all, he found, he rather liked boys, so long as he could keep on the right side of them. If you got wrong with them, they were hell; but if you kept right, they were much more tolerable than most adults. The work was respectable useful work. Indeed, it was in a way the most important of all work, if it was done properly. Moreover, it was the only work he could do. And he must have money; not much, but enough to make him independent.</p>
<p>His aim at this time was to live an easy and uneventful life. He told himself that he did want to pull his weight, but he was not prepared to overstrain himself. He must have time to enjoy himself. He proposed to enjoy himself in two ways, and both together. He wanted to win his spurs in the endless tournament of sex; for his virginity filled him with self-doubt, and disturbed his judgements about life. At the same time he promised himself a good deal of what he called ‘intellectual sight-seeing’ or ‘spiritual touring’. Comfortably, if not with a first-class ticket, at least with a second-class one, he would explore all the well-exploited travel-routes of all the continents of the mind. Sometimes he might even venture into the untracked wild, but not dangerously. Never again, if he could help it, would he lose touch with civilization, with the comfortable mental structure of his fellows, as he had done through pacifism. Thus did this war-weary young Terrestrial propose. Much of his plan suited my purpose admirably, but by no means the whole of it.</p>
<p>So Paul settled down to teach history, geography, English and ‘scripture’. He taught whatever he was told to teach, and for mere pity’s sake, he made the stuff as palatable as he could. On the whole the boys liked him. He seemed to regard the business much as they did themselves, as a tiresome necessary grind, which must be done efficiently but might be alleviated by jokes and stories. Even discipline, formerly such a trouble to Paul, now seemed to work automatically. This was partly because he did not worry about it, having outgrown the notion that he must preserve his ‘dignity’, and partly because in the critical first days of his new school career I influenced his mood and his bearing so that he gave an impression of friendliness combined with careless firmness. Paul himself was surprised and thankful that the self-discipline of his classes relieved him of a dreadful burden. He used to say to visitors, in front of the class, ‘You see, we have not a dictator, but a chairman.’ All the same, he secretly took credit for his achievement.</p>
<p>By thus helping Paul to establish himself in a tolerable routine, I enabled him to keep his mind free and sensitive for other matters. The boys themselves knew that there was another side to him. Occasionally in class the lesson would go astray completely, and he would talk astoundingly about things interesting to himself, things outside the curriculum, and sometimes of very doubtful orthodoxy. He would dwell upon the age and number of the stars, the localization of functions in the brain, the abstractness of science and mathematics, the kinds of insanity, the truth about Soviet Russia, the theatre and the cinema, the cellular structure of the body, the supposed electronic structure of the atom, death and old age, cosmopolitanism, sun-bathing, the crawl stroke, Samuel Butler, coitus and the birth of babies. To these floods of language the boys reacted according to their diverse natures. The stupid slipped thankfully into torpor, the industrious revised their home-work. The shrewd masticated whatever in the harangue seemed to have a practical value, and spat out the rest. The intellectually curious listened aloofly. The main mass fluctuated between amusement, enthusiastic support, and boredom. A few sensitive ones took Paul as their prophet. After these outbursts Paul became unusually reticent and cautious. Sometimes he even told the boys they’ had better forget it all’ because he was’ a bit mad then’. He was obviously anxious that rumour of his wildness should not go beyond the class-room door. The sense of secrecy appealed to the boys. Whatever their diverse opinions of the stuff, all could enjoy a conspiracy. Paul’s own class began to regard itself as something between a band of disciples (or even prophets), a secret revolutionary society, and a pirate crew.</p>
