<h2>第十一章</h2>
<p>“所以，就是这个了，嗯，奥林？”莫兹利说。</p>
<p>“是的，先生，就是这里。”站在他左边的奥林骄傲地笑着说。“您觉得怎么样，先生？”</p>
<p>莫兹利慢慢转了一圈，打量着草地、群山、太阳、河流和森林。他脸上毫无表情。“你觉得怎么样，布鲁克赛德？”</p>
<p>布鲁克赛德声音发颤地说：“这个嘛，先生，我觉得奥林和我干得不错。真的很不错——如果考虑到这是我们第一次独立负责项目的话。”</p>
<p>“奥林，你也同意这个判断？”莫兹利问。</p>
<p>“当然，先生。”奥林说。</p>
<p>莫兹利弯下腰，拔起一根草叶，闻了闻，又把它扔掉。他用鞋尖蹭了蹭脚下的泥土，随后直视灼热的太阳足足看了几秒。他不紧不慢地说：“我很吃惊，真的很吃惊。不过是以一种极其不愉快的方式。我让你们两个替我的一个客户造一个世界，你们就给我交出这种东西！你们真的认为自己配叫工程师吗？”</p>
<p>两个助手都没回答。他们全身僵硬，像两个等着挨藤条的男孩。</p>
<p>“工程师！”莫兹利说道，硬是往这个词里塞进了将近五十英尺磅的轻蔑。“‘富有创造力、同时讲求实际的科学家，能在你想要的地点和时间建造你想要的行星。’你们两个有谁认得这句话？”</p>
<p>“标准宣传册上的。”奥林说。</p>
<p>“答对了。”莫兹利说。“那么，你们认为眼前这东西算得上‘富有创造力而又讲求实际的工程’的好例子吗？”</p>
<p>两个人都没说话。过了一会儿，布鲁克赛德脱口而出：“这个——先生，是的，先生，我觉得算！”</p>
<p>“我们非常仔细地研究过工程规格。客户要的是一颗34Bc4型行星，附带若干变体。我们造出来的也正是这个。当然，这里只是其中一个角落。不过即便如此——”</p>
<p>“即便如此，我也已经看得出你们干了些什么，并足够据此作出判断。”莫兹利说。“奥林！你们用了什么型号的加热装置？”</p>
<p>“O5型太阳，先生。”奥林回答。“它正好符合热量要求。”</p>
<p>“我敢说它当然符合。可你们应该还记得，这是一颗预算型世界。成本压不下来，我们就赚不到钱。而最大的一项单项成本，就是加热装置。”</p>
<p>“这点我们知道，先生。”布鲁克赛德说。“我们也一点都不愿意给一个单行星系统配O5型太阳。可是热量和辐射要求——”</p>
<p>“你们难道什么都没从我这里学会吗？”莫兹利叫道。“这种恒星完全多余。你们几个——”他朝工人们招了招手。“把它拆下来。”</p>
<p>工人们拿着一架折叠梯匆匆跑来。一个人扶住梯子，另一个开始把它展开——十次，一百次，一百万次。另外两个工人则沿着梯子一路狂奔，梯子升多快，他们就爬多快。</p>
<p>“小心点！”莫兹利朝上面喊。“还有，记得戴手套！那玩意儿烫着呢！”</p>
<p>梯子最顶端的工人摘下恒星，把它向自身折叠起来，装进一个带软垫的箱子。箱子上写着：“恒星：小心轻放。”</p>
<p>箱盖一合，四周顿时一片漆黑。</p>
<p>“这里就没人有点常识吗？”莫兹利问。“见鬼，来点光。”</p>
<p>于是，就这么一下，有了光。</p>
<p>“好。”莫兹利说。“那颗O5型太阳送回仓库。这种项目，用G13型恒星就行。”</p>
<p>“可是先生，”奥林紧张地说，“它不够热。”</p>
<p>“我知道。”莫兹利说。“这才是需要你们发挥创造力的地方。把恒星挪近一些，它自然就够热了。”</p>
<p>“是的先生，这样确实会够热。”布鲁克赛德说。“可它会发射PR射线，而恒星离得太近，空间不够让那些射线在抵达行星前无害地耗散。那样可能会把将来居住在这颗行星上的整个种族都杀光。”</p>
<p>莫兹利一字一顿、极其清楚地说道：“你是在告诉我，G13型恒星很危险？”</p>
<p>“呃，不，我并不完全是那个意思。”奥林说。“我是说，如果没有采取适当的防护措施，它们可能有危险——就像宇宙里的任何东西一样。”</p>
<p>“这还像话。”莫兹利说。</p>
<p>“而在这个案例中，”布鲁克赛德说，“适当的防护措施是穿一套大约五十磅重的铅制防护服。但这不现实，因为这个种族的普通个体平均只有八磅重。”</p>
<p>“那是他们自己的问题。”莫兹利说。“我们又不是来告诉他们该怎样过日子的。难道他们在我放到行星上的一块石头上踢伤脚趾，我也得负责？何况他们也不是非得穿铅衣不可。他们可以买我的选配附件——一面能挡住PR射线的太阳屏蔽罩。”</p>
<p>两个人都紧张地笑了笑。但奥林还是怯生生地说：“先生，我想这是个相当贫困的种族。恐怕他们买不起太阳屏蔽罩。”</p>
<p>“现在买不起，也许以后买得起。”莫兹利说。“再说，PR辐射又不会立刻致命。就算暴露在里面，他们的平均寿命也还有9.3年；谁活这么久都该够用了。”</p>
<p>“是的，先生。”两个助理工程师回答，听起来一点也不高兴。</p>
<p>“下一个问题，”莫兹利说，“那些山有多高？”</p>
<p>“平均海拔六千英尺。”布鲁克赛德说。</p>
<p>“至少高了三千英尺。”莫兹利说。“你们以为山是树上长出来的吗？给我削矮，把剩下的材料送进仓库。”</p>
<p>布鲁克赛德掏出笔记本，把这项修改记下来。莫兹利继续来回踱步，一边看，一边皱眉。</p>
<p>“那些树设计寿命是多少？”</p>
<p>“八百年，先生。它们是新改良型‘苹果橡树’。能结果、遮阴、产坚果、供应清凉饮料，还能提供三种实用织物；木材非常适合建筑，也能保持水土，而且——”</p>
<p>“你们是想让我破产吗？”莫兹利吼道。“树活两百年完全够了！把它们大部分生命活力抽出来，存进生命力蓄积器！”</p>
<p>“那样它们就没法完成全部设计功能了。”奥林说。</p>
<p>“那就砍掉一些功能！能遮阴、能结坚果就够了，我们又不是非得把每棵树做成该死的百宝箱！还有，那边那些牛是谁放进去的？”</p>
<p>“是我，先生。”布鲁克赛德说。“我觉得这样会让这里看起来——呃，更有点吸引力，先生。”</p>
<p>“蠢货。”莫兹利说。“让一个地方显得有吸引力，是出售以前该干的事，不是卖掉以后！这里卖的是不带家具的空房。把那些牛扔进原生质槽。”</p>
<p>“是，先生。”奥林说。“非常抱歉，先生。还有别的问题吗？”</p>
<p>“还有大约一万处毛病。”莫兹利说。“不过我希望你们能自己找出来。比如说，这是什么？”他指着卡莫迪。“雕像还是别的东西？等新种族到达以后，他会唱首歌还是背首诗？”</p>
<p>卡莫迪说：“先生，我不是这个项目的一部分。您的一个朋友梅利克隆把我送来的，我正在设法回我自己的行星——”</p>
<p>莫兹利显然压根没听见卡莫迪说什么。因为卡莫迪还在努力解释时，他已经说道：“不管他是什么，工程规格里都没有这一项。所以把他和那些牛一起塞回原生质槽。”</p>
<p>“喂！”工人们抓住卡莫迪的胳膊把他抬起来时，他大叫道。“喂，等一下！”他尖叫起来。“我不是这颗行星的一部分！是梅利克隆让我来的！等等，停下，听我说！”</p>
<p>“你们两个真应该为自己感到羞耻。”莫兹利继续说道，对卡莫迪的尖叫充耳不闻。“刚才那到底是什么？你搞的室内装饰点缀吗，奥林？”</p>
<p>“哦，不是。”奥林说。“不是我把他放进去的。”</p>
<p>“那就是你了，布鲁克赛德。”</p>
<p>“我这辈子头一次见他，头儿。”</p>
<p>“嗯。”莫兹利说。“你们两个都是傻瓜，不过从来没撒过谎。喂！”他朝工人们喊。“把他带回来！”</p>
<p>“好了，振作一点。”莫兹利对还在不可控制地发抖的卡莫迪说。“控制住自己，我可没工夫站在这里等你歇斯底里发作！好些了？很好。那你介不介意解释一下，你为什么擅闯我的地产，以及我为什么不该把你转化成原生质？”</p>
</section>
<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="en" lang="en" aria-label="English original" hidden>
<p><b>CHAPTER 11</b></p>
<p>‘So this is it, eh, Orin?’ Maudsley said.</p>
<p>‘Yes sir, this is it,’ Orin, the man on his left said, smiling proudly. ‘What do you think of it, sir?’</p>
<p>Maudsley turned around slowly and surveyed the meadow, the mountains, the sun, the river, the forest. His face betrayed no expression. He said, ‘What do <i>you</i> think of it, Brookside?’</p>
<p>Brookside said, in a tremulous voice, ‘Well sir, I think that Orin and I did a nice job. A <i>really</i> nice job, if you take into account that it was our first independent project.’</p>
<p>‘And do you concur in that judgement, Orin?’ Maudsley asked.</p>
<p>‘Certainly, sir,’ Orin said.</p>
<p>Maudsley bent down and plucked a blade of grass. He sniffed it and threw it away. He scuffed the dirt beneath his feet, then stared for several moments full into the blazing sun. In a measured voice, he said, ‘I am amazed, truly amazed. But in a most unpleasant way. I ask you two to build a world for one of my customers and you come up with <i>this!</i> Do you really consider yourselves engineers?’</p>
<p>The two aides did not reply. They had stiffened, like boys awaiting the birch rod.</p>
<p>‘Engineers!’ Maudsley said, getting almost fifty foot-pounds of contempt into the word. ‘“Creative but practical scientists who can build the planet where and when you want it.” Do either of you recognize those words?’</p>
<p>‘They’re from the standard brochure,’ Orin said.</p>
<p>‘That is correct,’ Maudsley said. ‘Now, do you consider <i>this</i> a good example of “creative, practical engineering”?’</p>
<p>Both men were silent. Then Brookside blurted out, ‘Well sir, yes sir, I do!</p>
<p>‘We examined the job specs very carefully. The request was for a Type 34Bc4 planet with certain variations. And that’s exactly what we built. This is only a corner of it, of course. But still –’</p>
<p>‘But still, I can see what you did and judge accordingly,’ Maudsley said. ‘Orin! What kind of a heating unit did you use?’</p>
<p>‘A type O5 sun, sir,’ Orin replied. ‘It fitted the thermal requirements nicely.’</p>
<p>‘I daresay it did. But this was a budget world, you will remember. If we don’t keep the costs down, we don’t make a profit. And the biggest single cost item is the heating unit.’</p>
<p>‘We are aware of that, sir,’ Brookside said. ‘We didn’t at all like to use an O5-type sun for a single-planet system. But the heat and radiation requirements –’</p>
<p>‘Haven’t you learned anything from me?’ Maudsley cried. ‘This type of star is entirely superfluous. You there – ’ He beckoned to the workmen. ‘Take it down.’</p>
<p>The workmen hurried forward with a folding ladder. One man braced it and another man unfolded it, ten times, a hundred times, a million times. Two other workmen raced up the ladder as fast as it went up.</p>
<p>‘Handle it carefully!’ Maudsley called up to them. ‘And be sure you’re wearing gloves! That thing’s hot!’</p>
<p>The workmen at the very top of the ladder unhooked the star, folded it into itself and put it into a padded box marked ‘star: handle with care.’</p>
<p>When the lid fell, everything went black.</p>
<p>‘Hasn’t anyone any sense around here?’ Maudsley asked. ‘Damn it all, let there be light.’</p>
<p>And just like that, there was light.</p>
<p>‘OK,’ Maudsley said. ‘That O5-type sun goes back into storage. On a job like this we can use a G13-type star.’</p>
<p>‘But sir,’ Orin said nervously, ‘it isn’t hot enough.’</p>
<p>‘I know that,’ Maudsley said. ‘That’s where you have to use your creativity. If you move the star closer in, it’ll be hot enough.’</p>
<p>‘Yes sir, it will,’ Brookside said. ‘But it’ll be emitting PR rays without enough space to allow them to dissipate harmlessly. And that might kill off the entire race that’s going to occupy this planet.’</p>
<p>Maudsley said, very slowly and distinctly, ‘Are you trying to tell me that G13-type stars are dangerous?’</p>
<p>‘Well, no, I didn’t mean it exactly <i>that</i> way,’ Orin said. ‘I meant to say, they <i>can</i> be dangerous, just like anything else in the universe, if proper precautions are not taken.’</p>
<p>‘That’s more like it,’ Maudsley said.</p>
<p>‘The proper precautions,’ Brookside said, ‘involve, in this case, the wearing of protective lead suits weighing some fifty pounds each. But this is impractical, since the average member of this race only weighs eight pounds.’</p>
<p>‘That’s their lookout,’ Maudsley said. ‘It’s not our business to tell them how to live their lives. Am I supposed to be responsible whenever they stub their toe on a rock I put on their planet? Besides, they don’t have to wear lead suits. They can buy one of my optional extras, a solar screen that’ll block out the PR rays.’</p>
<p>Both men smiled nervously. But Orin said timidly, ‘I believe this is a somewhat underprivileged species, sir. I think perhaps they can’t afford the solar screen.’</p>
<p>‘Well, if not right now, maybe later,’ Maudsley said. ‘And anyhow, the PR radiations aren’t instantly fatal. Even with it, they’ll have an average lifespan of 9·3 years, which ought to be enough for anyone.’</p>
<p>‘Yes sir,’ the two assistant engineers said, not happily.</p>
<p>‘Next,’ Maudsley said, ‘what’s the height of those mountains?’</p>
<p>‘They average six thousand feet above sea level,’ Brookside said.</p>
<p>‘At least three thousand feet too high,’ Maudsley said. ‘Do you think mountains grow on trees? Pare them down and put what you have left over into the warehouse.’</p>
<p>Brookside took out a notebook and jotted down the change. Maudsley continued to pace around, looking and frowning.</p>
<p>‘How long are those trees supposed to last?’</p>
<p>‘Eight hundred, years, sir. They’re the new improved model Appleoak. They give fruit, shade, nuts, refreshing beverages, three useful fabrics, they make excellent building material, hold the soil in place, and –’</p>
<p>‘Are you trying to bankrupt me?’ Maudsley roared. ‘Two hundred years is entirely long enough for a tree! Drain off most of their <i>élan vital</i> and store it in the life-force accumulator!’</p>
<p>‘They won’t be able to perform all of their designed functions, then,’ Orin said.</p>
<p>‘Then cut down on their functions! Shade and nuts is plenty, we don’t have to make a damned treasure chest out of those trees! Now then, who put those cows out there?’</p>
<p>‘I did, sir,’ Brookside said. ‘I thought it would make the place look – well, sort of inviting, sir.’</p>
<p>‘You oaf,’ Maudsley said. ‘The time to make a place look inviting is before the sale, not after! This place was sold unfurnished. Put those cows into the protoplasm vat.’</p>
<p>‘Yes sir,’ Orin said. ‘Terribly sorry, sir. Is there anything else?’</p>
<p>‘There’s about ten thousand other things wrong,’ Maudsley said. ‘But you can figure out those for yourselves, I hope. What, for example, is this?’ He pointed at Carmody. ‘A statue or something? Is he supposed to sing a song or recite a poem when the new race arrives?’</p>
<p>Carmody said, ‘Sir, I am not part of this. A friend of yours named Melichrone sent me, and I’m trying to get home to my own planet –’</p>
<p>Maudsley clearly did not hear what Carmody was saying. For, while Carmody was trying to speak, Maudsley was saying, ‘Whatever he is, the job specs don’t call for him. So stick him back in the protoplasm vat with the cows.’</p>
<p>‘Hey!’ Carmody shouted as workmen lifted him up by his arms. ‘Hey, wait a minute!’ he screamed. ‘I’m not a part of this planet! Melichrone sent me! Wait, hold on, listen to me!’</p>
<p>‘You really ought to be ashamed of yourselves,’ Maudsley went on, oblivious to Carmody’s shrieks. ‘What was that supposed to be? One of your interior decorating touches, Orin?’</p>
<p>‘Oh no,’ Orin said. ‘I didn’t put him there.’</p>
<p>‘Then it was you, Brookside.’</p>
<p>‘I never saw him before in my life, chief.’</p>
<p>‘Hmm,’ Maudsley said. ‘You’re both fools, but you’ve never been liars. Hey!’ he shouted to the workmen. ‘Bring him back here!’</p>
<p>‘All right, pull yourself together,’ Maudsley said to Carmody, who was shaking uncontrollably. ‘Get a grip on yourself, I can’t wait around here while you have a fit of hysterics! Better now? All right, would you mind explaining just what you’re doing trespassing on my property and why I shouldn’t have you converted into protoplasm?’</p>
</section>
