<h3>第十三章</h3>
<h3>战争的影响</h3>
<p>到1940年秋天，天狼星已经在凯尔·布莱稳稳立足，正在筹划对牧场、羊种和耕地作大规模改进；附近的人也已经习惯称他为“普格的人狗”。至于他的心智能力究竟达到什么程度，却没有人说得清。普格把关于他的全部真相都说了出来，反而让人们摸错了方向。大家都知道这条人狗对羊具有不可思议的本领，也知道他正按照最新科学原则管理羊群；可这些事情仍被模模糊糊地理解为一种由科学神秘植入他的“超级本能”，而不是真正的智力。人们也知道，他能听懂相当多的话，而且只要掌握他那古怪发音的窍门，甚至能听懂他自己说出来的词。他也在学一点威尔士语；而正因为他的威尔士语还十分粗浅，偏偏当地唯一普遍使用的语言就是威尔士语，这反而掩盖了他的真正语言才能和完全达到人类水平的心智。</p>
<p>即便如此，若不是正逢战争时期，报纸也一定早就大肆报道他，而且造成的轰动，肯定会远远超过早些年那场“会说话的獴”新闻噱头。</p>
<p>天狼星同周围大多数农民和村民都处得很好，彻底赢得了人们喜爱；不过仍有少数人固执地用怀疑眼光看他。虔诚的礼拜堂信徒断言，这条人狗真正的主人不是普格，而是撒旦；普格为了度过劳动力短缺，已经把灵魂卖给魔鬼。另一些满脑子性念头的人知道人狗同科学家小女儿之间感情极深，于是悄悄流传说，最先卖掉灵魂的其实是托马斯本人：他为了获得科学名望同魔鬼做交易；而撒旦如今化身为狗，经常通过同托马斯女儿进行变态的性交来满足自己。至于那个姑娘，他们说，尽管颇有魅力，也比女巫好不到哪里去。任何人一看就知道，她身上有某种奇怪而非人的东西。另一类谣言则来自比较粗鲁的爱国者。他们宣称托马斯受纳粹收买，而纳粹已经在他的人狗身上找到了最理想的间谍。那头动物被安置在一个大型炮兵营地附近，绝非偶然。</p>
<p>大多数人都足够理智，没有认真相信这些传言。普格很受欢迎，天狼星也是；他在牧羊方面毫无疑问是个天才，也给整个地区增添了光彩。托马斯虽然是英格兰人，也已经在本地赢得敬意；他的女儿尽管作风新潮，仍是个讨人喜欢的姑娘。直到战争压力持续得太久，使朴素的人开始到处寻找替罪羊时，公众态度才逐渐转向敌意。</p>
<p>当伦敦开始遭受大规模空袭时，伊丽莎白收到杰弗里一封很长的信，信里描述了他教区的情况，并请求她收留几个逃难儿童，再设法把其他孩子安排到附近合适的人家。杰弗里属于那种极相信个人责任的人。他一向不太信任政府组织，因此很希望尽可能避免把自己照看的孩子简单交给官方疏散机构。</p>
<p>杰弗里描述大轰炸中出现的毁灭、英雄主义、混乱、麻木和人类善意，这些内容深深震动了天狼星。他清楚记得杰弗里家里的气味，记得那座朴素小教堂的气味，也记得自己拜访过的一间间空气沉闷的住宅。那些嗅觉形象在他脑中唤起一幅丰富图景：人类一面同敌对环境搏斗，一面又同自身的不足搏斗。杰弗里报告的伤亡者中，有许多他还记得；那些需要别人收留的孩子里，也有不少他见过。他产生一阵慷慨冲动，恨不得立刻把鞍囊装满急救用品赶去伦敦。可那终究只是愚蠢冲动。他去了大概只会碍事。再说，享受一次慷慨冲动是一回事，真正把它付诸行动又是另一回事。他怀疑自己一旦遇到空袭，会是个十足的胆小鬼；而且从根本上说，他始终同整场战争保持距离。人类如果蠢到用这种疯狂方式折磨自己，那跟他又有什么关系？尽管如此，杰弗里的叙述仍使他深受感动，他对杰弗里本人的感情更使这种感受无法摆脱。后来，一个脱离编队的袭击者随手丢下一枚炸弹，却恰巧准确落在附近一所孤零零的村舍上，屋内所有人不是死亡就是受伤。战争中这种偶然似乎极常见；而这件事又使伦敦的处境对天狼星和当地居民都变得更加具体、更加真实。</p>
<p>伊丽莎白答应在自己家里接收三个伦敦孩子；普格太太尽管十分不安，也同意再收留两个。天狼星则把自己在凯尔·布莱的房间让了出来。附近大多数主妇早已接收过从西北部遭轰炸城市疏散来的孩子，或者干脆拒绝接收；不过伊丽莎白逐户拜访后，还是能够告诉杰弗里，本地还能安排十五个孩子和两位母亲。碰巧这一带此前接收的小移民整体上相当走运。虽然一些女主人抱怨很多，总体来说疏散计划运行得还算不错。可后来这二十个伦敦小家伙却完全是另一回事。他们浑身有味、长虱子、无法无天，简直是一群小野崽子；当地人议论说，任何体面主妇如果事先知道他们是什么样，绝不会让他们跨进自家门槛。他们把屋里弄得一团糟，打坏家具，毁掉花园，说谎、偷窃，彼此咬，也咬女主人，折磨猫，还满口骇人的脏话。</p>
<p>有些女主人有足够见识，明白这些孩子不过是环境的产物。她们说，社会竟允许最不幸的成员在如此堕落的处境中长大，才真正令人震惊。缺乏想象力的主妇们却沉醉于一种自以为正义的愤怒，把怒火全倒在孩子和他们父母身上。还有些人把问题归结为：这些移民是英格兰人，而英格兰人就是这个德性。至于伊丽莎白，她的声望也多少受了损害。毕竟大家认为，最近这场麻烦完全是她招来的。有些人不仅想起她自己也是英格兰人，还想起她丈夫据说曾把灵魂卖给魔鬼。更让事情显得糟糕的是，偏偏她家收留的孩子表现得相当不错。她有一种天然本领，能把孩子当作真正的人来对待，同时也理直气壮地要求他们用体面的方式回应。起初当然麻烦不断；可几周之后，那个小姑娘和她两个弟弟已经会骄傲地帮忙做家务、整理花园。</p>
<p>一天，伊丽莎白收到杰弗里的消息，说他的教堂已经被炸毁，不过他仍把全部时间都用来照顾教区居民。经过长期奔走，他和其他人终于推动当局大幅改善当地公共防空洞条件，这让他非常振奋。几天后，伊丽莎白又收到一封陌生笔迹的信，告诉她：她的表兄已经遇难。</p>
<p>杰弗里的死，使战争第一次奇异地逼近天狼星。第一次，一个他认识、也爱的人彻底消失了。不知为什么，这让整件事显出一种新的比例。本来不应该如此；他一直以为自己已经相当充分地想象过战争落到个人身上的冲击，显然并没有。杰弗里就这样不再存在，像一根火柴的火焰被吹灭一样。如此简单，却又如此不可思议！更奇怪的是，如今杰弗里反而仿佛比从前更真实，也离他更近。连续好几天，天狼星都会发现自己悄悄同杰弗里交谈，并且在脑中得到十分像样的回答。古怪！毫无疑问只是想象玩的把戏。可在内心深处，他又怎么也无法真正相信杰弗里已经彻底熄灭。或者更准确地说，他的一部分很有把握地相信，另一部分却就是做不到。他还做了一个荒诞的梦：杰弗里到地狱里寻找思韦茨，发现思韦茨口袋里装着天狼星的灵魂。后来杰弗里不知怎样把思韦茨带上了天堂，而他的奖赏，就是天狼星获得释放。</p>
<p>战争很快还会再向天狼星靠近一步。五月，托马斯开车带他去沙普附近一座农场参观。那里正在成功使用几只超级牧羊犬，几乎把日常全部牧羊工作都承担下来。返回北威尔士的路线要经过利物浦。默西塞德一带时不时遭到相当猛烈的空袭，托马斯判断，最好天黑前就过河。不幸的是，他们出发得晚，到利物浦时已近黄昏。城市外围某处，汽车又出了发动机故障；等一名忙得筋疲力尽的修车工把问题处理好，天已经黑了。他们重新上路，却因为城市状况耽搁许久。前一晚刚发生过一次严重空袭，街道还没有清理干净。结果，在他们赶到著名的默西河隧道入口之前，新一轮空袭开始了。隧道已经不远，托马斯决定继续赶过去。天狼星却吓坏了。那些声音对他敏感的耳朵而言，大概比对迟钝得多的人耳更加难熬。无论如何，除了进入狼性状态的时候，他本来就一直很胆小。飞机的呻吟，防空炮巨大而猛烈的轰响，炸弹撕裂空气的疾冲——在他听来像一种沙哑、被放大到不可思议程度的耳语——随后是大到他此前无法相信世上竟会存在的爆炸；接着砖石坠落，火焰轰鸣噼啪，人脚仓皇奔跑；汽车经过一座被炸毁的防空洞时，伤员尖叫着求救。这一切彻底击垮了他的胆气。他坐在汽车后座，什么也做不了，只能害怕。还有那些气味：炸药气体刺鼻的气味，碎裂砖石的尘土味，木料燃烧时辛辣的烟味，以及偶尔飘来的、人体被炸烂后的恶臭。</p>
<p>再继续前进似乎已经发疯，于是托马斯把车靠到路边，两人朝最近的防空洞冲去。一枚炸弹爆炸产生的冲击波把一所房子的侧墙整个推过街道，正好向他们压来。托马斯被压在下面；天狼星虽然撞伤、割伤，却仍能活动。托马斯下半身被砖石埋住。他忍着巨大痛苦，费力喘道：“你自己逃。往隧道。沿这条街。然后回威尔士。救你自己，看在我的份上。走吧，求你，走！”天狼星发疯似的用爪子和牙齿搬砖石，却根本搬不动。“我去找人。”他说。“不，自己逃，”托马斯喘道，“反正我——完了——。祝你好运。”可天狼星还是冲了出去。不久，他咬住一个男人的外套往回拽，一边呜咽。很明显他是在替某个人求救，于是一队人跟着他回来。可他们赶到托马斯原先所在的地方时，那里只剩一个新炸出的弹坑。那些人又回去处理自己的工作，留下天狼星茫然望着。他在那里嗅了很久，凄惨地呜咽。随后，先前被行动暂时压下去的恐惧再次涌上来。不过他的头脑仍然清醒。他必须找到隧道入口，托马斯说过，那里很近。他借着云层反射的火光匆匆前进。有一处道路完全被坍塌砖石堵死，他只得爬过去。最后总算找到隧道，并设法没被人注意就溜了进去。他沿人行道小跑；尽管一股汽车流正朝伯肯黑德方向行驶，在封闭隧道里制造出可怕噪声，却没有人太在意他。到了伯肯黑德一端，他猛地冲出去获得自由，于是再次置身于战争那威胁性的轰响下，也再次看见被火光映亮的天空。不过炸弹主要仍落在默西河另一边的利物浦。</p>
<p>天狼星后来曾非常详细地向我讲述从伯肯黑德一路跋涉到特劳斯瓦尼德的长途行程，不过这里没有必要逐一记录。身体疲惫、精神又遭到重创的他，向西穿过城镇，再横越威勒尔半岛，朝瑟斯塔斯通荒原前进。夜里一路小跑时，他的思绪不断回到托马斯那场彻底消失。这个人创造了他；最初，他曾以毫无批判的犬类忠诚崇拜他；后来则开始严厉批评他，可始终保留着深厚感情，也始终尊重他的能力。从理智上说，天狼星几乎不怀疑托马斯已经单纯地停止存在；可和杰弗里的死亡一样，他仍无法真正相信。沿一段公路慢跑时，他忽然发现自己正在就这个问题同托马斯争辩。那个死人坚持说，如今整个宇宙里已经没有任何东西可以合理称为“托马斯·特雷隆”，也不存在任何一个心灵继续承接那个心灵的思想、欲望和感受。“好吧，你自己当然最清楚。”天狼星说。随后他突然停住，怀疑自己是不是已经疯了。</p>
<p>从瑟斯塔斯通，他沿迪河河口岸边前进，越过盐沼到昆斯费里；随后穿过公路、田野和荒原，始终朝西南方向走。一路上，他常常猜想，到底有多少方向感来自低于人类的哺乳动物著名的“归巢本能”，又有多少来自自己对托马斯地图的模糊记忆。长距离走公路非常累。机动车不断给他造成麻烦，司机几乎不怎么体谅他。他开始想象，暴君物种其实是一种由人和机器拼接成的复合生物。他多么憎恨那种刺耳声音和粗暴作风！可就在昨天，他自己还坐在托马斯敞篷车里，汽车飞驰穿过兰开夏平原时，他也曾因为速度和扑面风流而兴奋。眼下的狼狈处境却让他比以往任何时候都更清楚地看见，人类对那些碰巧不是自己宠物的“不会说话的动物”有多轻蔑、多无情。</p>
<p>每当经过人口较多的地方，他都尽量不引人注意。他会放慢速度，在路边随意游荡，像当地任何一条狗那样嗅路灯柱。若有人因为好奇或友善向他招呼——这种情况颇常见，毕竟他是一头十分惹眼的动物——他就懒洋洋摆一下尾巴，却绝不停下。越过克卢伊迪安山脉和宽阔的克卢伊德谷之后，他重新进入大片荒原，又在雾里严重迷路；不过最后，他还是在彭特雷沃拉斯附近下降到低地，不久便朝米格奈恩高而荒凉的丘陵前进。他费力爬上一道陡峭草坡，钻进厚重云雾，雨也开始落下。尽管已经极其疲惫，而且前方还有一件痛苦任务等待着他，那股冰冷湿润的风、沼泽与草皮的气味、羊群的气味，仍令他产生一种狂喜。有一次，他闻到明确无误的狐狸气味——那种罕见、使人陶醉、属于最难追踪猎物的气味。上帝啊！感觉本身的性质仿佛总在暗示某些精妙而隐藏的东西：永远值得追逐，却永远无法真正找到。连视觉也有这种能力。被风赶着疾走的雾，时隐时现的岩石，以及一簇簇草尖上像珠宝一样挂着的雾滴，都用一种甜美熟悉和永远无法理解的诱惑刺进他心里！当然，这一切也许不过是电子、波列，以及神经末梢受到刺激；可它又是如此甜美、如此神秘、如此令人害怕，仿佛其中藏着某种尚未理解的真理！而最近亲眼见过的恐怖，不知怎么又把眼前全部美景强化到近乎疼痛的程度。</p>
<p>他不停向上爬，甚至对山势之高感到惊讶。忽然之间，雾短暂散开，他发现自己已经接近一座相当高的山峰顶端，很快认出那是卡内兹·菲拉斯特。很久以前，在开始牧羊之前，他常常从加斯出发来这些荒原打猎，只不过很少走得这么远。</p>
<p>如今重新站到高地上，一种不同心境渐渐占据他。自己为什么还要回到暴君物种那里？为什么非得承受告诉伊丽莎白“你丈夫不会回来”的痛苦？为什么不干脆在荒原上野生，保持自由，蔑视全人类，以兔子为食，偶尔也杀一只羊，直到最后某个人把自己打死？为什么不？杀死思韦茨之后，他曾短暂野生过，可那次被良心毁掉了；这一次会不同。到现在已经非常清楚，生命没有多少东西可提供给他。不错，他算是找到一个位置，可那位置仍依靠人的帮助和宽容，而且束缚得很。他无法充分展开自己的能力。奇怪的是，这一次把他从这些阴沉想法中拉回来的，并不是托马斯，而是想到自己那群没人照看的羊。</p>
<p>雾如今重新厚厚压住群山，天色也已经进入黄昏；不过天狼星已经看清方位，因此能够摸索着下降到一条高处的泥炭沼泽谷地，再绕上阿雷尼格·瓦赫山肩。很快他到了小卡内兹·亚戈，随后在黑暗中跌跌撞撞下坡，朝公路走去，在库姆·普里索尔谷口附近穿过。把荒凉高地谷地留在左边之后，他终于进入熟悉的家乡牧场。此刻哪怕在黑暗里，每一块峭岩、每一个小土丘、每一泓水潭，几乎每一丛石南和草，都熟悉得充满联想。这里，他曾发现过一只死羊和一只半出生的羔羊；这里，他曾同托马斯一起坐着吃三明治，那是从前一次漫长步行中的休息，而那样的步行永远不会再发生；这里，他曾杀死一只野兔。虽然每一步都熟悉，越来越深的黑暗和浓雾仍严重拖慢了他。抵达加斯时，已经将近午夜。我估算，从清晨离开瑟斯塔斯通荒原算起，把一路上所有偏离直线的漫长绕行都算进去，他总共走了八十多英里。旅程很大一部分是在坚硬公路上完成的，另一部分则穿过围着树篱、极难通行的农业地区。</p>
<p>到了黑暗房屋门前，他发出自己那种专门用来叫人的吠声。伊丽莎白立刻把门打开，让他一下进入刺眼灯光和熟悉的家的气味里。他还一个字没说，她已经关上门，跪下抱住他，说：“感谢上帝，你们至少有一个安全回来了。”“只有我。”他说。她短促地呻吟一声，随后沉默地紧紧搂住他。这个姿势有些别扭，再加上过去几天的紧张已经把他耗空，室内空气又让他觉得压抑，他忽然眼前发黑，整个身体软倒在她怀里。伊丽莎白把他的头放低到地板上，去取白兰地。可他很快又恢复意识，摇摇晃晃站起来，仍尽职却虚弱地在门垫上擦了擦脚，然后步子不稳地走进起居室。直到这时，他才意识到自己腹部和腿下全沾满沼泽里湿黑的泥。伊丽莎白回来时，看见他四腿发抖、垂着头站在那里，不知道该怎么办。“躺下吧，宝贝，”她说，“弄脏一点算不了什么。”不久，她先让他一口口舔着甜茶，又给他吃了面包泡牛奶。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a580"></span>CHAPTER XIII</h3>
<p><span class="t28">B</span><span class="t30">Y </span><span class="t31">THE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">AUTUMN</span><span class="t30"> </span>of 1940 Sirius was well established at Caer Blai, was planning great improvements for the pasture, the breed and the arable land, and was known in the neighbourhood as “Pugh’s man-dog.” Precisely what his mental stature was, no one could decide. Pugh, by telling the whole truth about him, had put them off the scent. It was known that the man-dog had a marvellous facility with sheep, and was managing them on the latest scientific principles. But all this was vaguely thought to be less a matter of intelligence than of a sort of super-instinct mysteriously implanted in him by science. It was known too that he could understand a good deal of speech, and was actually able to use words himself to those who had the key to his queer pronunciation. He was learning a bit of Welsh; and the fact that his Welsh was so rudimentary, and that this was the language which alone was familiar in the district, disguised from everyone his true linguistic gifts and his fully human mentality.</p>
<p>Even so, had it not been war-time, the newspapers would certainly have publicized him, and with far more success than befell the stunt of the talking mongoose at an earlier date.</p>
<p>Sirius made himself thoroughly popular with most of the surrounding farmers and villagers, but there were a few who stubbornly regarded him with suspicion. Devout chapel-goers affirmed that the man-dog’s real master was not Pugh but Satan, and that Pugh had sold his soul to the Devil to help him out of the labour-shortage. Some of the sexually obsessed, aware of the great affection that held between the man-dog and the scientist’s younger daughter, whispered that it was Thomas, in the first instance, who had sold his soul, in order to gain scientific fame, and that Satan, incarnate in the dog, habitually gratified himself in perverse sexual intercourse with Thomas’s daughter. And she, they said, for all her charm, was little better than a witch. Anyone could see that there was something queer and inhuman about her. Rumours of a different type were spread by the cruder sort of patriots. They declared that Thomas was in the pay of the Nazis, who had found in his man-dog the ideal kind of spy. It was no accident that the animal was established in the neighbourhood of a big artillery camp.</p>
<p>Most people were too sensible to take these rumours seriously. Pugh was popular, and so was Sirius; for he was certainly a genius with sheep, and he lent distinction to the district. Thomas, though an Englishman, had won a place in local esteem; and his daughter, in spite of her new-fangled ways, was an attractive girl. Not till the pressure of war had been much prolonged, driving simple folk to look for scapegoats, was public opinion to become hostile.</p>
<p>When the great air attacks on London began, Elizabeth received a long letter from Geoffrey describing conditions in his parish, and urging her to take in some of his refugee children, and plant out others in suitable homes in her district. Geoffrey was one of those who believed in personal responsibility. He suspected all Government organization; consequently he was anxious to avoid as far as possible merely handing over his charges to the official evacuation authorities.</p>
<p>Geoffrey’s account of the devastation, heroism, muddle, callousness, and human kindness in the great blitzes had a deep effect on Sirius. He remembered vividly the smell of Geoffrey’s home, and of the bare little church and all the stuffy houses that he had visited. And these olfactory images called up in his mind a rich picture of human beings struggling against a hostile environment and their own inadequacy. He remembered many of the people whom Geoffrey reported as casualties, and many of the children for whom hospitality was required. He had a generous impulse to rush off to London at once with his panniers filled with first-aid equipment. But it was a foolish impulse. He would only be in the way. Besides, it was one thing to enjoy a generous impulse and quite another to put it in action. He suspected that he would be a thorough coward in an air raid; and anyhow, fundamentally he felt aloof from the whole war. If the human race was fool enough to torture itself in this crazy manner, what had he to do with it? Nevertheless he could not help being deeply moved by Geoffrey’s story, and by affection for Geoffrey himself. The plight of London became even more vivid to him and to the local population when, by one of those flukes which seem common in war, a single bomb, dropped at random by a stray raider, fell neatly on a lonely cottage in the neighbourhood, killing or wounding all its occupants.</p>
<p>Elizabeth undertook to take three London children into her house, and Mrs. Pugh, with much misgiving, agreed to accommodate two more. Sirius gave up his room at Caer Blai. Most of the local wives had already either taken on evacuees from blitzed towns in the north-west, or had refused to do so; but Elizabeth, after paying many calls, was able to tell Geoffrey that she had accommodation for fifteen more children and two mothers. It so happened that the neighbourhood had been rather lucky so far with its little immigrants. Though there had been a good deal of grumbling on the part of some hostesses, on the whole the scheme had worked. But the twenty little Londoners were a different kettle of fish. They were smelly, lousy, unruly little brats, and it was said in the district that no decent housewife would have had them across her doorstep if she had known what they were like. They made horrid messes in the house, broke the furniture, ruined the garden, lied, stole, bit one another and their hostesses, tormented the cat, and used dreadful language.</p>
<p>Some of the hostesses had the wit to realize that these children were simply the product of circumstances. It was shocking, they said, that society should allow its more unfortunate members to grow up in such degradation. The less imaginative housewives, however, indulged in orgies of self-righteous indignation against the children themselves and their parents. Some took the line that the immigrants were English, and that was what the English were like. As for Elizabeth, her popularity suffered somewhat. She alone was responsible for this recent affliction. It was remembered in some quarters not only that she was English, but that her husband had sold his soul to the Devil. Matters were made worse by the fact that her own evacuees turned out quite well. She was one of those who had a natural gift for treating children as human persons and expecting to be treated decently in return. There were troubles enough at first. But in a few weeks the little girl and her two small brothers were proudly helping with the house and the garden.</p>
<p>One day Elizabeth had news from Geoffrey that his church had been destroyed, but that he was continuing to devote his whole time to the care of his parishioners. He was elated that after long agitation he and others had secured great improvements in the public shelters in the district. A few days later she received a letter in an unknown hand saying that her cousin had been killed.</p>
<p>The news of Geoffrey’s death seemed to bring the war strangely near to Sirius. For the first time someone whom he knew and loved had vanished. This somehow put the whole thing into a new perspective. It should not have done so. He thought he had imagined the personal impact of war pretty well, but evidently he had not. Geoffrey had simply ceased to be, like a match flame when it is blown out. So simple, and yet somehow so incredible! For in a queer way Geoffrey now seemed more real than before, and nearer to him. For days he caught himself quietly talking to Geoffrey and getting perfectly good answers from him in his own mind. Queer! Just a trick of imagination, no doubt. But somehow he couldn’t really in his heart believe that Geoffrey had simply been snuffed out. Or rather, part of him believed it confidently and another part just couldn’t. He had a fantastic dream. Geoffrey sought out Thwaites in Hell, and found him with Sirius’s soul in his pocket. Somehow Geoffrey brought Thwaites up to Heaven, and his reward was the freeing of Sirius.</p>
<p>The war was soon to come even nearer to Sirius. In May Thomas took him by car to visit a farm near Shap, where several super-sheep-dogs were being successfully used to do practically the whole routine work on the sheep. The route back to North Wales passed through Liverpool. There had been a good deal of raiding on Merseyside from time to time, and Thomas judged it wise to be well across the river before dark. Unfortunately they were late in starting, and did not arrive in Liverpool till dusk. Somewhere in the outskirts of the town they developed engine trouble, and by the time an overworked garage hand had put matters right it was dark. They set out once more, but were much delayed by the condition of the town. There had been a bad raid on the previous night, and the streets had not yet been properly cleared. The result was that, before they could reach the entrance of the famous tunnel under the Mersey, a raid began. It was not far to the tunnel, so Thomas decided to hurry on. Sirius was terrified. Probably the noise was even more trying to his sensitive ears than to the duller human organ. Anyhow he had always been a coward, save in the wolf-mood. The moaning of planes, the prodigious smack and racket of anti-aircraft guns, the tearing rush of bombs (like a raucous and vastly amplified whisper, he thought), followed by such a crash as he had not believed possible, and then the clatter of falling masonry, the roaring and crackling of fires, the scurrying of human feet, the screams of casualties demanding help as the car passed a wrecked shelter, all this had a shattering effect on his morale. Sitting there in the back of the car he had nothing to do but be terrified. Then there were the smells, the stinging smells of gases from explosives, the dusty smell of shattered masonry, the pungent smell of burning woodwork, and occasionally the stench of mangled human bodies.</p>
<p>It seemed madness to go on any farther, so Thomas drew the car into the side of the road, and they dashed for the nearest shelter. The blast from a bomb pushed the side of a house across the street at them. Thomas was pinned under it; Sirius, though bruised and cut, was free. The lower part of Thomas’s body was covered with masonry. With great difficulty and in great pain he gasped out, “Save yourself. By the tunnel. Down the street. Then to Wales. Save yourself, for my sake. Please go, please!” Sirius tried frantically to shift the debris with his paws and teeth, but could not. “I’ll get help,” he said. “No, save yourself,” Thomas gasped. “I’m — done — anyhow. Good luck.” But Sirius hurried off, and presently was tugging at a man’s coat, and whimpering. It was obvious that he wanted help for someone, so a party came back with him. But when they reached the place where Thomas had been, they found only a fresh crater. The men returned to their former task, leaving Sirius blankly gazing. He sniffed about for a long while, whimpering miserably. Then his terror, which had been blotted out by action, welled up again. But his head was clear. He must find the tunnel entrance, which Thomas had said was quite near. He hurried along by the light of fires reflected from the clouds. At one point the road was completely blocked by fallen masonry, and he had to clamber over it. At last he reached the tunnel and managed to sneak in unobserved. He cantered along the footpath; and though a stream of cars was travelling towards Birkenhead, making a terrifying noise in the confined space of the tunnel, no one took much notice of him. At the Birkenhead entrance he made a dash for liberty, and found himself once more in the threatening racket of war and under the fire-lit sky. But the bombs were falling mostly on the Liverpool side of the Mersey.</p>
<p>Sirius gave me a very full account of his long trek from Birkenhead to Trawsfynydd, but there is no need to report it here in detail. Tired and mentally shattered, he made his way westward through the town and out across the Wirral towards Thurstastone Common. As he cantered through the night his mind kept reverting to the complete disappearance of Thomas, the being who had made him, whom he had at first adored with uncritical canine devotion, and more recently had strongly criticized, while always retaining a deep affection for him, and a deep respect for his powers. Intellectually he had little doubt that Thomas had simply ceased to exist; yet, as with Geoffrey’s death, he could not really believe it. Lobbing along a stretch of road, he found himself arguing with Thomas on the subject. The dead man insisted that there was now nothing anywhere in the universe which could reasonably be called Thomas Trelone, no mind continuous with that mind’s thoughts, desires, feelings. “Well, you ought to know,” said Sirius; and then came to a sudden halt, wondering if he was going mad.</p>
<p>From Thurstastone he travelled along the shore of the Dee Estuary and over the salt marshes to Queensferry, then by road and field and moorland, always in a south-westerly direction. Often he wondered how much he was guided by the sub-human mammal’s proverbial “homing faculty,” how far by vague memory of Thomas’s maps. The long stretches of road he found very tiring. The motor traffic was a constant worry, for the drivers gave him little consideration. He imagined the tyrant species as a composite creature made up of man and machine. How he hated its harsh voice and brutal ways! Yet only yesterday he himself, sitting in Thomas’s open tourer, had exulted in the speed and the wind as they streamed across the Lancashire plain. His present plight made him realize more clearly than ever how contemptuous and heartless men were towards “dumb animals” that did not happen to be their own pets.</p>
<p>Whenever he passed through a populous area he was careful to attract as little attention as possible. He slowed down and strayed about the road, sniffing at lamp-posts, like any local dog. When anyone made inquisitive or friendly advances, which was fairly often, for he was a striking animal, he replied with a nonchalant tail-wag, but never stopped. After crossing the Clwydian Range and the wide Vale of Clwyd, he made his way up into ample moors, and went badly astray in mist; but at last he dropped down into lower country near Pentrevoelas, and soon he was heading for the high wild hills of Migneint. Toiling up a steep grassy spur he entered heavy cloud, and it began to rain. Tired as he was, and with a painful task awaiting him, he exulted in the cold wet wind, the scents of the bog and turf and sheep. Once he caught the unmistakable odour of fox, that rare, intoxicating smell of the most illusive quarry. God! How the qualities of sensation seemed to hint at exquisite hidden things, always to be pursued, never found. Even sight could do it. The driving mist, the half-seen rocks, appearing and vanishing, and all the little grass-plumes jewelled with mist-drops, how they stabbed him with sweet familiarity and with a never-comprehended lure! No doubt it was all electrons and wave-trains and the tickling of nerve-endings, but oh how sweet and mysterious and frightening with uncomprehended truth! Somehow the beauty of it all was intensified to the point of agony by the horrors that he had so recently witnessed.</p>
<p>On and on he climbed, surprised at the height. Then suddenly the mist cleared for a moment, and he found himself almost at the top of a considerable mountain, which he soon identified as Carnedd Filast. Long ago, before he had taken to sheep, he used to hunt on these moors from Garth, but it was not often that he came quite so far afield.</p>
<p>Now that he was once more on the high hills, a change of mood came over him. Why should he go back to the tyrant species at all? Why give himself the pain of telling Elizabeth that her husband would not return? Why not live wild on the moor, free, spurning all mankind, feeding on rabbits and perhaps an occasional sheep, till at last man did him to death? Why not? He had lived wild for a time after killing Thwaites, but that had been spoilt by conscience. This time it would be different. It was clear by now that life had little to offer him. True, he had found a niche of sorts, but only by man’s aid and tolerance. And it was a cramping niche. He could not extend his powers thoroughly. Strangely, on this occasion, it was the thought not of Thomas but of his sheep unshepherded that turned his attention from these gloomy meditations.</p>
<p>The mist now closed down heavily on the mountains, and it was already twilight; but he had taken his bearings, and was able to grope his way down towards a high boggy valley, and then up round a shoulder of Arenig Fach. Soon he was on little Carnedd Iago, then stumbling down in darkness towards the road, which he crossed near the head of Cwm Prysor. Leaving the wild moorland valley on his left, he came into home pastures. Now, even in darkness, every crag, every hummock, every pool, almost every tussock of heather or grass was familiar, and redolent with associations. Here he had found a dead sheep and half-born lamb. Here he had sat with Thomas, eating sandwiches on one of those long walks that would never be repeated. Here he had killed a hare. But though every step was familiar, increasing darkness and the heavy mist greatly delayed him. It was almost midnight when he reached Garth. I calculate that, since leaving Thurstastone Common in the early morning, he must have covered altogether, including all his lengthy aberrations from the direct route, well over eighty miles. Much of the journey was on hard roads or through difficult, hedged agricultural country.</p>
<p>At the door of the darkened house he gave his special summoning bark. Elizabeth promptly let him into the blinding light and the familiar smells of home. Before he had said a word, she had closed the door, knelt down, and put her arms round him, saying “Thank God one of you is safe.” “Only me,” he said. She gave one small moan, and clung to him in silence. Held in a rather awkward position, tired out after the strain of the last few days, and oppressed by the indoor atmosphere, he suddenly went deadly faint, and wilted in her arms. She laid his head low on the floor and went to fetch some brandy. But in a moment he recovered, staggered on to his feet, dutifully but feebly wiped them on the door-mat, and walked unsteadily into the sitting-room. Then he realized that his under surface was covered with the wet black mud of the bog. When Elizabeth returned, he was standing with trembling legs and hanging head, wondering what to do. “Lie down, precious,” she said. “The mess is nothing.” Presently she had him lapping up sweet tea, and then eating bread and milk.</p>
