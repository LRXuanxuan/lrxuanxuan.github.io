<h3>讨论</h3>
<p>R. A. 史密斯先生提议向演讲者致谢。他说，斯特普尔顿博士为行星际事业提供的哲学支持尤其令他感激。不过，对于斯特普尔顿博士为殖民那些超大行星所设想的一些奇形怪状的“人类”，实在很难产生任何亲缘感或同情。斯特普尔顿博士回答说，只要某种生命形式以根本价值为方向，无论它与我们多么不同，他都能够接受；而在他看来，这些根本价值在一切时代、对一切种族都应当成立。</p>
<p>随后，A. C. 克拉克先生正式开启讨论，并首先评论了一些技术问题。他认为，太阳系中的生命——虽然未必是智慧生命——可能比人们通常想象得更普遍，种类也更多。即使暂且不算四颗巨行星——它们看来有相当部分是气态的——太阳系中仍有十四个面积超过一百万平方英里的天体，还有至少两百颗面积超过一万平方英里的小行星。我们有得探索呢！</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿博士谈到了通过心灵感应与其他种族建立联系的可能性；但至少就太阳系而言，大概还需要某种“千里眼”能力，因为另一端并不存在一个智慧主体来“发送”信息。克拉克先生说，他经常从一些灵媒式人物那里听到对火星的生动描述，希望在第一次火星探险之前能够把这类报告收集起来，因为那将提供一次独一无二的机会，对这些说法作出令人信服的检验。不过，要是技术委员会辛辛苦苦研究了多年原子飞船，最后却让那些千里眼先到火星，他们一定会非常恼火。</p>
<p>克拉克先生认为，斯特普尔顿博士多少高估了星际飞行的困难——这些困难当然确实巨大。如果能够研制出高效率的原子多级火箭，理论上速度可以超过光速的百分之九十。这样一来，人类也许五到十年就能抵达最近的恒星；而由于相对论效应，对乘员本人而言经过的时间还可能比这短得多。</p>
<p>克拉克先生认为，即使相关技术困难能够克服，人类也未必能够参加一个银河文明。即便只是管理一个由十几个世界组成的太阳系文明，人类心智都可能已经被逼到极限；银河社会所意味着的复杂度，或许要再增加十亿倍。不过，为这些终极问题忧虑是愚蠢的。现在真正应该做的，是尽可能经营好我们自己的行星，以及等我们抵达之后的其他世界。</p>
<p>随后，众多听众参加了讨论。下面列出其中若干问题。</p>
<p>一位发言者提出，在人类殖民金星之前，也许可以先把合适的植物生命形式从地球送过去，借此改变金星的气候和大气；他还补充说，也许地球生命最初正是以类似方式从火星来到这里的。</p>
<p>一位化学家反对轻率谈论利用原子能毁灭整个人类。他指出，当时全球人口每天的增加量，就与长崎原子弹造成的死亡人数相当。裂变材料如此稀少，看起来几乎不可能被用来改变气候或地理特征。</p>
<p>另一位发言者想知道：如果我们遇到一个文化发展水平与我们大致相当的种族，会发生什么？到底是谁把谁“腌起来”？还有人提出，如果我们遇到的存在具有与我们完全不同的感觉系统，沟通也许会遇到无法克服的困难；彼此之间甚至可能像威尔斯《盲人国》中描写的那样彻底无法理解。</p>
<p>一位会员询问，与其设法进化出新型人类来殖民其他行星，是否不如改造地球上的非人类生命形式，让它们承担这一任务。</p>
<p>最后一位发言者说，如果我们遇到比我们落后的种族，绝不能把它们赶进保留地，而应该教育它们。如何挑选合适的殖民者，本身也会是一个困难的心理学问题。</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿博士在回应中说，虽然当前危机要求我们先把自己家里收拾好，但科学事业绝不能过分功利化，而应当始终开辟新的边疆。我们还必须对未知保持谦逊和敬畏；近来人类已经犯下了“hubris”——因自身成就而骄傲自大的罪。</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿博士并不同意自己夸大了原子能的潜力。我们目前对重元素的利用只不过是开端；以后也许能够像恒星那样，从轻元素中释放能量。</p>
<p>如果我们遇到一个文化水平与我们相近的种族，斯特普尔顿博士希望双方能够合作，而不是像过去对待原住民族那样对待它。反过来，如果对方比我们先进，把地球变成一个“保留地”，那就会成为人类的终结，因为人类将失去继续生活的意志。他同意我们应该设法教育其他种族——但前提是，我们必须先确定自己是在把它们教育得更好，而不是把它们降低到我们的水平。</p>
<p>他认为，拥有与我们彻底不同感觉系统的种族是不可能的。如果它们与我们共享同一个物理宇宙，就必然至少有一部分感觉与人类相通，因为它们同样需要感知和操纵物质。即使它们拥有超出我们范围的感觉——例如电感觉、红外视觉或紫外视觉——我们仍然能够理解这些感觉。</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿博士不认为，为行星际殖民专门发展非人类生命形式是实际可行的。首先把它们提升到人类当前水平，本身就是过于庞大的任务。按照赫胥黎的看法，现存其他物种都不可能发展出人类级智力，因为它们在其他方向上都已经过度专门化。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" lang="en" hidden>
<p>DISCUSSION</p>
<p>Mr. R. A. SMITH proposed a vote of thanks to the speaker, saying that he was particularly grateful for the philosophical support he had given to the interplanetary enterprise. It was hard, however, to feel any kinship or sympathy with some of the weird types of “man” that Dr. Stapledon had invented for the colonisation of very large planets. To this Dr. Stapledon replied that he could accept any life-form, however alien, if it was orientated to the fundamental values — which he considered must hold for all races at all times.</p>
<p>Mr. A. C. CLARKE then formally opened the discussion, commenting first on some technical points. He thought that life — though not intelligent life — might be more common and more varied in the Solar System than generally imagined. Even ignoring the four giant planets (which seemed to be partly gaseous) there were fourteen bodies of over a million square miles, and at least two hundred asteroids of over ten thousand square miles area. We had plenty of exploring to do!</p>
<p>Dr. Stapledon had touched on the possibility of telepathic contact with other races: but presumably as far as the Solar System was concerned, some sort of clairvoyance would be required, since there would be no intelligence to “transmit” at the other end. Mr. Clarke had often been given vivid descriptions of Mars by mediumistic characters, and he hoped that, before the first expedition, such reports could be collected as it would provide a unique opportunity for a convincing proof. However, the Technical Committee would be most annoyed if, after it had worked for years on atomic spaceships, the clairvoyants got to Mars first.</p>
<p>Dr. Stapledon, Mr. Clarke thought, had somewhat over-estimated the — admittedly enormous — difficulties of <span class="t24">interstellar flight</span>. If highly efficient atomic step-rockets could be developed, speeds of over 90 per cent. of the velocity of light were theoretically possible. The nearest stars could thus be reached in five or ten years — though the elapsed time to the crew might be much less than this, thanks to relativity effects.</p>
<p>Mr. Clarke did not think that Man could ever take part in a Galactic culture, even assuming that the technical difficulties involved could be overcome. The human mind would be strained to the utmost to deal with a solar civilisation of even a dozen worlds. A <span class="t24">galactic</span> society implied an increase in complexity of perhaps a thousand million. However, it was foolish to worry about these ultimate questions: what we had to do now was to make the best of our own planet, and of the other worlds when we reached them.</p>
<p>Many members of the large audience then took part in the discussion, and the following are some of the points raised.</p>
<p>One speaker suggested that before human beings colonised Venus, its climate and atmosphere might be modified by suitable plant forms projected from Earth, adding that perhaps terrestrial life came originally from Mars in a similar manner.</p>
<p>A chemist decried loose talk of destroying the human race by atomic energy: the <span class="t24">daily</span> increase in population equalled the casualties produced by the Nagasaki bomb. Fissile materials were so rare that it seemed most unlikely that they could ever be used to alter climates or geographical features.</p>
<p>Another speaker wanted to know what would happen if we met a race at about the same level of culture. Which would pickle which? It was suggested that there would be insuperable difficulties in communication if we encountered beings whose senses were entirely different from our. There might be a total lack of understanding like that depicted in Wells’ <span class="t24">Country of the Blind</span>.</p>
<p>One member wanted to know if it might not be better to adapt <span class="t24">non-human</span> terrestrial life forms to colonise the planets, instead of trying to evolve new types of man.</p>
<p>A final speaker said that if we encountered inferior races, we must not put them in reservations, but must educate them. The selection of suitable colonists would also be a difficult psychological problem.</p>
<p>In reply, Dr. Stapledon said that though in this time of crisis we must put our own house in order, scientific endeavour must not be too utilitarian, but must always be opening up new frontiers. We must also preserve a sense of humility and piety towards the unknown: of late, humanity had been guilty of <span class="t24">hubris</span> — pride in achievement.</p>
<p>Dr. Stapledon did not agree that he had exaggerated the potentialities of atomic energy. Our present use of heavy elements was only a beginning — later we might release power from the lighter ones, as the stars do.</p>
<p>If we met a race that nearly matched us in culture, Dr. Stapledon hoped that co-operation would be possible, and that we would not treat it as we had treated the aborigines. On the other hand, if it was superior and turned Earth into a reservation, that would be the end of humanity, as it would lose the will to live. He agreed that we should try to educate other races — as long as we were sure we were educating them <span class="t24">up</span> and not <span class="t24">down</span> to our level.</p>
<p>He thought that races with <span class="t24">utterly</span> different senses were impossible. If they shared the physical universe with us, then they must have at least some senses in common with mankind, since they would have to perceive and manipulate matter. Even if they possessed senses outside our range (e.g. electrical, infra- or ultra-visual) we would still be able to comprehend them.</p>
<p>Dr. Stapledon did not think that it would be practicable to develop non-human life forms for interplanetary colonisation. It would be too great a task to bring them up to our level first. According to Huxley, no other existing species could develop human intelligence — they were all too specialised in other directions.</p>
</section>
