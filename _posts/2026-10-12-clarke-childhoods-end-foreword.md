<h2>FOREWORD</h2>
<p><em>Childhood’s End</em> was written between February and December 1952, then extensively revised in the spring of 1953. The first section was based on an earlier story, “Guardian Angel,” published in 1950, after a good deal of creative editing by James Blish.</p>
<p>I mention these dates to put the tale in historical perspective, as the great majority of today’s readers were not even born when the first Ballantine edition appeared on August 24, 1953. The first Earth satellite was still four years in the future—though not even the most optimistic space enthusiast dreamed it was that close; “around the turn of the century” was the best we had hoped for. If anyone had told me that, before the next decade had ended, I would be standing ten kilometers away from the first spaceship lifting off for the moon, I would have laughed at him.</p>
<p>Yet this book was already sixteen years old when Armstrong and Aldrin landed on the Sea of Tranquillity and the race between the United States and the USSR was conclusively won. In 1989, I updated the opening to bring the narrative forward into the next century; I was halfway through this task when, on the twentieth anniversary of the Apollo 11 landing, President Bush announced that Mars would be the next goal of the U.S. space program. It was a goal, unfortunately, that was soon forgotten.</p>
<p>For this edition, I have reverted to the original opening—but have also given the 1989 one as an appendix, partly for sentimental reasons. Five years ago, I was privileged to visit Star City—one of the first westerners to do so, thanks to the influence of my friend Cosmonaut Alexi Leonov. I stood in front of that famous statue and have been in Yuri Gagarin’s office, where the clock is stopped at the time of his death.</p>
<p>When this book was written in the early 1950s, I was still quite impressed by the evidence for what is generally called the paranormal and used it as a main theme of the story. Four decades later, after spending some millions of dollars of Yorkshire Television’s money researching my <em>Mysterious World</em> and <em>Strange Powers</em> programs, I became an almost total skeptic. I had seen far too many claims dissolve into thin air, far too many demonstrations exposed as fakes. It had been a long, and sometimes embarrassing, learning process.</p>
<p>When <em>Childhood’s End</em> first appeared, many readers were baffled by the opening disclaimer: “The opinions expressed in this book are not those of the author.” This was not entirely facetious; a year earlier, I had published <em>The Exploration of Space</em> and painted an optimistic picture of our future expansion into the universe. Now, I had written a book that said, “The stars are not for Man” and did not want anyone to think I had suddenly recanted.</p>
<p>Today, I would like to change the target of that disclaimer to cover 99 percent of the “paranormal” (it can’t all be nonsense—or can it?) and 100 percent of UFO “encounters.” I would be greatly distressed if this book contributed still further to the seduction of the gullible, now cynically exploited by all the media. Bookstores, newsstands, and airwaves are all polluted with mind-rotting bilge about UFOs, psychic powers, astrology, pyramid energies, channeling—you name it, someone is peddling it in a final outburst of fin-de-siècle decadence…</p>
<p>Does this mean that <em>Childhood’s End</em>—which deals both with the paranormal and visitors from space—no longer has any relevance? Not in the least; it’s a work of fiction, for heaven’s sake! We can still enjoy <em>The War of the Worlds</em>, despite the fact that the Martians never barbecued Woking in 1898—or, for that matter, New Jersey in 1938. And, as I’ve said more times than I can remember, I have little doubt that the universe is teeming with life. SETI (the Search for Extra-Terrestrial Intelligence) is now a fully accepted area of astronomy. The fact that it is still a science without a subject should be neither surprising nor disappointing. Only within half a single human lifetime have we possessed the technology to listen to the stars.</p>
<p>Soon after publication, <em>Childhood’s End</em> was optioned to the movies; since then it has passed through innumerable hands and been adapted by countless scriptwriters. For a while I collaborated with a refugee from the disgraceful McCarthy era—Howard Koch, best known for <em>Casablanca</em> and <em>The Fox</em>, which starred <em>2001</em>’s Keir Dullea! I was delighted when Howard sold, for a nice six-figure sum, the notorious radio script with which he and Orson Welles terrorized the United States (see previous paragraph).</p>
<p>According to the latest information from the Hollywood gulags, the current asking price for <em>Childhood’s End</em> is several hundred times that of the perfectly satisfactory fee I received in 1956. And even if it never makes it to the big screen, millions of people have watched a very impressive version of the opening in the box office hit <em>Independence Day</em>.</p>
<p>So when/if <em>Childhood’s End</em> is finally made into a movie, the popcorn set will undoubtedly think we’ve ripped off <em>I.D.</em> However, Theodore Sturgeon copyrighted this situation long ago. Back in 1947—yes, ’47!—he wrote a story with the unforgettable title, and last line: “The sky was full of ships.”</p>
<p>Half a decade before Ted’s story, I actually witnessed such a scene myself. No, I’ve not suddenly gone crazy through writing too much science fiction…</p>
<p>It was on a beautiful summer evening in 1941, when I was being driven up to London by my closest friend, the late Arthur Valentine Cleaver—chief engineer of the Rolls-Royce Rocket Division and, like me, an enthusiastic member of the British Interplanetary Society.</p>
<p>The sun was setting behind us, and the city was twenty miles ahead. We came over the crest of a hill, and there was a sight so incredible that Val brought the car to a halt. It was both beautiful and awe inspiring, but no future generations will ever see it again: Technology has bypassed it, for better or for worse.</p>
<p>Scores—hundreds—of gleaming silver barrage balloons were anchored in the sky above London. As their stubby torpedo shapes caught the last rays of the sun, it did indeed seem that a fleet of alien spaceships was poised above the city. For a long moment, we dreamed of the distant future and banished all thoughts of the present peril that the aerial fence had been erected to guard the city against.</p>
<p>In that instant, perhaps, <em>Childhood’s End</em> was conceived.</p>
<p>Arthur C. Clarke<br/>
Colombo, Sri Lanka<br/>
June 17, 2000</p>
