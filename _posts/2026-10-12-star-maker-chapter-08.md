<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="zh" lang="zh-CN" aria-label="中文译文">
<p>我和布瓦尔图，与越来越多加入我们的探索者一道，访问了形形色色的许多世界。有些世界里，我们按当地时间只停留几周；另一些世界则一待就是几个世纪，或者完全依兴趣而定，从一个历史节点跳到另一个历史节点。我们像一群蝗虫一样降落到新发现的世界，每个人各自挑选一个合适的宿主。经过或长或短的一段观察之后，我们就离开，也许过些时候又在这个世界的另一个时代再次落下；也可能把整个队伍分散到许多世界，彼此在时间和空间上都相隔极远。</p>
<p>这种奇特生活把我变成了一个与从前完全不同的存在，早已不再只是那个在人类历史某一天夜里独自走上山丘的英国人。不仅我的直接经验已经远远超出正常寿命所能容纳的范围，而且通过与同伴之间一种异常亲密的结合，我本人也可以说被“倍增”了。因为从某种意义上说，此时的我既是那个英国人，也同样是布瓦尔图，还是我们每一位同伴。</p>
<p>发生在我们身上的这种变化值得仔细说明。它不仅本身很有意思，而且后来还成为一把钥匙，使我们能够理解许多宇宙级生命；若没有这把钥匙，它们的本性对我们原本会一直晦暗不明。</p>
<p>在新的状态下，我们的共同体已经完善到这样一种程度：每一个人的经验都可以为所有人共享。因此，新的“我”能够同样轻易地参与那个英国人的经历、布瓦尔图的经历，以及其余所有人的经历。我也拥有他们在各自故乡世界、彼此仍然分离存在时的全部记忆。</p>
<p>也许有哲学兴趣的读者会问：“你的意思是，许多个具有经验的个体最后变成了一个个体，只剩一条单一的经验之流？还是说，仍然存在许多个具有经验的个体，只不过它们各自拥有数量上彼此不同、内容却完全相同的经验？”我的回答是：我不知道。但有一点我知道。作为那个英国人的我，以及我的每一位同伴，都逐渐“醒来”，开始拥有彼此的经验，同时也进入一种更加清明的智能状态。至于作为经验主体的我们究竟仍然是许多个，还是已经成为一个，我不知道。我甚至怀疑，这个问题属于那种永远无法真正回答的问题，因为追究到底，它本身也许就没有意义。</p>
<p>在我们以共同体身份观察无数世界时，也同样在我向内观察自身共同体心智活动时，注意力会轮流以不同成员为主要工具。有时是某一个探索者，有时又换成另一个，有时甚至是某一群探索者；他们各自特殊的本性与经历，会成为整个共同体进行沉思的材料。有些时候，我们格外警觉而充满渴望，于是每个人都会醒入一种更加清明的知觉、思想、想象和意志模式，其清晰程度超过我们任何一个人在孤立个体状态下所曾体验过的一切。因此，尽管我们每一个人在某种意义上都与朋友们彼此同一，他同时又成为一个高于任何单独成员的心灵。不过这种“醒来”，在性质上似乎并没有比普通生活中许多经验更神秘：比如心灵忽然欣喜地把此前彼此隔绝的经验联系起来，或者在原本混乱的事物中突然发现从未注意到的图样与意义。</p>
<p>绝不能以为这种奇特的精神共同体抹去了每一位探索者的人格。人类语言里没有准确术语可以描述我们的特殊关系。说我们已经失去个体性，或者溶解进一个共同体个体之中，并不比说我们始终只是彼此分立的个体更正确。此时，“我”这个代词适用于我们全体的集合，可“我们”这个代词也同样适用。在一个方面，也就是意识的统一性上，我们确实是一个单一的经验主体；但与此同时，我们又以一种非常重要、而且令人愉悦的方式彼此有别。虽然只有一个共同的“我”，却也可以说存在一个多重而斑斓的“我们”：一群彼此非常不同的人格作为对象被观察着，每一个都以创造性的方式，为整个宇宙探索事业贡献自己不可替代的部分；同时所有人又被一张细密的人际关系网络联结在一起。我很清楚，这种说明在读者看来必定自相矛盾；事实上，对如今的我自己来说也是如此。但我找不到别的方法来表达那段仍然鲜明的记忆：我既是共同体中一个特定成员，又同时拥有整个共同体汇集起来的全部经验。</p>
<p>换一种说法：就意识的同一性而言，我们是一个个体；就各自不同、而且具有创造力的独特性而言，我们又是由共同“我”所观察到的不同人格。每一个人作为共同“我”，都会把全部成员——包括自己的个体自我——体验为一群真实的人，他们在气质和私人经历上各不相同。我们每个人都把全体体验为一个真正的共同体，其中有诸如我与布瓦尔图之间那样的感情关系和相互批评。然而，在另一个经验层次，也就是创造性思想与想象的层次上，单一的共同注意力又可以暂时退出这张人格关系网络，把全部精力都投入宇宙探索。若只说一部分真话，可以这样概括：在爱之中，我们彼此有别；而在知识、智慧和崇拜之中，我们则彼此同一。下面几章讲述的，是这个共同“我”的宇宙经验。从逻辑上说，探索心灵始终应该用单数来指称，用“我”这个代词，只需说“我做了什么”“我怎样想”；但我仍会大体使用“我们”，以保留这确实是一项共同事业的真实印象，也避免给人一种错误感觉，仿佛探索者仅仅就是本书这个人类作者。</p>
<p>我们每一个人都曾经在许多世界中的某一个，过着属于自己的活跃个体生活。对于每个人来说，故乡世界里那段小小的、磕磕绊绊的一生，都始终保留着一种特殊的具体感和光辉，就像成年人回忆童年时那种格外鲜明的感觉。不仅如此，以个体身份回望过去时，他还会赋予自己那段私人生活一种紧迫性和重要性；而以共同体身份来看时，这种重要性却会被更宏大的宇宙事务完全压过。然而，恰恰是每一段小小私人生命所具有的这种具体感、光辉、紧迫性和重要性，对我们共同参与的那个共同“我”意义重大。它把自身的鲜明与哀感投射到共同经验之中。因为只有在自己的故乡世界真正生活时，我们每个人才曾经像生命战争中的一名普通士兵那样，近距离同敌人扭打。正是对那个受束缚、被囚禁、蒙着眼睛却又满怀热望的私人个体的回忆，使我们观察宇宙事件展开时，不只是把它看作一场景观，还能感到每一个个体生命在闪现又消逝时所具有的刺痛感。于是，作为英国人的我，为共同心灵贡献了自己始终鲜明的记忆——记得我在那个动荡世界里一切无能为力的行为。而那种盲目人类生活真正的意义，那颗由一点微小而不完美的共同体经验所赎回的宝石，对于作为共同“我”的我来说，曾经显现得无比清楚；那个仍处于原始昏睡中的英国人在当时绝不可能达到这种清明，如今也再无法恢复。我现在唯一还记得的是：以共同“我”的身份回顾自己在地球上的人生时，我对它既比个体状态下更加严厉批判，却又更少感到罪责；而看待那段人生中的伴侣时，我既能更加清醒、冷静地理解我们彼此造成的影响，也怀有更加宽厚的爱。</p>
<p>关于探索者的共同经验，还有一个方面必须提及。我们每个人最初踏上这场伟大冒险，主要都希望发现“共同体”在整个宇宙中究竟扮演什么角色。这个问题仍然没有答案；然而与此同时，另一个问题却越来越顽固地逼近我们。我们在许多世界中经历得太多，而心灵又获得了新的清明，于是每个人内部都产生一种尖锐的理智与感情冲突。从理智上说，一个独立于宇宙本身的“神祇”创造了宇宙，这种观念在我们看来越来越难以置信。理智上我们毫不怀疑：宇宙自身足以成立，是一个既不需要逻辑上的外部根据，也不需要创造者的体系。然而与此同时，我们越来越强烈地感到另一件事。一个人可以透过被感官直接看见的爱人或敌人，感受到对方作为心灵的真实存在；与此类似，我们也在宇宙的物质存在中，感受到那个被我们称为“造星主”的精神存在。无论理智怎么说，我们都知道整个宇宙远远小于“存在”的全部，而无限的存在整体潜伏在宇宙的每一个瞬间之下。我们怀着不讲理的激情，不断试图从宇宙每一个最微小、最具体的事件背后窥看那种无限本身的面貌；因为没有更准确的名字，我们才把它叫作造星主。然而无论怎样窥探，我们什么都看不到。尽管在整体宇宙、也在每一件具体事物中，那令人敬畏的存在都无可怀疑地与我们相对，但恰恰因为它是无限的，我们无法赋予它任何确定特征。</p>
<p>有时，我们倾向于把它想象成纯粹的力量，并用许多世界里数不清的力量神祇作为象征。有时，我们又确信它就是纯粹理性，而整个宇宙不过是那位神圣数学家进行的一次演算。有时，爱似乎才是它最根本的性质，于是我们借用所有世界中全部“基督”的形象来想象它：人类的基督、棘皮人的基督、鹦鹉螺人的基督、共生种族的双重基督、昆虫种族那由无数个体汇成的群体基督。但同样经常地，它又像一种毫无理性的创造力，既盲目又精巧，既温柔又残酷，仿佛唯一关心的只是不断生育、不断生育出无限多样的生命；在一千件空洞作品中，它偶尔创造出一件脆弱的美丽之物。它也许会暂时像母亲一样细心呵护自己的作品，却又会突然因嫉妒这个造物本身的卓越，而把自己创造出来的一切毁掉。</p>
<p>然而我们很清楚，所有这些想象都严重失真。造星主那种被直接感受到的存在仍然不可理解，尽管它越来越照亮整个宇宙，就像黎明时尚未升起的太阳，其光辉已经先一步铺满天空。</p>
</section>

<h3>VIII. CONCERNING THE EXPLORERS</h3>
<p>BVALLTU AND I, in company with the increasing band of our fellow explorers, visited many worlds of many strange kinds. In some we spent only a few weeks of the local time; in others we remained for centuries, or skimmed from point to point of history as our interest dictated. Like a swarm of locusts we would descend upon a new-found world, each of us singling out a suitable host. After a period of observation, long or short, we would leave, to alight again, perhaps, on the same world in another of its ages; or to distribute our company among many worlds, far apart in time and in space.</p>
<p>This strange life turned me into a very different being from the Englishman who had at a certain date of human history walked at night upon a hill. Not only had my own immediate experience increased far beyond the normal range, but also, by means of a peculiarly intimate union with my fellows, I myself had been, so to speak, multiplied. For in a sense I was now as much Bvalltu and each one of my colleagues as I was that Englishman.</p>
<p>This change that had come over us deserves to be carefully described, not merely for its intrinsic interest, but also because it afforded us a key for understanding many cosmical beings whose nature would otherwise have been obscure to us.</p>
<p>In our new condition our community was so perfected that the experiences of each were available to all. Thus I, the new I, participated in the adventures of that Englishman and of Bvalltu and the rest with equal ease. And I possessed all their memories of former, separate existence in their respective native worlds.</p>
<p>Some philosophically minded reader may ask, ‘Do you mean that the many experiencing individuals became a single individual, having a single stream of experience? Or do you mean that there remained many experiencing individuals, having numerically distinct but exactly similar experiences?’ The answer is that I do not know. But this I know. I, the Englishman, and similarly each of my colleagues, gradually ‘woke’ into possession of each other’s experience, and also into more lucid intelligence. Whether, as experients, we remained many or became one, I do not know. But I suspect that the question is one of those which can never be truly answered because in the last analysis it is meaningless.</p>
<p>In the course of my communal observation of the many worlds, and equally in the course of my introspection of my own communal mental processes, now one and now another individual explorer, and now perhaps a group of explorers, would form the main instrument of attention, affording through their particular nature and experience material for the contemplation of all. Sometimes, when we were exceptionally alert and eager, each awakened into a mode of perception and thought and imagination and will more lucid than any experiencing known to any of us as individuals. Thus, though each of us became in a sense identical with each of his friends, he also became in a manner a mind of higher order than any of us in isolation. But in this ‘waking’ there seemed to be nothing more mysterious in kind than in those many occasions in normal life when the mind delightedly relates together experiences that have hitherto been insulated from one another, or discovers in confused objects a pattern or a significance hitherto unnoticed.</p>
<p>It must not be supposed that this strange mental community blotted out the personalities of the individual explorers. Human speech has no accurate terms to describe our peculiar relationship. It would be as untrue to say that we had lost our individuality, or were dissolved in a communal individuality, as to say that we were all the while distinct individuals. Though the pronoun ‘I’ now applied to us all collectively, the pronoun ‘we’ also applied to us. In one respect, namely unity of consciousness, we were indeed a single experiencing individual; yet at the same time we were in a very important and delightful manner distinct from one another. Though there was only the single, communal ‘I’, there was also, so to speak, a manifold and variegated ‘us’, an observed company of very diverse personalities, each of whom expressed creatively his own unique contribution to the whole enterprise of cosmical exploration, while all were bound together in a tissue of subtle personal relationships.</p>
<p>I am well aware that this account of the matter must seem to my readers self-contradictory, as indeed it does to me. But I can find no other way of expressing the vividly remembered fact that I was at once a particular member of a community and the possessor of the pooled experience of that community.</p>
<p>To put the matter somewhat differently, though in respect of our identity of awareness we were a single individual, in respect of our diverse and creative idiosyncrasies we were distinct persons observable by the common ‘I’. Each one, as the common ‘I’, experienced the whole company of individuals, including his individual self, as a group of actual persons, differing in temperament and private experience. Each one of us experienced all as a real community, bound together by such relations of affection and mutual criticism as occurred, for instance, between Bvalltu and myself. Yet on another plane of experience, the plane of creative thought and imagination, the single communal attention could withdraw from this tissue of personal relationships. Instead, it concerned itself wholly with the exploration of the cosmos. With partial truth it might be said that, while for love we were distinct, for knowledge, for wisdom, and for worship we were identical. In the following chapters, which deal with the cosmical, experiences of this communal ‘I’, it would be logically correct to refer to the exploring mind always in the singular, using the pronoun ‘I’, and saying simply, ‘I did so and so, and thought so and so’; nevertheless the pronoun ‘we’ will still be generally employed so as to preserve the true impression of a communal enterprise, and to avoid the false impression that the explorer was just the human author of this book.</p>
<p>Each one of us had lived his individual active life in one or other of the many worlds. And for each one, individually, his own little blundering career in his remote native world retained a peculiar concreteness and glamour, like the vividness which mature men find in childhood memories. Not only so, but individually he imputed to his former private life an urgency and importance which, in his communal capacity, was overwhelmed by matters of greater cosmical significance. Now this concreteness and glamour, this urgency and importance of each little private life, was of great moment to the communal ‘I’ in which each of us participated. It irradiated the communal experience with its vividness, its pathos. For only in his own life as a native in some world had each of us actually fought, so to speak, in life’s war as a private soldier at close grips with the enemy. It was the recollection of this fettered, imprisoned, blindfold, eager, private individuality, that enabled us to watch the unfolding of cosmical events not merely as a spectacle but with a sense of the poignancy of every individual life as it flashed and vanished.</p>
<p>Thus I, the Englishman, contributed to the communal mind my persistently vivid recollections of all my ineffectual conduct in my own troubled world; and the true significance of that blind human life, redeemed by its little imperfect jewel of community, became apparent to me, the communal ‘I’, with a lucidity which the Englishman in his primeval stupor could never attain and cannot now recapture. All that I can now remember is that, as the communal ‘I’, I looked on my terrestrial career at once more critically and with less guilt than I do in the individual state; and on my partner in that career at once with clearer, colder understanding of our mutual impact and with more generous affection.</p>
<p>One aspect of the communal experience of the explorers I have still to mention. Each of us had originally set out upon the great adventure mainly in the hope of discovering what part was played by community in the cosmos as a whole. This question had yet to be answered; but meanwhile another question was becoming increasingly insistent. Our crowded experiences in the many worlds, and our new lucidity of mind, had bred in each of us a sharp conflict of intellect and feeling. Intellectually the idea that some ‘deity’, distinct from the cosmos itself, had made the cosmos now seemed to us less and less credible. Intellectually we had no doubt that the cosmos was self-sufficient, a system involving no logical ground and no creator. Yet increasingly, as a man may feel the psychical reality of a physically perceived beloved or a perceived enemy, we felt in the physical presence of the cosmos the psychical presence of that which we had named the Star Maker. In spite of intellect, we knew that the whole cosmos was infinitely less than the whole of being, and that the whole infinity of being underlay every moment of the cosmos. And with unreasoning passion we strove constantly to peer behind each minute particular event in the cosmos to see the very features of that infinity which, for lack of a truer name, we had called the Star Maker. But, peer as we might, we found nothing. Though in the whole and in each particular thing the dread presence indubitably confronted us, its very infinity prevented us from assigning to it any features whatever.</p>
<p>Sometimes we inclined to conceive it as sheer Power, and symbolized it to ourselves by means of all the myriad power-deities of our many worlds. Sometimes we felt assured that it was pure Reason, and that the cosmos was but an exercise of the divine mathematician. Sometimes Love seemed to us its essential character, and we imagined it with the forms of all the Christs of all the worlds, the human Christs, the Echinoderm and Nautiloid Christs, the dual Christ of the Symbiotics, the swarming Christ of the Insectoids. But equally it appeared to us as unreasoning Creativity, at once blind and subtle, tender and cruel, caring only to spawn and spawn the infinite variety of beings, conceiving here and there among a thousand inanities a fragile loveliness. This it might for a while foster with maternal solicitude, till in a sudden jealousy of the excellence of its own creature, it would destroy what it had made.</p>
<p>But we knew well that all these fictions were very false. The felt presence of the Star Maker remained unintelligible, even though it increasingly illuminated the cosmos, like the splendour of the unseen sun at dawn.</p>
