<h3>三、如果其他行星无人居住</h3>
<p>如果人类发现其他行星无人居住，应该拿它们怎么办？如果人类仍然四分五裂，毫无疑问，各个帝国主义势力会竞相吞并这些辽阔的处女地。未来美国与亚洲之间、以欧洲为战场的斗争，很可能一路蔓延到火星。据说，我们大西洋彼岸那些精力旺盛却在文化上仍处青春期的表亲中，已经有人提议尽快吞并月球，把它变成美国开发利用的工业区。唉！难道第一面插在地球疆界之外的旗帜，竟必须是星条旗，而不是统一人类的旗帜吗？</p>
<p>顺便提醒自己：能够环绕一颗行星飞行，并不必然意味着就能在上面着陆和行走，更别说长期停留，进行任何形式的勘察或工业活动。显然，先驱者不仅要携带旅途所需的食物、水和空气，还必须带上加压服、氧气以及在行星表面停留期间所需的大量水。在当前世界格局下，他们甚至还可能需要武器，用来抵御来自地球敌对国家的使者。</p>
<p>不过，让我们假设人类终于在政治和社会上实现了有效统一。那么，一个统一的人类社会应该怎样对待其他行星？</p>
<p>显然，第一件事是探索。单纯的科学好奇心就足以要求我们对它们进行彻底勘察。它们会产生一种诱惑，就像十九世纪的“黑暗非洲”、南北极以及尚未登顶的喜马拉雅山对人类产生的诱惑一样。勇敢的年轻人会非常乐意投身行星探索。他们真正的动机也许只是冒险本身，虽然这种昂贵而危险事业的理性正当性当然是推进科学。不过也可以设想：关于这些荒凉世界的一切真正重要的知识，也许只需环绕飞行而不必登陆便能获得。人类或许会太沉浸于科学的一般进步，以及在地球上创造真正成熟人类社会这一令人兴奋的事业，以至于根本懒得费那些力气踏上另一颗行星。这个可能性当然不大，因为开辟未知世界那种非理性的浪漫魅力实在太强，哪怕那些世界最后只是荒凉、冷酷、毫不宜人的废土。</p>
<p>除纯粹好奇与冒险之外，探索行星最显而易见的动机，是希望发现巨大的自然资源宝库，并利用它们造福人类。那里也许蕴藏大量铀或其他原子能来源，也可能富含人类需要的稀有元素或矿物。我在这个领域完全没有资格发言，只能指出：经济利益很可能在人类处理其他行星时扮演主要角色。</p>
<p>不过我们应当承认，人类最好把对异星的开发推迟到认真改善地球环境之后。拥有高度发展的科学知识和原子能之后，人类首先应当完成那个相对容易的任务：把自己的母星改造成一个更便利、更宜人的家园。我们可以改善气候，改变海岸线，灌溉沙漠，驯服丛林，把碍事的山脉炸开；可以从地球深处开采矿藏，使南极洲和北极的大岛变暖并在那里殖民，等等。但愿这些巨型工程都不要在人类获得更高程度的智慧、并且清楚知道怎样的世界才真正有利于人的发展之前贸然开始。就我个人而言，因为天性中多少有点野人气质，我还希望能够保留许多地区的荒野状态，让我们这个毕竟在生物学上适应于原始环境的物种，有地方休憩和恢复精神。</p>
<p>不过，不管重建地球表面多么值得去做，一个拥有科学和力量的统一人类社会迟早还是会把注意力投向其他行星，不仅为了经济开发，也为了寻找新的家园。最有希望的大概是火星。如果这项事业确实值得投入，那么那个小而寒冷、干旱的世界至少可以被改造成宜居之地，即使称不上人类的天堂。所需的一切材料都存在于火星地壳本身。借助原子能，人类的巧思应该能够增加大气与水的供应，灌溉荒漠表面，培育合适的植被，甚至提高地表温度。这项巨型工程是否真的值得，我稍后再讨论。现在我只提出一点：它大概可以做到。闲来胡思乱想时，人会觉得火星或许可以当作一个地外“西伯利亚”，把所有真正麻烦的人都流放过去。不过我担心，这颗小行星很快就会人满为患，而地球反倒被掏空人口。</p>
<p>像火星一样，月球也许可以被改造成一个让地球人勉强痛苦地生存的地方；不过在这种情况下，人工制造的大气想必会逃逸得快得多，必须持续补充。顺便说一句，其中相当一部分还会被地球吸走，从而增加地球的大气压，使我们自己的问题更复杂。</p>
<p>那么金星呢？任务大概会艰巨得多。根据我们对金星环境那一点点了解，第一个问题似乎是改变现有大气的组成；按目前所知，这种大气完全不适合地球生命。如果那里确实没有水，还必须大规模制造水。然后，我们还得制造植被，以维持游离氧的供应。不过总体来说，虽然金星比火星更难处理，长远看却可能成为更令人满意的人类家园。它大得多，当然也温暖得多。它未必只给殖民者提供一种痛苦而岌岌可危的生存状态；经过足够时间，金星甚至可能在作为智慧生命家园这一点上媲美乃至超过地球。</p>
<p>至于那些巨行星，由于大气不利、距离太阳遥远而寒冷，再加上强大到足以让人体成为无法承受负担的重力，它们看来没有任何供现有人类殖民的可能。</p>

<h3><a id="a1085"></a>III. If the Planets are Uninhabited</h3>
<p><span class="t28">I</span><span class="t31">F</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">MAN</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">FINDS</span><span class="t30"> </span>the planets uninhabited, what should he do with them? If mankind is still disunited, no doubt there will be a race between rival imperialisms to annex those vast virgin territories. The coming struggle between America and Asia, with Europe as a battlefield, might well spread to Mars. Already one of our vigorous but still culturally adolescent cousins across the Atlantic is reported to have suggested that the moon should be annexed as soon as possible as an industrial field for American exploitation. Alas! Must the first flag to be planted beyond the earth’s confines be the Stars and Stripes, and not the banner of a united Humanity?</p>
<p>In passing, let us remind ourselves that merely to circumnavigate a planet does not necessarily imply the possibility of landing on it and walking about, let alone staying there and undertaking any sort of survey or industrial operation. Clearly the pioneers would have to be equipped not only with food and water and air for their journey but with pressure-suits, oxygen and an ample water supply for their stay on the planet. And as things stand, they might also need weapons to defend themselves from attack by emissaries from rival terrestrial states.</p>
<p>But let us suppose that mankind has at last become effectively united, both politically and socially. Then what should a united mankind do with the planets?</p>
<p>Obviously, the first thing to do would be to explore them. Sheer scientific curiosity would certainly insist on thoroughly surveying them. They would offer the kind of lure that was offered in the nineteenth century by Darkest Africa, the north and south poles and the unclimbed Himalayas. Bold young people would be very ready to give their services for planetary exploration. Their effective motive would probably be sheer adventure, though the rational justification of such costly and dangerous undertakings would of course be the advance of science. It is conceivable, however, that everything really significant about those desert worlds might be learned by merely circumnavigating them without landing on them. Perhaps men will be so absorbed in the general advance of science and in the exciting task of creating a really adult human society on earth, that they will simply never bother to take the necessary trouble to set foot on another planet. No doubt this is unlikely, because the irrational, romantic glamour of opening up unexplored worlds will be too strong, even if those worlds turn out to be inhospitable and dreary wastes.</p>
<p>Apart from the motives of sheer curiosity and sheer adventure, the obvious motive for exploring the planets is the hope of discovering immense fields of natural resources, and exploiting them for human welfare. They might, for instance, yield valuable stores of uranium or other sources of atomic power, or any of the rarer elements or minerals needed by man. I am quite incompetent in this field, and must merely note that the motive of economic gain may play a leading part in man’s dealings with the planets.</p>
<p>Let us admit, however, that it would be far best for man to postpone his exploitation of alien planets until he had concentrated seriously on improving terrestrial conditions. Equipped with highly developed scientific knowledge and atomic power, he should first undertake the comparatively easy task of turning his native planet into a more convenient and more delectable home. Climates might be improved, coastlines modified, deserts irrigated, jungles tamed, mountains (where they happened to be obstructive) blasted out of the way. Mineral wealth might be brought up from the depths of the terrestrial globe, Antarctica and the great Arctic Islands might be warmed and colonized. And so on. But let us hope that none of these vast enterprises will be attempted till mankind has attained a rather higher level of wisdom, and has a clear knowledge of the kind of world that would really favour human development. For my part, since I am by nature something of a savage, I cannot help hoping also that many regions will be preserved in their wild state for the recreation and refreshment of a species which, after all, is biologically adapted to a primitive environment.</p>
<p>But however desirable this reconstruction of the earth’s surface, sooner or later for good or ill, a united mankind, equipped with science and power, will probably turn its attention to the other planets, not only for economic exploitation, but also as possible homes for man. Perhaps the most promising is Mars. If the venture seemed really worth while, that small cold, arid world might be rendered at least habitable, if not a paradise for man. All the necessary materials would be present in the crust of the planet itself. Human ingenuity, with atomic power, should be able to increase the atmosphere and the water supply, irrigate the desert surface, produce a suitable vegetation, and even raise the surface temperature. Whether this huge undertaking would be in fact worth while or not, is a question which I shall consider later. At present I suggest merely that it probably could be accomplished. In frivolous moments one feels that Mars might be used as an extra-terrestrial “Siberia” in which to exile all our really tiresome people. But I fear that the little planet would soon be overcrowded, and Earth depopulated.</p>
<p>Like Mars, the moon could perhaps be rendered distressfully habitable by terrestrial man; though in this case, presumably, the artificial atmosphere would escape far more rapidly, and would need to be constantly replenished. Incidentally, much of it would be drawn off by the Earth, thus complicating our terrestrial problems by increasing our atmospheric pressure.</p>
<p>What of Venus? The task would probably be much more formidable. From the little that we know of Venerian conditions, it would seem that the first problem would be to alter the composition of the already existing atmosphere, which, so far as is now known, is quite unsuited to terrestrial life. And water, if indeed it is absent, would have to be created in bulk. Then we should have to produce a vegetation for the maintenance of a supply of free oxygen. But on the whole, though Venus offers a more difficult problem than Mars, it might in the long run become a more satisfactory home for man. It is much larger, and of course far warmer. Instead of affording the human colonists a distressful and precarious existence, it might in time rival and surpass the Earth as a home for intelligent beings.</p>
<p>The greater planets would seem to offer no possibility of human colonization, owing to unfavourable atmosphere, chilly remoteness from the sun, and such gravitational pressure that a man’s body would be an insupportable burden.</p>
