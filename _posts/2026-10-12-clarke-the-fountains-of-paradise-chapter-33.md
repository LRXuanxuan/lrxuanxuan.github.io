<h2 id="c33">33. CORA</h2>
<p>“<em>Why</em> did you leave it until now?” Dr. Sen had asked, in a tone appropriate for a retarded child.</p>
<p>“The usual reason,” Morgan answered as he ran his good thumb along the seal of his shirt. “I was too busy—and whenever I felt short of breath, I blamed it on the height.”</p>
<p>“Altitude was partly to blame, of course. You’d better check all your people on the mountain. How could you have overlooked anything so obvious?”</p>
<p>How indeed? thought Morgan, with some embarrassment.</p>
<p>“All those monks—some of them were over eighty! They seemed so healthy that it never occurred to me…”</p>
<p>“The monks have lived up there for years. They’re completely adapted. But <em>you’ve</em> been hopping up and down several times a day—”</p>
<p>“Twice, at the most.”</p>
<p>“—going from sea level to half an atmosphere in a few minutes. Well, there’s no great harm done—if you follow instructions from now on. Mine, and CORA’s.”</p>
<p>“CORA’s?”</p>
<p>“Coronary alarm.”</p>
<p>“Oh—one of those things.”</p>
<p>“Yes—one of <em>those</em> things. They save about ten million lives a year. Mostly top civil servants, senior administrators, distinguished scientists, leading engineers, and similar nitwits. I often wonder if it’s worth the trouble. Nature may be trying to tell us something, and we’re not listening.”</p>
<p>“Remember your Hippocratic Oath, Bill,” retorted Morgan with a grin. “And you must admit that I’ve always done just what you told me. Why, my weight hasn’t changed a kilo in the last ten years.”</p>
<p>“Um—well, you’re not the worst of my patients,” said the slightly mollified doctor. He fumbled around in his desk, and produced a large holopad. “Take your choice—here are the standard models. Any color you like as long as it’s medic red.”</p>
<p>Morgan triggered the images, and regarded them with distaste.</p>
<p>“Where do I have to carry the thing?” he asked. “Or do you want to implant it?”</p>
<p>“That isn’t necessary, at least for the present. In five years, maybe, but perhaps not even then. I suggest you start with <em>this</em> model. It’s worn just under the breastbone, so doesn’t need remote sensors. After a while, you won’t notice it’s there. And it won’t bother you unless it’s needed.”</p>
<p>“And then?”</p>
<p>“Listen.”</p>
<p>The doctor threw one of the numerous switches on his desk console, and a sweet mezzo-soprano voice remarked in a conversational tone: “I think you should sit down and rest for about ten minutes.” After a brief pause, it continued: “It would be a good idea to lie down for half an hour.” Another pause. “As soon as convenient, make an appointment with Dr. Smith.” Then:</p>
<p>“Please take one of the red pills immediately.”</p>
<p>“I have called the ambulance. Just lie down and relax. Everything will be all right.”</p>
<p>Morgan almost clapped his hands over his ears to cut out the piercing whistle.</p>
<p>“THIS IS A CORA EMERGENCY! WILL ANYONE WITHIN RANGE OF MY VOICE PLEASE COME IMMEDIATELY. THIS IS A CORA EMERGENCY! WILL—”</p>
<p>“I think you get the general idea,” said the doctor, restoring silence to his office. “Of course, the programs and responses are individually tailored to the subject. And there’s a wide range of voices, including some quite famous ones.”</p>
<p><em>“That</em> will do nicely. When will my unit be ready?”</p>
<p>“I’ll call you in about three days. Oh yes—there’s an advantage of the chest-worn units I should mention.”</p>
<p>“What’s that?”</p>
<p>“One of my patients is a keen tennis player. He tells me that when he opens his shirt, the sight of that little red disk has an absolutely devastating effect on his opponent’s game….”</p>
