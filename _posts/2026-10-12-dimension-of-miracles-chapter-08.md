<h2>第八章</h2>
<p>“依我看，”卡莫迪说，“你这个问题的解决办法——可能——可能是——”</p>
<p>“是什么？”梅利克隆急切地问。</p>
<p>卡莫迪根本不知道自己接下来要说什么。他只是绝望地希望：说话这一行为本身能自动产生意义；毕竟，词语有意义，而句子拥有的意义还比词语更多。</p>
<p>“你的问题，”卡莫迪继续说道，“在于要从你自身内部找到一种内住的功能主义，使其能够指涉某种外部现实。然而这种探索也许本身就是不可能的，因为你自己就是现实，因此你无法把自己设定在自己之外。”</p>
<p>“我想设就能设，”梅利克隆闹别扭似的说。“这里归我管，我爱设定什么鬼东西就设定什么。你要知道，当上帝并不意味着‘吾’就非得是唯我论者。”</p>
<p>“对，对，对，”卡莫迪飞快地说。（他还剩七分钟？还是六分钟？而时间到了以后究竟会发生什么？）“所以很显然，你的‘内在性’与‘内住性’，在你对自己的理解中是不充分的；而既然你自己以‘定义者’的形态判定这些属性不充分，那么从事实意义上说，它们也就确实不充分。”</p>
<p>“推得漂亮，”梅利克隆说。“你本来应该去当神学家。”</p>
<p>“此时此刻我就是神学家。”卡莫迪说。（六分钟？五分钟？）“那么很好，你究竟该做什么？……你有没有考虑过，把所有知识——内部知识和外部知识都包括在内，假定外部知识这种东西确实存在——作为自己的追求？”</p>
<p>“考虑过。事实上，我确实想过这个办法。”梅利克隆说。“我干过的事包括：读完银河里每一本书，探究自然与人的秘密，考察宏观宇宙和微观宇宙，诸如此类。顺便说，我在学习方面相当有天分；虽然后来忘掉了一些东西，比如生命的秘密，以及死亡不可告人的真正动机。不过只要我愿意，随时都能重新学会。我确实学到了一点：学习是一桩枯燥而被动的事业，虽然里面偶尔也有些愉快的惊喜；我还学到，学习对于我并没有什么特殊而独有的重要性。事实上，我发现‘忘却所学’差不多也一样有趣。”</p>
<p>“也许你注定应该成为艺术家。”卡莫迪建议。</p>
<p>“那个阶段我也经历过，”梅利克隆说。“我用肉体和黏土雕塑；我把日落画在画布上，也画在天空里；我用文字写书，也用事件写另一些书；我用乐器制造音乐，还为风和雨谱写交响曲。我想我的作品已经足够好；可我不知道为什么，总明白自己永远只会是个业余爱好者。你看，我的全能不给我留下足够犯错的空间；而我对现实本身掌握得太完整，也就很难认真对待‘再现现实’这件事。”</p>
<p>“唔，我明白了。”卡莫迪说。（肯定最多只剩三分钟了！）“为什么不当征服者？”</p>
<p>“我没有必要征服已经属于我的东西，”梅利克隆说。“至于其他世界，我也不想要。我的属性只适用于我自己的环境，而我的环境就是这一颗行星。占有其他世界，会迫使我采取违背自身本性的行动。再说了——连这一颗世界我都不知道该拿它怎么办，要别的世界又有什么用？”</p>
<p>“看来你确实把这件事想得非常透彻。”卡莫迪说；他的绝望正在从急迫变成彻底的灰心。</p>
<p>“当然。我几百万年来几乎没想过别的。我一直寻找某种在我之外、却又对我的存在本性必不可少的目的。我寻找过一道指令；可最后找到的始终只有我自己。”</p>
<p>如果卡莫迪自己的处境没那么危急，他也许会为上帝梅利克隆感到难过。此刻他已经混乱了；他能感觉时间不断缩短，而自己的恐惧竟荒唐地和对这位未能实现自我的上帝的担忧混在了一起。</p>
<p>随后，他灵光一闪。</p>
<p>这个念头简单、直接，而且同时解决梅利克隆的问题和他自己的问题——这正是一个好灵感的检验标准。梅利克隆愿不愿接受，则是另一回事。但卡莫迪也只能试一试。</p>
<p>“梅利克隆，”他大胆说道，“我已经解决你的问题了。”</p>
<p>“哦，真的？”梅利克隆兴奋地说。“我是说，真的真的？你不是因为如果不能提出一个让我满意的解答，你就注定会在七十三秒后死掉，所以才这么说吧？我是说，你没有让这件事过分影响自己的判断吧？”</p>
<p>“我确实允许迫近的命运影响我，”卡莫迪庄严地说，“但其影响严格限于解决你这个问题所必需的程度。”</p>
<p>“哦。好吧。那请快告诉我，我太激动了！”</p>
<p>“我很想告诉你，”卡莫迪说。“可如果你打算在六七十秒以后杀死我，那我不可能——从物理上就不可能——把一切解释清楚。”</p>
<p>“我？我可没打算杀你！天哪，你真觉得我嗜血到那个地步？不，你即将遭遇的死亡是一件外部事件，和我完全无关。顺便说一句，你还剩十二秒。”</p>
<p>“这根本不够。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“当然够！这里是我的世界，你知道，我控制这里的一切，包括时间持续多久。刚才在还剩十秒的时候，我已经改变了局部时空连续体。这种操作对一个上帝来说很容易，不过事后清理起来特别麻烦。因此，你的十秒钟，将消耗我这里大约二十五年的本地时间。够了吗？”</p>
<p>“绰绰有余，”卡莫迪说。“你真是太好心了。”</p>
<p>“别放在心上，”梅利克隆说。“现在，请让我听听你的解决办法。”</p>
<p>“好。”卡莫迪深吸一口气。“你这个问题的解法，本来就内含在你观察问题时所采用的那些条件之中。它不可能是别的样子；每一个问题内部，都必然包含自身解决办法的种子。”</p>
<p>“必然吗？”梅利克隆问。</p>
<p>“必然。”卡莫迪坚定地说。</p>
<p>“好吧。目前我暂时接受这个前提。继续。”</p>
<p>“考虑你的处境，”卡莫迪说。“同时考虑它的内部方面与外部方面。你是这颗行星的上帝——但只限这颗行星。你全能而全知——但只限这里。你的智力成就极为惊人，同时你感觉到一种召唤，希望为自身之外的某种东西服务。可你的天赋拿到别处都会浪费，而在这里却除了你自己以外谁也没有。”</p>
<p>“对，对，这正是我的处境！”梅利克隆叫道。“可你还是没告诉我应该怎么办！”</p>
<p>卡莫迪深吸一口气，又慢慢呼出。“你必须做的，”他说，“就是使用你所有伟大的天赋，并且在这里——你自己的行星上——使用它们，因为只有这里能让它们发挥最大作用；而且要把这些能力用于服务他人，因为这才是你最深层的愿望。”</p>
<p>“服务他人？”梅利克隆问。</p>
<p>“你的处境就是如此指示的。”卡莫迪说。“哪怕只作最表面的考察，结论也很清楚。你孤身存在于一个多重宇宙之中；但你若要实施面向外部的行动，就必须存在一个‘外部’。然而你的本质本身，又禁止你前往那个外部。因此，外部必须来到你这里。那么，当外部来到时，你与它应当是什么关系？这一点同样很清楚。既然你在自己的世界里全能，你不可能受到援助或帮助；但你可以援助和帮助别人。这就是你与外部宇宙之间唯一自然的关系。”</p>
<p>梅利克隆想了一阵，说：“你的论证确实有力量，这一点我愿意承认。不过有些困难。比如说，外面的世界极少来到这里。你是两个又四分之一银河公转周期以来，我遇到的第一个访客。”</p>
<p>“这份工作确实需要耐心，”卡莫迪承认。“不过耐心正是你应该努力培养的品质。对你来说这会容易些，因为时间是变量。至于访客数量——首先，数量不会影响质量。单纯计数毫无价值。一个人也好，一个上帝也好，只需完成自己的工作；这才重要。那份工作要求处理一次事务还是一百万次事务，并没有区别。”</p>
<p>“可如果我有工作要做，却没人让我对他做这份工作，那我的处境岂不是和以前一样糟？”</p>
<p>“我得非常谦虚地指出，你现在有我。”卡莫迪说。“我从外部来到你这里。我有一个问题；事实上，我有好几个问题。对我来说，这些问题无解。对你来说——我不知道。不过我怀疑，它们会把你的能力逼到极限。”</p>
<p>梅利克隆思考了很长时间。卡莫迪鼻子开始发痒，但他忍住没去挠。他等待着，整颗行星也跟着一起等待，直到梅利克隆作出决定。</p>
<p>终于，梅利克隆抬起乌黑的头，说：“我真的觉得你这个办法有点门道！”</p>
<p>“承蒙你这么说。”卡莫迪回答。</p>
<p>“可我是认真的，真的！”梅利克隆说。“在我看来，你的解决办法既必然，又优雅。顺着这个结论再往前推，我甚至觉得，统治人、上帝与行星的命运，一定早就注定这一切会发生：我，一个创造者，被创造出来，却没有问题可供解决；而你，一个被造物，却成为一个问题的创造者，而那个问题只有上帝才能解决。你活过自己的一生，一直等待我来替你解决问题；而我则在这里等了半个永恒，等待你把问题带给我解决！”</p>
<p>“我一点也不会惊讶。”卡莫迪说。“你想知道我的问题是什么吗？”</p>
<p>“我已经推出来了，”梅利克隆说。“事实上，凭借我更高的智能与经验，我对这件事知道得比你多得多。表面上看，你的问题是怎样回家。”</p>
<p>“没错。”</p>
<p>“不，那还不是。我从不轻率使用词语。表面上，你需要知道自己的行星‘在哪里、在何时、是哪一个’；你还需要一种到达那里的办法，而且抵达时最好和现在保持大致相同的状态。即使只有这些，也已经够困难了。”</p>
<p>“还有什么？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“还有那个正在追杀你的死亡。”</p>
<p>“哦。”卡莫迪说。他突然觉得双腿发软。梅利克隆体贴地为他造出一张安乐椅、一支哈瓦那雪茄、一杯朗姆柯林斯鸡尾酒、一双羊毛衬里的拖鞋，以及一条水牛皮盖毯。</p>
<p>“舒服吗？”梅利克隆问。</p>
<p>“非常舒服。”</p>
<p>“很好。现在仔细听。我会用很简短而扼要的方式解释你的处境，只动用我智能的一小部分来完成这件事；我的其余部分则要承担另一项相当艰巨的工作——寻找一个可行的解决办法。不过你必须认真听，并尽量在我第一次说的时候就把一切听懂，因为我们时间非常少。”</p>
<p>“我还以为你把我的十秒钟拉长成二十五年了。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“是的。但时间这种变量很棘手，即使对我也是如此。你的二十五年已经用掉十八年，剩下的也正在以极快速度消失。现在注意！你的命就靠这个了。”</p>
<p>“好。”卡莫迪说。他身体前倾，吸了一口雪茄。“我准备好了。”</p>
<p>“你首先必须理解的，”梅利克隆说，“就是那个正在猎杀你的、不可阻挡的死亡究竟是什么性质。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪强忍住一个寒战，俯身倾听。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 8</b></p>
<p>‘It seems to me,’ Carmody said, ‘that the solution to your problem – is – is possibly –’</p>
<p>‘Yes?’ said Melichrone eagerly.</p>
<p>Carmody had no idea of what he was going to say. He was speaking in the desperate hope that the act of speaking would of itself produce meaning, since words do have meanings, and sentences have even more meanings than words.</p>
<p>‘Your problem,’ Carmody continued, ‘is to find within yourself an indwelling functionalism which will have reference to an exterior reality. But this may be an impossible quest, since you yourself are reality, and therefore you cannot posit yourself exterior to yourself.’</p>
<p>‘I can if I want to,’ Melichrone said sulkily. ‘I can posit any damned thing I please since I’m in charge around here. Being a God, you know, doesn’t mean that One must be a solipsist.’</p>
<p>‘True, true, true,’ Carmody said rapidly. (Did he have seven minutes left? Or six? And what was going to happen at the end of that time?) ‘So it is clear, your Immanence and Indwellingness are insufficient to your view of yourself, and therefore are factually insufficient since you yourself, in your form of Definer, consider these qualities to be insufficient.’</p>
<p>‘Nicely reasoned,’ Melichrone said. ‘You should have been a theologian.’</p>
<p>‘At the moment I am a theologian,’ Carmody said., (Six minutes, five minutes?) ‘Very well, then, what are you to do? … Have you ever considered making all knowledge both internal and external (assuming that there is any such thing as external knowledge), of making knowledge your quest?’</p>
<p>‘Yes, as a matter of fact, I did think of that,’ Melichrone said. ‘Among other things, I read every book in the galaxy, plumbed the secrets of Nature and of Man, explored the macrocosm and the microcosm, and so forth. I had quite an aptitude for learning, by the way, though I have subsequently forgotten a few things, like the secret of life and the ulterior motive of death. But I can learn them again whenever I please. I did learn that learning is a dry, passive business, though filled with some pleasant surprises; and I also learned that learning has no particular and peculiar importance for me. As a matter of fact, I find unlearning almost as interesting.’</p>
<p>‘Maybe you were meant to be an artist,’ Carmody suggested.</p>
<p>‘I went through that phase,’ Melichrone said. ‘I sculpted in flesh and in clay, I painted sunsets on canvas and on the sky, I wrote books in words and other books in events, I made music on instruments, and composed symphonies for wind and rain. My work was good enough, I believe; but I knew somehow that I would always be a dilettante. My omnipotence does not allow me enough room for error, you see; and my grasp of the actual is too complete to allow me to bother seriously with the representational.’</p>
<p>‘Hmm, I see,’ Carmody said. (Surely no more than three minutes left!) ‘Why not become a conqueror?’</p>
<p>‘I do not need to conquer what I already possess,’ Melichrone said. ‘And as for other worlds, I do not desire them. My qualities are peculiar to my milieu, which consists of this single planet. Possession of other worlds would involve me in unnatural actions. And besides – what use do I have for other worlds when I don’t even know what to do with this one?’</p>
<p>‘I see that you’ve given the matter a great deal of thought,’ Carmody said, his desperation merging into despair.</p>
<p>‘Of course I have. I have thought of little else for some millions of years. I have looked for a purpose exterior to myself yet essential to the nature of my being. I have looked for a directive; but I have found only myself.’</p>
<p>Carmody could have felt sorry for the God Melichrone if his own situation had not been so desperate. He was confused now; he could feel his time dwindling, and his fears were absurdly mingled with concern for the unfulfilled God.</p>
<p>Then he had an inspiration. It was simple, straightforward, and solved both Melichrone’s problem and his own – which is the test of a good inspiration. Whether Melichrone would accept it was another matter. But Carmody could only try.</p>
<p>‘Melichrone,’ he said boldly, ‘I have solved your problem.’</p>
<p>‘Oh, have you really?’ Melichrone said eagerly. ‘I mean <i>really</i> really, I mean you’re not just saying that because, unless you do solve it to my satisfaction, you’re fated to die in seventy-three seconds? I mean, you haven’t let that influence you unduly, have you?’</p>
<p>‘I have allowed my impending fate to influence me,’ Carmody said majestically, ‘only to the extent that such an influence is needed to solve your problem.’</p>
<p>‘Oh. All right. Please hurry up and tell me, I’m so excited!’</p>
<p>‘I wish to do so,’ Carmody said. ‘But I can’t – it is physically impossible to explain everything – if you are going to kill me in sixty or seventy seconds.’</p>
<p>‘I? I am not going to kill you! Good heavens, do you really think me as bloody-minded as all that? No, your impending death is an exterior event quite without reference to me. By the way, you have twelve seconds left.’</p>
<p>‘It isn’t long enough,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Of course it’s long enough! This <i>is</i> my world, you know, and I control everything in it, including the duration of time. I have just altered the local space-time continuum at the ten-second mark. It’s an easy enough operation for a God, though it requires a lot of cleaning up afterwards. Accordingly, your ten seconds will consume approximately twenty-five years of my local time. Is <i>that</i> long enough?’</p>
<p>‘It’s more than ample,’ Carmody said. ‘And it’s very kind of you.’</p>
<p>‘Think nothing of it,’ Melichrone said. ‘Now, please, let me hear your solution.’</p>
<p>‘Very well,’ Carmody said, and took a deep breath. ‘The solution to your problem is inherent in the terms in which you view the problem. It could be no other way; every problem must contain within it the seeds of its own solution.’</p>
<p>‘Must it?’Melichrone asked.</p>
<p>‘Yes, it must,’ Carmody said firmly.</p>
<p>‘All right. For the moment I’ll accept that premise. Go on.’</p>
<p>‘Consider your situation,’ Carmody said. ‘Consider both its interior and exterior aspects. You are the God of this planet; but only of this planet. You are omnipotent and omniscient; but only here. You have impressive intellectual attainments, and you feel a call to serve something outside of yourself. But your gifts would be wasted any place but here, and here there is no one but you.’</p>
<p>‘Yes, yes, that is exactly my situation!’ Melichrone cried. ‘But you still haven’t told me what to do about it!’</p>
<p>Carmody took a deep breath and exhaled slowly. ‘What you must do,’ he said, ‘is to use all of your great gifts, and to use them here, on your own planet, where they will be of maximum effect; and use them in the service of others, since this is your deepest desire.’</p>
<p>‘In the service of others?’ Melichrone asked.</p>
<p>‘It is so indicated,’ Carmody said. ‘The most superficial consideration of your situation points the verdict. You are alone in a multiplex universe; but in order for you to perform exterior deeds, there must be an exterior. However, you are barred by your very essence from going to that exterior. Therefore the exterior must come to you. When it comes, what will be your relationship to it? That also is clear. Since you are omnipotent in your own world, you cannot be aided or assisted; but you can aid and assist others. This is the only natural relationship between you and the outside universe.’</p>
<p>Melichrone thought about it, then said, ‘Your argument has force; that much I freely admit. But there are difficulties. For example, the outside world rarely comes this way. You are the first visitor I have had in two and a quarter galactic revolutions.’</p>
<p>‘The job does require patience,’ Carmody admitted. ‘But patience is a quality you must strive for. It will be easier for you since time is a variable. And as for the number of your visitors – first of all, quantity does not affect quality. There is no value in mere enumeration. A man or a God does his job; that is what counts. Whether that job requires one or a million transactions makes no difference.’</p>
<p>‘But I am as badly off as before if I have a job to perform and no one to perform it on.’</p>
<p>‘With all modesty, I must point out that you have me,’ Carmody said. ‘I have come to you from the exterior. I have a problem; indeed, I have several problems. For me, these problems are insoluble. For you – I do not know. But I suspect that they will tax your powers to the utmost.’</p>
<p>Melichrone thought about it for a very long time. Carmody’s nose began to itch, but he resisted the desire to scratch it. He waited, and the entire planet also waited while Melichrone made up his mind.</p>
<p>At last Melichrone raised his jet-black head and said, ‘I really think you have something there!’</p>
<p>‘It’s good of you to say that,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘But I mean it, I really do!’ Melichrone said. ‘Your solution seems to me both inevitable and elegant. And, by extension, it seems to me that Fate, which rules men, Gods and planets, must have destined this to happen: that I, a creator, was created with no problem to solve; and that you, a created, became the creator of a problem that only a God could solve. And that you have lived out your lifetime waiting for me to solve your problem, while I have waited here for half of eternity for you to bring me your problem to solve!’</p>
<p>‘I wouldn’t be a bit surprised,’ Carmody said. ‘Would you like to know what my problem is?’</p>
<p>‘I have already deduced,’ Melichrone said. ‘In fact, due to my superior intellect and experience, I know much more about it than you do. Superficially, your problem is how to get home.’</p>
<p>‘That’s it.’</p>
<p>‘No, that’s not it. I do not use words lightly. <i>Superficially,</i> you need to know Where, When and Which your planet is; and you need a way of getting there, and you need to arrive in much the same condition you are presently in. If that were all, it would still be difficult enough.’</p>
<p>‘What else is there?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Why, there is also the death which is pursuing you.’</p>
<p>‘Oh,’ Carmody said. He suddenly felt weak in the knees, and Melichrone graciously created an easy chair for him, and a Havana cigar, a Rum Collins, a pair of sheep-lined slippers, and a buffalo-hide lap robe.</p>
<p>‘Comfortable?’ Melichrone asked.</p>
<p>‘Very.’</p>
<p>‘Good. Pay close attention now. I will proceed to explain your situation briefly but succinctly, utilizing only a fraction of my intellect for that task while I use the rest of me for the considerable job of finding a feasible solution._But you will have to listen carefully and try to understand everything the first time I say it because we have very little time.’</p>
<p>‘I thought you stretched my ten seconds into twenty-five years,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I did. But time is a tricky sort of variable, even for me. Eighteen of your twenty-five years are already used up, and the rest of them are going with extreme rapidity. Pay attention, now! Your life depends on it.’</p>
<p>‘All right,’ Carmody said. He sat forward and puffed on his cigar. ‘I’m ready.’</p>
<p>‘The first thing you must understand,’ Melichrone said, ‘is the nature of the implacable death that is hunting you.’</p>
<p>Carmody controlled a shudder and bent forward to listen.</p>
