<h2>iv. 保罗安顿下来</h2>
<p>到这时，保罗已经差不多完成我要求他承担的任务。</p>
<p>他不但替我收集了关于自己物种危机的大量事实——这些事实的意义，他自己往往完全看不出来——而且通过自己的亲身经历，也使我极其准确地认识到你们这一类心灵究竟具有多大的能力。</p>
<p>在多年内心动荡中，他向我展示了一个原始存在受到更高层次异象纠缠时，会产生怎样的愿望，又会产生怎样的抗拒。</p>
<p>尤其重要的是，他让我看见了你们的智力与意志究竟止步在哪里。</p>
<p>有无数问题，即使经过我耐心帮助，保罗也永远无法理解；有些美具有一种严峻性，即使我以最强烈的方式向他照亮，他也无法真正欣赏。</p>
<p>不过，至少有一项考验，他以相当漂亮的方式通过了。</p>
<p>他证明：第一人类中一个普通水平的心灵，只要得到精心训练，确实有可能毫无疑问地把握个体心灵两项最高、看起来却互相冲突的职责——忠于人类，以及崇拜命运。</p>
<p>对于最后人来说，这两项职责天然互相有机连接；但对第一人类而言，它们似乎永远彼此矛盾。</p>
<p>至少，保罗的理智看见了这种矛盾，也拒绝接受那些虚假的问题解答。</p>
<p>至于其他方面，虽然他非常清楚地认识到一切人类目标都应该从属于这两个最高目标，他本人却仍和自己的同类一样，不过是一件脆弱的容器。</p>
<p>既然我已经不再需要通过保罗这件视觉和听觉仪器来考察你们的世界，我便停止用那种永不满足的调查欲折磨他——自战争结束以来，这种欲望一直支配着他。</p>
<p>此前，他始终不知疲倦地执行任务；如今，精力终于开始衰退。</p>
<p>一部分原因是我撤回了刺激；一部分原因是他那容量并不大的大脑早已装满，再也承受不了更多；还有一部分原因，则是长期“双重生活”给身体造成的消耗。</p>
<p>保罗的确正在把自己磨坏。</p>
<p>凯瑟琳——他此时仍偶尔会和她一起度过一个星期——曾说，他还没到年纪就已经开始枯萎；胳膊腿瘦得像木棍，肋骨活像一只旧捕鼠夹。</p>
<p>她私下也注意到，他拥抱时的力量已经减弱。</p>
<p>她劝他：“先轻松一阵吧。”</p>
<p>如今，他终于发现，自己事实上既没有精力，也没有兴趣再继续过去那种到处奔波、喧闹不休的生活。</p>
<p>我让他明白：我不再要求他继续工作；不过，我仍会留在他身边，观察他在正常人生中思想怎样运动。</p>
<p>因为，虽然我已经不再把他当作探索仪器使用，我仍打算看看这种“海王星染色”会怎样影响他的中年与老年。</p>
<p>就在这个阶段，保罗以一种颇有意思的方式暴露了你们物种的弱点：他进行了一场徒劳反抗，试图追求一条根本不适合自己的英雄式道路。</p>
<p>我不能在这件事上花太多篇幅，不过，鉴于它能说明你们的天性，仍可以简要记录。</p>
<p>你们应该记得，他早已相信自己的种族正在走向一场巨大灾难；唯一可能拯救它的，是一种几乎不可能发生的心灵与先天能力转变。</p>
<p>尽管当时正处在所谓“世界经济危机”之中，他并不认真相信灾难会突然爆发。</p>
<p>他想象的，是一种极其漫长的精神衰退；物质繁荣也许会重新恢复，却恰好把衰退遮住。</p>
<p>他预见到几百年、甚至几千年的停滞：人类那颗只醒了一半的心灵会再次沉入昏睡。</p>
<p>然而，在他看来，这仍不过是整颗行星生命中的一个短暂插曲。</p>
<p>现代世界那场既渺小又巨大的悲剧，归根结底也是可以接受的；它同样是宇宙之美的一项特征。</p>
<p>而且，他虽然并不能清清楚楚地说明，却十分确信：自己已经在某种很小的程度上参与了这样一项工作——把自己时代的戏剧捕捉下来，交给那个成熟而最终的人类心灵去认识。</p>
<p>他也知道，如果没有我持续支持，自己并没有任何特别出众的才能。</p>
<p>然而，虽然从根本上他已经接受这一切，内心甚至非常平静，他却无法把这种接受与平静真正落实为行动。</p>
<p>他就是无法让自己什么也不做地参与悲剧，而非要努力阻止它；明明他自己也知道这些努力注定无效。</p>
<p>一方面，他根本不是做先知的材料；另一方面，他的整个物种能力本来就不足。</p>
<p>尽管理智已经知道这一点，一股不合理的党派式狂热却突然抓住了他。</p>
<p>他本可以继续做自己尚且做得不错的工作；本可以通过教书，让少数年轻心灵获得更强的知觉与更深的喜悦。</p>
<p>可他偏偏认定，自己必须成为一个新的社会秩序与精神秩序的先知。</p>
<p>我的目的并不要求详细叙述以下这些事情：保罗怎样认真寻找借口，为一个自己明知荒唐的计划辩护，然后辞去学校职位；怎样把自己投入写作与演讲，实际上就是去宣讲他以为自己掌握的“新真理”；怎样努力在身边聚集一群合作者，而内心又偷偷把他们当作门徒；怎样重新联系旧识——《最后与最初的人》的作者——把那个胆小、贪图安逸的人当成共同先知热烈欢迎，结果马上被对方当成疯子赶走；怎样很快连他自己也看出，自己的文字作品根本不可能找到出版门路，而口头布道只给他赢来“怪人”和“讨厌鬼”的名声；怎样试图说服共产党人，说自己比他们更懂他们自己真正的思想；怎样最终被共产党以“不可救药的资产阶级”身份开除；怎样有一位倾向共产主义的知名文学杂志编辑，起初还希望把保罗吸收进自己的圈子，没过多久却庆幸终于把他甩掉；怎样在海德公园制造几次混乱场面以后，被警察带走；怎样最终虽然离开警方照管，却只是转而住进自己郊区住宅附近的疯人院；怎样经过几个月强制冥想后终于获释，而且彻底治好了这种毛病；最后，又怎样在许多人为他动用很长的关系网之后，重新回到原先那所学校。</p>
<p>我之所以记录这些事实，主要是为了还能记录另一个事实。</p>
<p>在他人生整个这段荒诞时期里，保罗内心始终仍是那个平静的、带着同情和一点好笑观看自己疯狂的旁观者。</p>
<p>至于他后来的人生，只需再说几句。</p>
<p>不到一年，他便重新牢牢套上日常生活的挽具，并下定决心：这辈子再也不要走进疯人院。</p>
<p>后来，他确实成了一位相当优秀的教师，属于那种较先进、也较有同情心的类型；希望改革，却十分谨慎。</p>
<p>由于年轻时受过我的影响，他如今既以观点大胆闻名，也以耐心、圆融和异常认真负责闻名。</p>
<p>到了适当时候，他结婚，生儿育女，成为校长，并在课程、教学方法和服装规定等方面给学校引入许多影响深远的新做法。</p>
<p>从本书读者的角度看，上一句话其实应该使用将来时，因为直到本书出版之日，保罗仍然是单身汉。</p>
<p>不过，我已经观察过他未来的整段人生，因此可以提前报告：</p>
<p>等到退休时，他会怀着感激和一种克制的自豪回顾自己的工作；</p>
<p>虽然更早以前曾决定退休不久后便结束自己的生命，他后来会改变主意，允许自己再活一两年，在安静老年的柔和光线中重新衡量自己的一生和自己的世界；</p>
<p>在这段时期，他还会进一步向我展示你们个体在衰老阶段的许多特征——智慧与幼稚奇怪地混合在一起，洞察潜力反而有所增加，却又被神经系统逐步退化一点一点阻断；</p>
<p>等到他终于再也找不到什么理由继续活下去时，他又已经失去主动结束生命的决断力，于是被困进衰老的泥沼。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" class="stapledon-body" lang="en" hidden>
<h2>iv. PAUL SETTLES DOWN</h2>
<p>PAUL HAD BY now almost fulfilled the task that I had demanded of him. Not only had he collected for me an immense store of facts about the crisis of his species, facts the significance of which he himself often entirely failed to see, but also in his own experience he had already afforded me a very precise insight into the capacity of his kind. He had shown me, through years of inner turmoil, the aspirations and reluctances of a primitive being haunted by visions from a higher sphere. In particular he had shown me the limits of your intelligence and of your will. There were innumerable problems which Paul even with my patient help could never understand. There were austerities of beauty which even with my most earnest illumination he could not admire. But one test at least Paul had triumphantly passed. He had shown that it was possible for the average mentality of the First Men, under careful tuition, to apprehend without any doubt whatever the two supreme and seemingly discordant offices of the individual mind, namely, loyalty to man and worship of fate. To the Last Men these offices are displayed as organic to one another; but to the First Men, they are for ever discrepant. Paul’s intellect at least saw this discrepancy, and refused to be put off with false solutions of the problem. For the rest though he so clearly recognized that all human purposes should be subordinated to these two supreme ends, he himself, like the rest of his kind, was but a frail vessel.</p>
<p>Since I had no further use for Paul as a visual and auditory instrument through which to examine your world, I now ceased to trouble him with the insatiable lust of inquiry which had mastered him since the conclusion of the war. Hitherto he had pursued his task indefatigably, but now his energy began to flag. This was due partly to the withdrawal of my stimulation, partly to the fact that his uncapacious brain was already charged to the full and could bear no more, partly also to the long physical strain of the double life which he had led. For Paul had indeed been wearing himself out. Katherine, with whom he still spent an occasional week, had declared that he was withering before his time, that his arms and legs were sticks and his ribs an old rat-trap. Privately she had noted also that his embraces had lost their vigour. She had urged him to ‘take things easy for a while’. And now at last he discovered that he had in fact neither the energy nor the inclination to pursue the old racketing way of life.</p>
<p>I made it clear to him that I required no more work of him, but that I should continue to be with him to observe the movements of his mind during his normal career. For, though I had no further use for him as an exploratory instrument, it was my intention to watch the way in which his Neptunian tincture would influence him in middle age and senility. At this juncture Paul displayed rather interestingly the weakness of his kind by futile rebellion, and an attempt to pursue a heroic career for which he was unsuited. I must not dwell upon this incident, but it may be briefly recorded for the light which it throws upon your nature. He had already, it will be remembered, become convinced that his race was heading for a huge disaster, from which nothing could save it but an impossible change of heart, of native capacity. In spite of the current ‘world-economic crisis’, he had no serious expectation of a sudden catastrophe; but he had come to believe in a long-drawn-out spiritual decline, masked by a revival of material prosperity. He looked forward to centuries, perhaps millennia, of stagnation, in which the half-awakened mind of man would have sunk back once more into stupor. But he saw this phase as but a momentary episode in the life of the planet. And he saw the little huge tragedy of the modern world as, after all, acceptable, a feature of cosmical beauty. He knew, moreover, not clearly but yet with conviction, that he himself had played some small part in apprehending the drama of his age for the cognizance of the mature and final human mind. He knew also that without my continuous support he had no outstanding ability. Yet, though fundamentally he had accepted all this, and was indeed profoundly at peace, he could not express this acceptance and this peace in conduct. He could not bring himself to participate in the tragedy without making efforts to avert it, efforts which he knew must be futile, because in the first place he himself was not made of the stuff of prophets, and because in the second place the nature of his species was inadequate. Though he knew it was so, an irrational fury of partisanship now seized him. Instead of continuing the work which he could do tolerably well, instead of striving by his school-teaching to elicit in certain young minds an enhanced percipience and delight, he must needs take it upon himself to become the prophet of a new social and spiritual order.</p>
<p>It is no part of my purpose to tell in detail how Paul, after earnestly seeking excuses for a project which he knew to be ridiculous, gave up his post in the school; how he devoted himself to writing and speaking, in fact to preaching the new truth that he supposed himself to possess; how he tried to gather round him a group of collaborators, whom secretly he regarded as disciples; how he made contact once more with his old acquaintance the author of Last and First Men, hailed that timid and comfort-loving creature as a fellow-prophet, and was hastily dismissed for a lunatic; how, subsequently, his literary products proved even to himself their inability to find their way into print, and his efforts at vocal prophecy earned him the reputation of a crank and a bore; how he tried to persuade the Communists that he knew their mind better than they did themselves; how he was expelled from the Communist Party as an incorrigible bourgeois; how the communistically inclined editor of a well-known literary journal, who at first hoped to bring Paul within his own circle, was soon very thankful to get rid of him; how after several disorderly scenes in Hyde Park, Paul was eventually marched off by the police, from whose care he finally escaped only to take up his abode in the Lunatic Asylum near his suburban home; how after some months of enforced meditation he was at last released, thoroughly cured of his disease; how, finally, but not until many far-reaching wires had been pulled in his interest, he was re-established in his old school. These facts I record chiefly so that I may also record another. Throughout this fantastic phase of his life, Paul remained inwardly the calm, the compassionately amused spectator of his own madness.</p>
<p>Of his subsequent career I need only say that within a year he had settled himself firmly into harness once more, with the determination never again to see the inside of a lunatic asylum. He became indeed an admirable schoolmaster of the more advanced and sympathetic type, bent upon reform, but cautious. In consequence of my influence during his youth he was now able to earn a reputation for daring ideas, yet also for patience, tact and extreme conscientiousness. In due season he married, begat children, became a head master, and introduced far-reaching novelties into his school, in respect of curriculum, teaching methods, and dress. From the point of view of readers of this book, the tense of this last sentence should have been future, for up to the date of publication, Paul remains a bachelor, Nevertheless I have already observed his future career, and can report that, when the time comes for him to retire, he will look back on his work with thankfulness and modest pride; that, having earlier determined to commit suicide soon after his retirement, he will change his mind and allow himself a year or two to re-assess his life and his world in the mellow light of a peaceful old age; that during this period he will afford me much further knowledge of the senescent phase of your individuality, with its strange blend of wisdom and puerility, its increased potentiality of insight, progressively thwarted by neural decay; that when there is no further reason for him to remain alive, Paul will no longer have the resolution to kill himself, and will be trapped in the quagmire of senility.</p>
