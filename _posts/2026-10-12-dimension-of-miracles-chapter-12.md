<h2>第十二章</h2>
<p>卡莫迪解释完以后，莫兹利说：“我明白了。故事倒挺有意思，不过我敢肯定你把它戏剧化得过了头。总之，你现在到了这里，而你正在找一颗叫作——地球的行星？”</p>
<p>“是的，先生。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“地球……”莫兹利挠着头沉思。“这对你倒很走运；我好像记得那个地方。”</p>
<p>“您真的记得，莫兹利先生？”</p>
<p>“是的，我相当确定。”莫兹利说。“一颗小小的绿色行星，居住着一种像你这样的单一形态类人生物。对不对？”</p>
<p>“完全正确！”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“这种东西我记性一向不错。”莫兹利说。“而且碰巧，这一颗地球就是我造的。”</p>
<p>“真的是您造的，先生？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“没错。我记得特别清楚，因为在建造地球的过程中，我还顺手发明了科学。这个故事你也许会觉得有趣。”他转向两个助手。“你们两个则最好把它当成一堂课。”</p>
<p>没人打算否认莫兹利讲故事的权利。于是卡莫迪和两名助理工程师都摆出聚精会神的姿势，莫兹利开始讲了起来。</p>
<p><strong>地球创造记</strong></p>
<p>那时候我还只是个规模很小的承包商。偶尔在这里造颗行星，在那里搭个世界，有时也能接到一颗矮星的活儿。不过工程总是很难找，客户又无一例外喜怒无常、吹毛求疵，付账还特别慢。那年月的客户难伺候得很，一点鸡毛蒜皮都要争。这个得改，那个也得改；水为什么非得往低处流？重力太大；热空气凭什么往上升，明明应该往下掉。诸如此类。</p>
<p>那时候我还相当天真。无论自己做了什么，我都会耐心解释其中的审美理由和实用理由。结果没过多久，提问题和作解释花掉的时间比真正施工还长。废话实在太多。我知道必须想个办法，可一直不知道该怎么办。</p>
<p>后来，就在接到地球工程以前不久，一种处理客户关系的全新方法渐渐在我脑子里成了形。我发现自己经常喃喃念叨：“形式追随功能。”我很喜欢这句话听起来的样子。不过接着我又会问自己：“形式为什么必须追随功能？”然后我给自己的答案是：“形式追随功能，是因为它是一条不可改变的自然法则，也是应用科学最基本的公理之一。”这句话听起来我也很喜欢，尽管其实没多少道理。</p>
<p>可是有没有道理并不重要。重要的是，我已经有了一个新发现。我无意中闯进了广告与推销术的领域，而且发现了一个潜力无穷的窍门——也就是所谓“科学决定论”的教义。</p>
<p>地球是我的第一个试验案例，所以我永远也不会忘记它。</p>
<p>当时，一个个子很高、满脸胡须、目光锐利的老人来找我，订购了一颗行星。（卡莫迪，你们的地球就是这么开始的。）我干得很快，好像六天就完工了，以为事情到这里也就结束了。那又是一个低预算行星项目，因此我这里省一点、那里凑合一点。不过要听那位业主怎么抱怨，你准会以为我把他的眼珠子都从脑袋里偷走了。</p>
<p>“为什么这里有这么多龙卷风？”他问。</p>
<p>“那是大气循环系统的一部分。”我告诉他。（其实那阵子我有点赶工，忘了安装空气循环过载阀。）</p>
<p>“这个地方有四分之三全是水！”他又对我说。“而我明明明确规定，陆地和水域的比例应该是四比一！”</p>
<p>“可我们没法照那个比例造！”我告诉他。（他的那套荒唐规格书其实已经被我弄丢了。我从来就记不住这些鸡毛蒜皮的单行星小工程。）</p>
<p>“而你给我的那一点陆地，还全塞满了沙漠、沼泽、丛林和山脉。”</p>
<p>“很有景观效果。”我指出。</p>
<p>“我才不管什么景观效果！”那家伙雷鸣般吼道。“是啊，一个海洋、十来个湖、几条河、一两道山脉，那当然很好。能把地方打扮打扮，也让居民心情愉快。可你给我的这是什么？一堆破烂！”</p>
<p>“这么设计是有原因的。”我说。（事实上，不用再加工的旧山脉、拿大量河流和海洋充填，再加上我从‘行星废品商奥里’那里便宜收来的几片沙漠，我们根本没法从这工程里赚到钱。但这话我当然不会告诉他。）</p>
<p>“原因！”他尖叫起来。“那我要怎么跟我的人民解释？我准备往那颗行星上放一个完整种族，也许两个、三个。他们会是人类，按照我自己的形象创造；而人类出了名地挑剔，就跟我一样。我该跟他们怎么说？”</p>
<p>这个嘛，我当然知道他可以怎么跟他们说；不过我不想显得无礼，于是假装认真思考了一会儿。奇怪的是，我还真的思考了。然后，我想出了一个终结一切推销窍门的终极窍门。</p>
<p>“你只要把朴素的科学真理告诉他们。”我说。“告诉他们：从科学上讲，凡是存在的东西，就必然应该存在。”</p>
<p>“什么？”他说。</p>
<p>“这叫决定论。”我当场现编了这个名字。“非常简单，只不过有一点深奥。首先，形式追随功能；所以，你这颗行星既然存在，单凭这个事实，它就必然恰好是它应该成为的样子。其次，科学是不变的；所以任何不具有不变性的东西，都不能叫科学。最后，一切事物都遵循明确的规则。你未必总能弄清那些规则是什么，但你可以确定，它们一定存在。因此，合乎理性的做法不是问‘为什么是这个而不是那个’，而应该问‘它是怎样运作的’。”</p>
<p>那老头接着问了我几个相当难的问题，而且他本人确实很聪明。不过他对工程学一窍不通；他的专业是伦理、道德、宗教，以及诸如此类的幽灵玩意儿。所以，他自然也提不出真正有效的反驳。他属于特别喜欢抽象概念的那种人，开始反复念叨：“‘凡存在者，即必然存在者。’嗯，这个公式很值得玩味，而且颇有几分斯多葛主义的古雅气息。我会把其中一些洞见纳入给我的人民的教导……可是请告诉我：我准备赋予他们自由意志，那么这种自由意志该怎样与科学那种不确定的宿命性相调和？”</p>
<p>说真的，这老头差一点把我问住。我笑了一下，又咳嗽几声，给自己争取思考时间，然后说：“答案显而易见！”只要用得合适，这始终是一个很不错的回答。</p>
<p>“我想也是。”他说。“可惜我没有看出来。”</p>
<p>“你看，”我说，“你要赋予你的人民的这种自由意志，它本身不也是一种宿命吗？”</p>
<p>“也可以这样理解。不过两者之间的区别——”</p>
<p>“再说了，”我赶紧插嘴，“谁规定自由意志和宿命彼此不相容？”</p>
<p>“它们看起来显然不相容。”他说。</p>
<p>“那只是因为你不懂科学。”我说，当着他那只鹰钩鼻的面玩了一次老掉牙的偷梁换柱。“你看，尊敬的先生，科学最基本的定律之一，就是偶然性参与一切过程。想必你也知道，偶然性在数学上就等价于自由意志。”</p>
<p>“可你说的话彼此矛盾。”他说。</p>
<p>“事情本来就是这样。”我说。“矛盾也是宇宙的一条基本规律。矛盾产生冲突，没有冲突，一切都会进入熵的状态。所以，如果事物不以一种表面上无法调和的矛盾状态存在，我们根本不可能拥有行星，也不可能拥有宇宙。”</p>
<p>“表面上？”他反应快得像闪电。</p>
<p>“完全正确。”我说。“所谓矛盾，我们暂且可以把它定义成现实中成对对立面的共存，但它绝不是问题的最终答案。举个例子，假定有一种孤立的单一趋势。那么，把一个趋势推到极限会发生什么？”</p>
<p>“我一点概念也没有。”老头说。“这种讨论完全缺乏具体内容——”</p>
<p>“会发生的事情，”我说，“就是这个趋势转化成它自己的反面。”</p>
<p>“真的会？”他问，明显被震动了不少。这些宗教人士一旦试图应付科学，真是有意思得很。</p>
<p>“当然会。”我向他保证。“证据都放在我的实验室里，不过演示过程有点冗长——”</p>
<p>“不，不必了，我相信你的话。”老头说。“毕竟，我们已经订立过圣约。”</p>
<p>他总把“合同”叫作“圣约”。意思完全一样，不过听着漂亮多了。</p>
<p>“成对的对立面……”他沉思着。“决定论。事物转化成自己的反面。恐怕这一切相当错综复杂。”</p>
<p>“而且还很有美感。”我说。“不过关于极端如何转化，我还没有讲完。”</p>
<p>“请继续。”他说。</p>
<p>“谢谢。现在，我们有熵；熵的意思是，事物会维持自身的运动，除非受到外界影响。（以我的经验看，有时候受到外界影响，它们也照样维持。）因此，我们就有熵推动一个东西向自己的反面发展。如果一件东西会被推向自己的反面，那么所有东西都会被推向自己的反面，因为科学必须保持一致。现在你看明白了吗？我们有一大群对立面发疯似的互相转化，不停变成自己的反面。在更高一级的组织层次上，则是整组整组的对立面重复同一套过程。再上一层，又上一层。到这里都明白吧？”</p>
<p>“我想是吧。”他说。</p>
<p>“很好。现在问题自然就来了：这就是全部吗？我的意思是，这些对立面一会儿把自己由里翻到外，一会儿又由外翻回里——整个游戏难道就只有这些？最漂亮的地方在于：不是！绝对不是。那些像训练有素的海豹一样翻来翻去的对立面，只是真正发生之事的一个侧面。因为——”说到这里，我停下来，用极其低沉的声音继续，“——因为存在一种智慧，它能够越过现象世界的碰撞与骚乱。这种智慧，先生，能够洞穿这些真实事物所具有的幻象性质，并越过它们，看见宇宙更深层的运作；而在那里，一切处于一种堪称伟大而庄严的和谐状态。”</p>
<p>“一个东西怎么可能既是幻象又是真实的？”他又像鞭子一样飞快地问我。</p>
<p>“这种问题的答案轮不到我知道。”我告诉他。“我只是一个卑微的科学工作者。我看见什么，就依据看见的东西行事。不过，也许这背后存在某种伦理理由。”</p>
<p>老头对这句话琢磨了好一会儿，我看得出来，他内心斗争得相当激烈。他当然和任何聪明人一样，一眼就能发现逻辑谬误；而我的整套论证里本来就布满了谬误。但像所有书呆子一样，他又偏偏迷恋矛盾，而且有一种强烈冲动，想把矛盾纳入自己的体系。我提出的全部命题——他的常识告诉他，事情不可能复杂到这种地步；可他的理智又告诉他，也许事情看上去真的就是如此复杂，而在这一切底下，也许还隐藏着一个漂亮、简单、统一的原则。就算没有统一原则，至少总该有个结实可靠的道德寓意。最后，我又把他彻底钓上了钩，仅仅因为我用了“伦理”这个词。因为这位老先生简直是个伦理学狂魔，浑身都被伦理饱和了；说他叫“伦理先生”也一点不过分。于是，我纯属偶然地给了他一个观念：整个该死的宇宙，都是一连串训诫与矛盾、法则与不公，而这一切最终都通往一种最精致、最稀薄、最纯化的伦理秩序。</p>
<p>过了一会儿，他说：“这里的深度超过了我原本的考虑。我本来只打算教导我的人民伦理，并把他们的注意力引向那些在道德上具有强制意义的问题——例如一个人应该怎样生活、为什么生活——而不是‘生命物质由什么构成’之类的问题。我希望他们成为探索者，去探测欢乐、恐惧、虔敬、希望与绝望的深处，而不是成为科学家，观察恒星和雨滴，再根据观察结果提出宏大而不实用的假说。我知道宇宙存在，却一直认为它是多余的。现在，你纠正了我。”</p>
<p>“这个嘛，”我说，“我可不是想给您添麻烦。我只是觉得这些事情应该指出来……”</p>
<p>老人笑了。“你给我添了麻烦，”他说，“却使我免于更大的麻烦。我可以按照自己的形象进行创造；但我不会创造一个塞满缩小版‘我自己’的世界。自由意志对我很重要。我的造物将拥有它，这既是他们的荣耀，也是他们的悲哀。他们会拿起你所谓‘科学’这件闪闪发光而毫无用处的玩具，并把它抬升成一个从未公开宣布的神位。物理矛盾和关于太阳的抽象概念会令他们着迷；他们会追求关于这些事物的知识，却忘记探索自己内心的知识。你已经使我相信这一点，我感谢你提前给我警告。”</p>
<p>坦白说，到了这里，他让我有点紧张了。我的意思是，他其实是个无名小卒，也不认识任何重要人物；可他偏偏有一种大人物的气派。我总觉得，他完全有能力给我惹出天大的麻烦，而且只需要说几句话就能办到——一句话像有毒的飞镖扎进我脑子，从此再也拔不出来。说实话，这让我有点害怕。</p>
<p>不过，先生，那老滑头大概已经读到了我的念头。因为他说：“不必害怕。我毫无保留地接受你替我建造的这个世界；它会很好用，就保持现在的样子即可。至于你同时建进这个世界的瑕疵和缺陷，我也接受；甚至不能说完全不带感激。我也为这些缺陷付账。”</p>
<p>“怎么付？”我问。“错误要怎么付款？”</p>
<p>“用不争辩地接受它们来付款。”他说。“然后，我现在转身离开你，去处理我自己的事务，也处理我人民的事务。”</p>
<p>说完，老先生一句话也没再讲，就走了。</p>
<p>“这件事确实让我想了很久。明明所有漂亮论据都在我这边，可不知怎么，最后一句话还是让那老头拿走了。我明白他的意思；他已经履行了和我的合同，事情到此为止。他离开时，没有留给我任何私人性质的话。在他看来，这是一种惩罚。</p>
<p>“不过，那只是他的看法。我需要他的话干什么？当然，我当时确实很想听；这也很自然。后来相当长一段时间，我还试着去找过他。可他根本不愿意见我。</p>
<p>“所以，其实没什么大不了的。我从那个世界上赚了一笔相当不错的利润；合同虽然有些地方被我掰弯了，但我并没有把它弄断。事情就是这样；人总得对自己负责，总得赚到利润。不能因为后果就把自己搞得过于激动。</p>
<p>“不过我讲这一大篇，本来是想说明一个道理，所以你们两个小子给我仔细听。科学里充满了各种规则，因为我当初就是这样发明它的。我为什么要把它发明成这样？因为规则对聪明的经营者帮助很大，就像一大堆法律对律师帮助很大一样。科学的规则、教义、公理、定律和原则，是来帮助你们的，不是来妨碍你们的。它们存在的目的，是替你们已经做过的事情提供理由。它们大多数多少都是真的，这也很有帮助。</p>
<p>“可你们永远都要记住——这些规则，是供你们在事情做完以后向客户解释自己为什么这么做，而不是在动手以前拿来约束自己的。接到项目时，你觉得怎样合适就怎样做；然后让事实去适配事件，而不是反过来让事件适配事实。</p>
<p>“记住：这些规则存在，是为了筑起一道语言上的屏障，挡住那些不停提问题的人。但你们自己绝不能拿它们当屏障。如果你们真从我这里学到了什么，那就应该明白：我们的工作从本质上说必然不可解释。我们就是去做；有时结果不错，有时不怎么样。</p>
<p>“但永远别试图向自己解释，为什么某些事情会发生，为什么另一些事情不会发生。不要问，也不要幻想真有一个解释存在。明白了吗？”</p>
<p>两个助手用力点头。他们看上去像突然得到启示的人，仿佛刚刚皈依了一种新宗教。卡莫迪敢拿任何东西打赌：这两个一本正经的年轻人已经把“建造者”说的每一个字都牢牢记住，而他们接下来要做的，就是把这些话抬升成——一条规则。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 12</b></p>
<p>‘I see,’ Maudsley said, after Carmody had finished explaining. ‘It’s an interesting story, though I’m sure you’ve overdramatized it. Still, here you are, and you’re looking for a planet called – Earth?’</p>
<p>‘That is correct, sir,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Earth,’ Maudsley mused, scratching his head. ‘This is most fortunate for you; I seem to remember the place.’</p>
<p>‘Do you really, Mr Maudsley?’</p>
<p>‘Yes, I’m quite sure of it,’ Maudsley said. ‘It’s a small green planet, and it supports a monomorphic humanoid race like yourself. Am I right?’</p>
<p>‘Completely right!’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I have rather a memory for these things,’ Maudsley said. ‘And in this particular case, as it happens, I built Earth.’</p>
<p>‘Did you really, sir?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Yep. I remember distinctly, because in the course of building it, I also invented science. Perhaps you will find the story amusing.’ He turned to his aides. ‘And <i>you</i> might find the tale instructive.’</p>
<p>No one was going to deny Maudsley the right to tell a story. So Carmody and the assistant engineers assumed attentive postures, and Maudsley began.</p>
<p>the story of the creation of earth</p>
<p>I was still quite a small contractor then. I put up a planet here and there, and I got to do an occasional dwarf star. But jobs were always hard to come by, and the customers were invariably capricious, fault-finding, and slow in their payments. Customers were hard to please in those days; they argued about every little detail. <i>Change this, change that, why must water flow downhill, the gravity’s too heavy, the hot air rises when it ought to fall.</i> And so forth.</p>
<p>I was quite naïve in those days. I used to explain the aesthetic and practical reasons for everything I did. Before long, the questions and the explanations were taking longer than the jobs. There was entirely too much talk-talk. I knew that I had to do something about it, but I couldn’t figure out what.</p>
<p>Then, just before the Earth project, a whole new approach to customers’ relations began to shape itself in my mind. I found myself muttering to myself, ‘Form follows function.’ I liked the way it sounded. But then I would ask myself, ‘<i>Why</i> must form follow function?’ And the reason I gave myself was, ‘Form follows function because that is an immutable law of nature and one of the fundamental axioms of applied science.’ And I liked the sound of that, too, although it didn’t make much sense.</p>
<p>But sense didn’t matter. What mattered was that I had made a new discovery. I had unwittingly stumbled into the art of advertising and salesmanship, and I had discovered the gimmick of great possibilities, namely, the doctrine of scientific determinism.</p>
<p>Earth was my first test case, and that is why I will always remember it.</p>
<p>A tall, bearded old man with piercing eyes had come to me and ordered a planet. (That was how your planet began, Carmody.) Well, I did the job quickly, in six days I believe, and thought that would be the end of it. It was another of those budget planets, and I had cut a few corners here and there. But to hear the owner complain, you’d have thought ‘I’d stolen the eyes out of his head.</p>
<p>‘Why are there so many tornadoes?’ he asked. ‘It’s part of the atmosphere circulation system,’ I told him. (Actually, I had been a little rushed at that time; I had forgotten to put in an air-circulation overload valve.)</p>
<p>‘Three-quarters of the place is water!’ he told me. ‘And I clearly specified a four-to-one land-to-water ratio!’</p>
<p>‘Well, we couldn’t do it that way!’ I told him. (I had lost his ridiculous specifications; I never can keep track of these absurd little one-planet projects.)</p>
<p>‘And you’ve filled what little land you gave me with deserts and swamps and jungles and mountains.’</p>
<p>‘It’s scenic,’ I pointed out.</p>
<p>‘I don’t care from scenic!’ the fellow thundered. ‘Oh, sure, one ocean, a dozen lakes, a couple rivers, one or two mountain ranges, that would have been fine. Dresses the place up, gives the inhabitants a good feeling. But what you gave me is <i>shlock!</i></p>
<p>‘There’s a reason for it,’ I said. (In point of fact, we couldn’t make the job pay except by using reconstituted mountains, a lot of rivers and oceans as filler, and a couple of deserts I had bought cheap from Ourie the Planet-Junker. But I wasn’t going to tell <i>him</i> that.)</p>
<p>‘A reason!’ he screamed. ‘What will I tell my people? I’m putting an entire race on that planet, maybe two or three. They’ll be humans, made in my own image; and humans are notoriously picky, just as I am. What am I supposed to tell them?’</p>
<p>Well, I knew what he could tell them; but I didn’t want to be offensive, so I pretended to give the matter some thought. And strangely enough, I <i>did</i> think. And I came up with the gimmick to end all gimmicks.</p>
<p>‘You just tell them the plain scientific truth,’ I said. ‘You tell them that, scientifically, everything that <i>is</i> must be.’</p>
<p>‘Huh?’ he said.</p>
<p>‘It’s determinism,’ I said, making up the name on the spur of the moment. ‘It’s quite simple, though a bit esoteric. To start with, form follows function; therefore your planet is exactly as it should be by the simple fact of <i>being</i> at all. Next, science is invariable; so if anything isn’t invariable, it ain’t science. And finally, everything follows definite rules. You can’t always figure out what those rules are, but you can be sure they’re there. So, it stands to reason that no one ought to ask <i>why this instead of that?</i> Instead, everyone ought to ask <i>how does it work?’</i></p>
<p>Well, he asked me some pretty tough questions, and he was a pretty smart old fellow. But he didn’t know damn-all about engineering; his field was ethics and morals and religion and spook stuff like that. So of course, he just wasn’t able to come up with any real objections. He was one of these types who love abstractions, and he started repeating, ‘“That which <i>is</i> is that which <i>must be.</i>” Hmm, a very intriguing formula and not without its patina of stoicism. I shall incorporate some of these insights into the lessons I give to my people … But tell me this: how can I reconcile this indeterminate fatality of science with the free will I plan to give to my people?’</p>
<p>Well, the old boy almost had me there. I smiled and coughed to give myself time to think, and then I said, ‘The answer is obvious!’ Which is always a good answer, as far as it goes.</p>
<p>‘I daresay it is,’ he said. ‘But I don’t perceive it.’</p>
<p>‘Look,’ I said, ‘this free will you’re giving your people, isn’t that a kind of fatality also?’</p>
<p>‘It could be considered as such. But the difference –’</p>
<p>‘And besides,’ I said hastily, ‘since when are free will and fatality incompatible?’</p>
<p>‘They certainly seem incompatible,’ he said.</p>
<p>‘That’s only because you don’t understand science,’ I said, performing the old switcheroo right under his hooked nose. ‘You see, my dear sir, one of the most basic laws of science is that chance plays a part in everything. Chance, I’m sure you know, is the mathematical equivalent of free will.’</p>
<p>‘But what you’re saying is quite contradictory,’ he said.</p>
<p>‘That’s how it goes,’ I said. ‘Contradiction is one more of the fundamental rules of the Universe. Contradiction generates strife, without which everything would reach a stage of entropy. So we couldn’t have any planet or any universe if things didn’t exist in an apparently irreconcilable state of contradiction.’</p>
<p>‘Apparently?’ he said, quick as a flash.</p>
<p>‘Right as rain,’ I said. ‘Contradiction, which we can define provisionally as the existence of reality-paired opposites, isn’t the last word on the subject. For example, let’s posit a single isolated tendency. What happens when you push a tendency to the limit?’</p>
<p>‘I haven’t the slightest idea,’ the old guy said. ‘The lack of specifics in this sort of discussion –’</p>
<p>‘What happens,’ I said, ‘is that the tendency turns into its <i>opposite.</i>’</p>
<p>‘Does it really?’ he asked, considerably shook up. These religious types are something when they try to tackle science.</p>
<p>‘It really does,’ I assured him. ‘I’ve got the proofs in my lab, though the demonstrations are a bit tedious –’</p>
<p>‘No, please, I take your word,’ the old guy said. ‘After all, we did make a Covenant.’</p>
<p>That was the word he always used for ‘contract.’ It meant the same thing, but sounded better.</p>
<p>‘Paired opposites,’ he mused. ‘Determinism. Things becoming their opposites. It’s all quite intricate, I’m afraid.’</p>
<p>‘And aesthetic as well,’ I said. ‘But I didn’t finish about the transformation of extremes.’</p>
<p>‘Kindly go on,’ he said.</p>
<p>‘Thanks. Now then, we have entropy, which means that things persist in their motion unless there is outside influence. (Sometimes even when there is outside influence, in my experience.) But so, we got entropy driving a thing towards its opposite. If one thing is driven towards its opposite, then all things are driven towards their opposite, because science is consistent. Now you get the picture? We’ve got all these opposites transforming themselves like crazy and becoming their opposites. On a higher level of organization, we have groups of opposites going through the same bit. And higher and higher. So far so good?’</p>
<p>‘I suppose so,’ he said.</p>
<p>‘Fine. Now, the question naturally arises, is this <i>all</i>? I mean, these opposites turning themselves inside out and then outside in, is that the whole ball game? And the beauty part is, it’s not! No, sir. These opposites flipping around like trained seals are only an aspect of what’s really happening. Because –’ And here I paused and spoke in a very deep voice. ‘– because there is a wisdom that sees beyond the clash and turmoil of the phenomenal world. This wisdom, sir, sees through the illusory quality of these real things, and sees beyond them into the deeper workings of the Universe, which are in a state of like great and magnificent harmony.’</p>
<p>‘How can a thing be both illusory and real?’ he asked me, quick as a whip.</p>
<p>‘It is not for me to know an answer like that,’ I told him. ‘Me, I am a mere humble scientific worker and I see what I see and act accordingly. But maybe there’s an ethical reason behind it.’</p>
<p>The old boy mused on that one for a while, and I could see he was having quite a tussle with himself. He could detect a logical fallacy as fast as anyone, of course, and my reasons had been shot through with them. But like all eggheads, he was fascinated with contradictions and he had the strong urge to incorporate them into his system. And all the propositions I had proposed, well, his common sense told him that things couldn’t be <i>that</i> tricky; but his intellectuality told him that maybe things did indeed seem that complicated, but maybe there was a nice simple unifying principle underneath it all. Or, if not a unifying principle, at least a good solid moral. And finally, I had hooked him all over again just because I had used the word ‘ethics.’ Because this old gent was a perfect demon for ethics, he was supersaturated with ethics; you could call him Mr Ethics, make no mistake. And so, quite accidentally, I had given him the idea that the whole bloody Universe was a series of homilies and contradictions, of laws and inequities, all leading to the most exquisite and rarefied sort of ethical order.</p>
<p>‘There is a greater depth here than I had considered,’ he said after a while. ‘I had planned to instruct my people in ethics only; and to direct their attention to morally imperative questions such as how and why a man should live instead of what constitutes living matter; I wanted them to be explorers plumbing the depths of joy, fear, piety, hope, despair, rather than scientists who examine stars and raindrops and form grandiose and impractical hypotheses on the basis of their findings. I was aware of the Universe, but considered it superfluous. Now you have corrected me.’</p>
<p>‘Well, look,’ I said, ‘I didn’t mean to cause trouble. I just thought I should point out this stuff …’</p>
<p>The old man smiled. ‘By causing me trouble,’ he said, ‘you have spared me greater trouble. I can create in my own image; but I will not create a world peopled with miniature versions of myself. Free will is important to me. My creatures will have it, to their glory and their sorrow. They will take this glittering useless toy which you call science and they will elevate it to an undeclared Godhead. Physical contradictions and solar abstractions will fascinate them; they will pursue knowledge of these things and forget to explore the knowledge of their own heart. You have convinced me of this, and I am grateful for the forewarning.’</p>
<p>I’ll be frank, he got me a little nervous just about then. I mean, he was a nobody, he didn’t know any important people; and yet, he had the grand manner. I had the feeling that he could cause me one hell of a lot of trouble, and I felt that he could do it with a few words, a sentence like a poisoned dart lodged in my mind and never to be removed. And that scared me a little, to tell the truth.</p>
<p>Well sir, the old joker must have been reading my mind. For he said, ‘Do not be frightened. I accept without reservation the world you have built for me; it will serve very well, exactly as it is. As for the flaws and defects which you also built into my world, I accept those also, not entirely without gratitude; and I pay for those, too.’</p>
<p>‘How?’ I asked. ‘How do you pay for errors?’</p>
<p>‘By accepting them without dispute,’ he said. ‘And by turning away from you now and going about my business and the business of my people.’</p>
<p>And the old gentleman left without another word.</p>
<p>‘Well, it left me pretty thoughtful. I’d had all the good arguments, but the old boy left somehow with the last word. I knew what he meant; he had fulfilled his contract with me and that ended it. He was leaving with no word for me personally. From his point of view, it was a kind of punishment.</p>
<p>‘But that’s only the way <i>he</i> saw it. What did I need with his word? I wanted to hear it, of course; that’s only natural; and for quite a while I tried to look him up. But he didn’t care to see me.</p>
<p>‘So it really doesn’t matter. I made a pretty nice profit on that world, and even if I bent the contract here and there, I didn’t break it. That’s how things are; you owe it to yourself to make a profit. You can’t get too worked up over the consequences.</p>
<p>‘But I was trying to make a point out of all this, and I want you boys to listen carefully. Science is filled with a lot of rules, because I invented it that way. Why did I invent it that way? Because rules are a great assistance to a smart operator, just as a lot of laws are a great help to lawyers. The rules, doctrines, axioms, laws, and principles of science are there to help you, not to hinder you. They’re there in order to provide you with reasons for what you do. Most of them are true, more or less, and that helps.</p>
<p>‘But always remember – these rules are there to help you explain to the customers what you do <i>after you do it,</i> not before. When you have a project, do it exactly as you see fit; then fit the facts around the event, not the other way around.</p>
<p>‘Remember – these rules exist as a verbal barrier against people who ask questions. But they should <i>not</i> be used as a barrier by you. If you’ve learned anything from me, you’ve learned that our work is inevitably inexplicable; we simply do it, and sometimes it comes out well and sometimes not.</p>
<p>‘But never try to explain to yourselves why some things happen and why other things don’t happen. Don’t ask, and don’t imagine that an explanation exists. Get me?’</p>
<p>The two assistants nodded vehemently. They looked enlightened, like men who have found a new religion. Carmody would have bet anything that those two earnest young men had memorized every one of the Builder’s words, and would now proceed to elevate those words into – a rule.</p>
