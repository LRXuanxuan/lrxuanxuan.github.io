<h2>第十章</h2>
<p>卡莫迪发现自己站在一片绿色草原上。此时应该正是中午，因为一颗闪耀的橙色太阳悬在正头顶。不远处，一小群带斑点的牛在高高的草丛中缓慢啃食。再往后，卡莫迪可以看见一圈深色森林的边缘。</p>
<p>他慢慢转了一圈。草地向四面八方延伸，但森林边缘生着浓密灌木。他听见狗叫。另一边还有群山，一条漫长、锯齿状的山脉，顶峰覆盖着积雪。灰云贴在较高的山坡上。眼角余光里，一抹红色闪过。他转头看去，似乎是一只狐狸。它好奇地打量他一眼，随后蹦跳着朝森林跑去。</p>
<p>“简直像地球。”卡莫迪评论。随后他想起了奖品——上一次见到它时，它还是一条正在冬眠的绿蛇。他摸了摸脖子，可奖品已经不在那里。</p>
<p>“我在这儿。”奖品说。</p>
<p>卡莫迪四下张望，看见一个小小的铜坩埚。</p>
<p>“这是你？”卡莫迪一边问，一边把坩埚捡起来。</p>
<p>“当然是我，”奖品说。“你连自己的奖品都认不出来？”</p>
<p>“这个……你变化也太大了。”</p>
<p>“这一点我很清楚，”奖品说。“但我的本质——真正的我——永远不变。怎么了？”</p>
<p>卡莫迪刚刚朝坩埚里面看了一眼，差点把它扔出去。里面是一具被剥了皮、已经吃掉一半的小动物尸体——也许是只小猫。</p>
<p>“你里面那个是什么？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“非得问的话，那是我的午餐，”奖品说。“我趁传送途中赶紧吃了两口。”</p>
<p>“哦。”</p>
<p>“就算是奖品，偶尔也得补充营养。”奖品讽刺地补充。“而且我还得休息，做适量运动，进行性交，间歇性地喝醉，以及偶尔排一次便；从我被颁给你到现在，这些需求你一项都没替我安排过。”</p>
<p>“可这些事我自己也一项没干过。”卡莫迪回答。</p>
<p>“你真的也需要？”奖品用吃惊的声音问。“对，当然，我想你应该需要。真奇怪，我好像一直把你看成一种忙忙碌碌的元素性存在，没有普通生物那些需求。”</p>
<p>“我看你也是一模一样！”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“我想这是不可避免的，”奖品说。“人总容易把外星生物想成——怎么说呢——从里到外都是实心的，而且没有肠子。当然，有些外星生物确实如此。”</p>
<p>“你的需求我会照顾，”卡莫迪说，忽然对自己的奖品生出一阵亲近感。“等这场该死的紧急事件一结束，我马上安排。”</p>
<p>“当然，老兄。原谅我刚才发了点小脾气。你介意我把午饭吃完吗？”</p>
<p>“请便。”卡莫迪说。他其实挺好奇，一个金属坩埚究竟会怎么吃掉一只剥皮动物；可真到了要看的时候，他又嫌恶心得受不了。</p>
<p>“啊，真他妈好吃，”奖品说。“我给你留了一点，你要不要尝尝？”</p>
<p>“我现在不太饿，”卡莫迪说。“你吃的到底是什么？”</p>
<p>“我们叫它们 orithi，”奖品说。“按你们的概念，大概会把它看成一种巨型蘑菇。生吃很美味，或者用它自身的汁液稍微汆一下也很好。白色带斑的比绿色品种更好吃。”</p>
<p>“我记住了，”卡莫迪说，“万一以后真碰上一只。你觉得地球人能吃吗？”</p>
<p>“我想可以，”奖品说。“顺便说一句，你要真有机会吃，一定要先让它给你朗诵一首诗。”</p>
<p>“为什么？”</p>
<p>“因为 orithi 都是很优秀的诗人。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪艰难地咽了口唾沫。这就是异域生命形态最麻烦的地方：每当你以为自己终于理解了什么，马上就会发现自己其实完全没懂；反过来，每当你觉得一切都神秘得无从理解，它们又会突然做出某件完全可以理解的事，把你的判断重新打乱。事实上，卡莫迪得出结论：外星人之所以如此彻底地“外星”，恰恰在于它们并非彻底地外星。一开始这挺有趣；时间久了就让人神经衰弱。</p>
<p>“呃——嗝。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“什么？”</p>
<p>“我打了个嗝，”奖品说。“失礼了。总之，我想你必须承认，我把那整件事处理得相当聪明。”</p>
<p>“你处理什么了？”</p>
<p>“当然是和梅利克隆的会面。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“你处理的？该死，你当时明明在冬眠！是我靠说话把我们从那困境里救出来的！”</p>
<p>“我不想反驳你，”奖品说，“但恐怕你对情况有些误解。我进入冬眠，唯一的目的，就是把自己的全部能力集中到梅利克隆这个问题上。”</p>
<p>“你疯了！你脑子出毛病了！”卡莫迪大喊。</p>
<p>“我说的不过是事实，”奖品说。“想一想你那篇漫长而推理严密的论证。你用不可反驳的逻辑，确立了梅利克隆在万物体系中的位置和职能。”</p>
<p>“那又怎么了？”</p>
<p>“你这辈子以前曾经那样推理过吗？你是哲学家还是逻辑学家？”</p>
<p>“我大学主修哲学。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“了不起哦。”奖品冷笑。“不，卡莫迪，你根本没有足够的背景或智力来处理那种论证。面对现实吧：那完全不符合你的性格和能力。”</p>
<p>“怎么不符合！我完全有能力作出非同寻常的逻辑推理！”</p>
<p>“‘非同寻常’这个词倒用得不错。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“可那确实是我做的！那些念头是我想出来的！”</p>
<p>“随你高兴吧，”奖品说。“我不知道这件事对你那么重要，更不是故意想刺激你。告诉我，你以前会不会突然晕厥，或者无缘无故大笑、痛哭？”</p>
<p>“从来没有。”卡莫迪说，重新控制住自己。“那你有没有反复梦见自己在飞，或者突然觉得自己圣洁无比？”</p>
<p>“我当然从来没有！”奖品说。</p>
<p>“确定？”</p>
<p>“当然确定！”</p>
<p>“那我们就没必要再讨论这个问题了。”卡莫迪说，心里产生了一种荒唐的胜利感。“不过我还想知道另一件事。”</p>
<p>“什么？”奖品警觉地问。</p>
<p>“梅利克隆那个我绝对不该提的残障到底是什么？还有，他唯一的限制是什么？”</p>
<p>“我以为两件事都明显得让人难受。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“对我一点也不明显。”</p>
<p>“你只要反思几个小时，立刻就会想到。”</p>
<p>“去他的反思，”卡莫迪说。“直接告诉我。”</p>
<p>“好吧，”奖品说。“梅利克隆唯一的残障，是他跛脚。那是先天缺陷，从他最早的起源阶段就存在；无论后来怎样改变形态，这种缺陷都会以相应形式持续存在。”</p>
<p>“那他唯一的限制呢？”</p>
<p>“他永远无法知道自己跛脚。作为一个上帝，他不具备比较性的知识。他的造物都是依照自己的形象创造；对梅利克隆来说，这意味着它们全都跛脚。而他和外部现实接触得又少得可怜，所以他一直认为跛脚才是正常状态，反而觉得那些不跛的生物带有一种奇怪缺陷。顺便说，比较性知识正是神性少数几项缺失之一。因此，上帝最基本的定义是自足性；而这种自足无论范围多大，都始终属于内部。对一切可控制之物拥有完美控制，对一切可知之物拥有完美知识——顺便说，这就是成为上帝最初的几个步骤，万一你哪天想试试这个项目。”</p>
<p>“我？试着当上帝？”</p>
<p>“为什么不？”奖品问。“抛开那个听起来很宏大的头衔，它也不过是一种职业。确实不容易；但它并不比成为一流诗人或工程师更难。”</p>
<p>“我觉得你一定疯了。”卡莫迪说。他感到一阵迅速而颤栗的宗教性恐惧——这种东西尤其喜欢折磨无神论者。</p>
<p>“一点也没有。我只是比你知道得多。不过现在你最好做好准备。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪迅速环顾四周，看见三个矮小的人影正在草地上缓缓走来。离他们一段恭敬的距离，另有十个人影跟在后面。</p>
<p>“中间那个就是莫兹利，”奖品说。“他永远很忙，不过也许有时间跟你说几句话。”</p>
<p>“他有没有什么限制或者缺陷？”卡莫迪讽刺地问。</p>
<p>“即使有，也不重要，”奖品说。“和莫兹利打交道，需要完全不同的一套方式，也会面对完全不同的问题。”</p>
<p>“他看起来像个人类。”那群人渐渐走近时，卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“外形像人类，”奖品承认。“不过当然，类人形态在银河这一带非常常见。”</p>
<p>“那我到底应该用什么方式跟他打交道？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“我实在没法描述，”奖品说。“莫兹利对我来说都太异质了，我无法理解或预测。不过有一条建议可以给你：一定要设法引起他的注意，并让他对你作为‘人类’这一点留下深刻印象。”</p>
<p>“那当然。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“听起来容易，做起来没那么简单。莫兹利是个忙得不得了的存在，脑子里同时装着大量事情。你知道，他是个天赋极高的工程师，而且极其投入。但他很容易心不在焉，尤其是在试验新工艺的时候。”</p>
<p>“听起来也不算太严重。”</p>
<p>“对莫兹利来说不严重。如果不是因为他一走神，就容易把周围一切都当成自己工艺流程的原材料，那顶多只能算个有趣的小怪癖。我有个熟人，叫杜尔·哈丁，很久以前拿着一份聚会邀请去找莫兹利。可怜的杜尔没能成功吸引他的注意。”</p>
<p>“后来怎么样？”</p>
<p>“莫兹利把他加工进了自己的一个项目。当然，完全没有恶意。不过可怜的杜尔如今成了某台往复式发动机里的三个活塞和一根凸轮轴；工作日可以去莫兹利的‘历史动力应用博物馆’参观他。”</p>
<p>“这也太吓人了，”卡莫迪说。“难道没人能管管吗？”</p>
<p>“谁也不想把这件事提醒给莫兹利，”奖品说。“莫兹利最讨厌承认自己犯错；如果觉得别人一直追着他纠缠，他会变得相当不好相处。”</p>
<p>奖品显然察觉到了卡莫迪脸上的表情，于是赶紧补充：“不过你千万别因此害怕！莫兹利从来不恶毒，事实上心地相当不错。和我们所有人一样，他喜欢赞扬，却讨厌谄媚。你只要大声说话，让他知道你的存在；表现出欣赏，但别肉麻吹捧；对你不喜欢的东西可以提出异议，但别固执地批评；简而言之，凡事保持适度——除非某种更极端的态度显然更合适。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪很想说，这种建议和没建议一样；事实上还更糟，因为它唯一的效果就是让他紧张。可现在已经没时间了。莫兹利到了：他身材高大，一头白发，穿着斜纹棉布裤和皮夹克，两侧各走着一个穿商务西装的男人；他正激烈地和两人交谈。</p>
<p>“您好，先生。”卡莫迪坚定地说。他向前迈了一步，随后赶在那三个完全没注意到他的家伙撞上来之前，赶紧侧身窜开。</p>
<p>“开局很糟。”奖品低声说。</p>
<p>“闭嘴。”卡莫迪也低声回了一句。随后，他带着某种咬牙切齿的决心，急忙追上那群人。</p>

<p><b>CHAPTER 10</b></p>
<p>Carmody found himself on a green meadow. It must have been noon, for a gleaming orange sun was directly overhead. Some distance away, a small herd of spotted cows grazed slowly over tall grass. Beyond them, Carmody could see a dark fringe of forest.</p>
<p>He turned around slowly. Meadowland extended on all sides of him, but the forest ended in dense underbrush. He heard a dog bark. There were mountains on the other side, a long, jagged range with snow-capped tops. Grey clouds clung to the upper slopes. Out of the corner of his eye, he saw a flash of red. He turned; it seemed to be a fox. It looked at him curiously, then bounded away towards the forest.</p>
<p>‘It’s like Earth,’ Carmody remarked. Then he remembered the Prize, which had last been a hibernating green snake. He felt around his neck, but the Prize was no longer there.</p>
<p>‘Here I am,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>Carmody looked around and saw a small copper cauldron.</p>
<p>‘Is this you?’ Carmody asked, picking the cauldron up.</p>
<p>‘Of course it’s me,’ the Prize said. ‘Can’t you even recognize your own Prize?’</p>
<p>‘Well … you’ve changed quite a lot.’</p>
<p>‘I am quite aware of that,’ the Prize said. ‘But my essence – the true me – never changes. What’s the matter?’</p>
<p>Carmody had peered into the cauldron and had nearly dropped it. Inside, he had seen the skinned and half-consumed body of some small animal – perhaps a kitten.</p>
<p>‘What’s that inside you?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘It’s my lunch, if you must know,’ the Prize said. ‘I was grabbing a quick bite during transit.’</p>
<p>‘Oh.’</p>
<p>‘Even Prizes need occasional nourishment,’ the Prize added sarcastically. ‘And, I might add, we also need rest, mild exercise, sexual congress, intermittent intoxication and an occasional bowel movement; none of which you have made provision for since I was awarded to you.’</p>
<p>‘Well, I haven’t had any of those things either,’ Carmody answered.</p>
<p>‘Do you really require them?’ the Prize said in an astonished voice. ‘Yes, of course, I suppose you do. It’s strange, but I guess I had thought of you as a sort of bustling elemental figure without creature requirements.’</p>
<p>‘Exactly the way I had thought of you!’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘It’s inevitable, I suppose,’ the Prize said. ‘One tends to think of an alien as – as solid all through and <i>bowelless,</i> somehow. And of course, some aliens are.’</p>
<p>‘I’ll take care of your requirements,’ Carmody said, feeling a sudden affection for his Prize. ‘I’ll do it as soon as this damned emergency is over.’</p>
<p>‘Of course, old man. Forgive my fit of pique. D’ye mind if I finish my bite of lunch?’</p>
<p>‘Go right ahead,’ Carmody said. He was curious to see how a metal cauldron would devour a skinned animal; but when it came down to it, he was too squeamish to watch.</p>
<p>‘Ah, that was damned good,’ the Prize said. ‘I’ve saved a bit for you, if you’d care for some.’</p>
<p>‘I’m not too hungry just now,’ Carmody said. ‘What are you eating?’</p>
<p>‘We call them <i>orithi</i>,’ the Prize said. ‘You would consider them a type of giant mushroom. Delicious raw or lightly poached in their own juices. The mottled white kind is better than the green.’</p>
<p>‘I’ll remember that,’ Carmody said, ‘in case I ever run across one. Do you think an Earthman could eat one?’</p>
<p>‘I think so,’ the Prize said. ‘By the way, if you ever do get the chance, be sure to have it recite a poem before you eat it.’</p>
<p>‘Why?’</p>
<p>‘Because the <i>orithi</i> are very good poets.’</p>
<p>Carmody swallowed hard. That was the trouble with exotic life-forms; just when you thought you understood something, you found that you didn’t understand at all. And conversely, when you thought you were completely mystified, they suddenly threw you off balance by acting in a completely comprehensible manner. In fact, Carmody decided, what made aliens so thoroughly alien was the fact that they weren’t <i>completely</i> alien. It was amusing at first; but after a while it got on your nerves.</p>
<p>‘Urp,’ said the Prize.</p>
<p>‘What?’</p>
<p>‘I belched,’ the Prize said. ‘Excuse me. Anyhow, I think you must admit that I handled it all rather cleverly.’</p>
<p>‘Handled all what?’</p>
<p>‘The interview with Melichrone, of course,’ said the Prize.</p>
<p>‘You handled it? Why, damn it all, <i>you</i> were hibernating! <i>I</i> talked us out of that spot!’</p>
<p>‘I don’t want to contradict you,’ the Prize said, ‘but I fear that you are labouring under a misapprehension. I went into hibernation solely in order to bring all of my powers to bear on the problem of Melichrone.’</p>
<p>‘You’re crazy! You’re out of your mind!’ Carmody shouted.</p>
<p>‘I am saying no more than the truth,’ the Prize said. ‘Consider that long, closely reasoned argument that you gave, in which you established Melichrone’s place and function in the scheme of things by irrefutable logic.’</p>
<p>‘What about it?’</p>
<p>‘Well, have you ever reasoned that way before in your life? Are you a philosopher or a logician?’</p>
<p>‘I was a philosophy major in college,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Big deal,’ the Prize sneered. ‘No, Carmody, you simply don’t have the background or the intellect to handle an argument like that. Face it: it was completely out of character.’</p>
<p>‘It was not out of character! I’m perfectly capable of extraordinary logic!’</p>
<p>‘“Extraordinary” would be a good word for it,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘But I did do it! I thought those thoughts!’</p>
<p>‘Just as you wish,’ the Prize said. ‘I hadn’t realized it meant so much to you, and I certainly didn’t mean to upset you. Tell me, have you ever been subject to fainting spells, or to inexplicable fits of laughter or tears?’</p>
<p>‘No, I have not,’ Carmody said, getting a grip on himself. ‘Have you ever had recurrent dreams of flying, or sensations of saintliness?’</p>
<p>‘I most certainly have not!’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘You’re sure?’</p>
<p>‘Sure, I’m sure!’</p>
<p>‘Then we needn’t discuss the matter any further,’ Carmody said, feeling absurdly triumphant. ‘But I’d like to know something else.’</p>
<p>‘What is that?’the Prize said warily.</p>
<p>‘What was Melichrone’s disability that I was not supposed to mention? And what was his one limitation?’</p>
<p>‘I thought that both were painfully obvious,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘Not to me.’</p>
<p>‘A few hours’ reflection would bring them to your mind at once.’</p>
<p>‘To hell with that,’ Carmody said. ‘Just tell me.’</p>
<p>‘Very well,’ the Prize said. ‘Melichrone’s one disability is that he is lame. It is a congenital defect; it has been present since his early origins. It persists throughout all his changes in analogous form.’</p>
<p>‘And his one limitation?’</p>
<p>‘He can never know about his own lameness. As a God, he is denied comparative knowledge. His creations are in his own image; which, in Melichrone’s case, means that they are all lame. And his contacts with exterior reality are so few that he believes that lameness is the norm, and that unlame creatures are curiously flawed. Comparative knowledge is one of the few deficiencies of Godhead, by the way. Thus, the primary definition of a God is in terms of his self-sufficiency, which, no matter what its scope, is always interior. Perfect control of the controllable and perfect knowledge of the knowable are the first steps towards becoming a God, by the way, in case you ever wish to try the project.’</p>
<p>‘Me? Try to be a God?’</p>
<p>‘Why not?’ the Prize asked him. ‘It’s an occupation like any other, despite its grand-sounding title. It’s not easy, I’ll grant you; but it’s no harder than becoming a first-class poet or engineer.’</p>
<p>‘I think you must be out of your mind,’ Carmody said, feeling the quick shuddering shock of religious horror which so bedevils the atheistic.</p>
<p>‘Not at all. I am merely better informed than you. But now you had better prepare yourself.’</p>
<p>Carmody looked around quickly and saw three small figures walking slowly across the meadow. Following them at a respectful distance were ten other figures.</p>
<p>‘The one in the middle is Maudsley,’ the Prize said. ‘He’s always very busy, but he may have time for a few words with you.’</p>
<p>‘Does he have any limitations or defects?’ Carmody said sarcastically.</p>
<p>‘If he does, they are not of significance,’ the Prize said. ‘One deals with Maudsley in quite different terms, and faces entirely different problems.’</p>
<p>‘He looks like a human,’ Carmody said as the group came nearer.</p>
<p>‘He is shaped like one,’ the Prize admitted. ‘But of course, the humanoid shape is common in this part of the galaxy.’</p>
<p>‘What terms am I supposed to deal with him in?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘I can’t really describe them,’ the Prize said. ‘Maudsley is too alien for me to understand or predict. But there <i>is</i> one piece of advice I can give you: be sure you get his attention and impress him with your humanity.’</p>
<p>‘Well, of course,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘It’s not so simple as it sounds. Maudsley is an extremely busy being with a great deal on his mind. He is a highly gifted engineer, you know, and a dedicated one. But he tends to be absentminded, especially when he is trying out a new process.’</p>
<p>‘Well, that doesn’t sound too serious.’</p>
<p>‘It’s not – for Maudsley. It could be considered no more than an amusing foible, if it were not for the fact that he absentmindedly tends to view everything as raw material for his processes. An acquaintance of mine, Dewer Harding, came to him some time ago with an invitation to a party. Poor Dewer failed to capture his attention.’</p>
<p>‘And what happened?’</p>
<p>‘Maudsley processed him into one of his projects. Quite without malice, of course. Still, poor Dewer is now three pistons and a camshaft in a reciprocating engine, and can be seen weekdays in Maudsley’s Museum of Historical Power Applications.’</p>
<p>‘That’s really quite shocking,’ Carmody said. ‘Can’t anyone do anything about it?’</p>
<p>‘No one wants to bring it to Maudsley’s attention,’ the Prize said. ‘Maudsley hates to admit an error and can be quite unpleasant if he feels he’s being chivvied.’</p>
<p>The Prize must have perceived the look on Carmody’s face, for he quickly added, ‘But you mustn’t let that alarm you! Maudsley is never vicious, and is in fact quite a good-hearted fellow. He likes praise, as do all of us; but he detests flattery. Just speak up and make yourself known, be admiring but avoid the fulsome, take exception to what you don’t like, but don’t be stubbornly critical; in short, exercise moderation except where a more extreme attitude is clearly called for.’</p>
<p>Carmody wanted to say that this advice was as good as no advice at all; worse, in fact, since it merely served to make him nervous. But now there was no time. Maudsley was here, tall and white-haired, in chinos and a leather jacket, flanked by two men in business suits with whom he was talking vehemently.</p>
<p>‘Good day, sir,’ Carmody said firmly. He stepped forward, then scuttled out of the way before the oblivious trio ran into him.</p>
<p>‘A bad beginning,’ the Prize whispered.</p>
<p>‘Shut up,’ Carmody whispered back. With a certain grimness, he hurred after the group.</p>
