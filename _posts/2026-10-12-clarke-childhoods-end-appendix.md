<h2>APPENDIX</h2>
<p><em>In 1989, the author updated the first chapter of</em> Childhood’s End <em>to reflect the common belief that the Russians—rather than the Germans—would be leading in space. After the breakup of the Soviet Union, he decided to revert back to the original text. Following is the revised 1989 version.</em></p>
<p>Before she flew to the launch site, Helena Lyakhov always went through the same ritual. She was not the only cosmonaut who did so, though few ever talked about it.</p>
<p>It was already dark when she left the Administration Building and walked past the pine trees, until she came to the famous statue. The sky was crystal clear, and a brilliant full moon had just risen. Automatically, Helena’s eyes focused on the Mare Imbrium, and her mind went back to the weeks of training at Armstrong Base—now better known as Little Mars.</p>
<p>“You died before I was born, Yuri—back in the days of the Cold War, while our country was still under the shadow of Stalin. What would you have thought, if you heard all the foreign accents in Star Village today? I think you would have been very happy…</p>
<p>“I <em>know</em> you would have been happy, if you could have watched us now; you would have been an old man, but you might still have been alive. What a tragedy that you—the very first to enter space—never lived to see men walk on the moon! But you too must have dreamed of Mars…</p>
<p>“And now we are ready to go there, and open the New Age of which Konstantin Tailolkovsky dreamed, a hundred years ago. When we meet again, I will have much to tell you.”</p>
<p>She was already halfway back to her office when a busload of belated tourists came to a sudden halt. The doors opened, and the passengers poured out, cameras at the ready. There was nothing that the Deputy Commander of the Mars Expedition could do but summon up her public smile.</p>
<p>Then, before a single photograph could be taken, everyone started shouting and pointing at the moon. Helena turned just in time to see it disappear behind the gigantic shadow sliding across the sky; and for the first time in her life, she felt the fear of God.</p>
<p>Mission Commander Mohan Kaleer stood on the rim of the crater, looking out across the sea of frozen lava to the far rim of the caldera. It was hard to grasp the scale of the scene, or to imagine the powers that had raged here as the tides of molten rock ebbed and flowed, creating the ramparts and terraces spread out before him. Yet everything he saw could be lost inside the stupendous volcano he must face less than a year from now; Kilauea was merely a scale-model of Olympus Mons, and all their training might leave them hopelessly unprepared for the reality.</p>
<p>He remembered how, on Inauguration Day, 2001, the President of the United States had echoed Kennedy’s forty-year-old promise “We must go to the moon!” by proclaiming that this would be the “Century of the Solar System.” Before 2100 dawned, he had confidently predicted, men would have visited all the major bodies orbiting the sun—and would be living permanently on at least one of them.</p>
<p>The rays of the just-risen sun were catching the wisps of steam emerging from cracks in the lava, and Dr. Kaleer was reminded of the morning mists that gathered in the Labyrinth of Night. Yes, it was easy to believe that he was already on Mars, with his colleagues from half a dozen countries. This time, no one nation would—or indeed could—go it alone.</p>
<p>He was returning to the helicopter when some premonition, some movement glimpsed out of the corner of his eye, made him stop. Puzzled, he glanced back into the crater; it was some little time before he thought of looking at the sky.</p>
<p>Then Mohan Kaleer realized, as did Helena Lyakhov at this same moment, that history as men had known it had come to an end. The glittering monsters sailing beyond the clouds, more miles above his head than he dared to guess, made the little cluster of spacecraft up there at Lagrange seem as primitive as log canoes. For a moment that seemed to last forever, Mohan watched, as all the world was watching, while the great ships descended in their overwhelming majesty.</p>
<p>He felt no regrets as the work of a lifetime was swept away. He had labored to take man to the stars, and now the stars—the aloof, indifferent stars—had come to him.</p>
<p>This was the moment when history held its breath, and the present sheared asunder from the past as an iceberg splits from its parent cliffs, and goes sailing out to sea in lonely pride. All that the past ages had achieved was as nothing now; only one thought echoed and re-echoed through Mohan’s brain:</p>
<p>The human race was no longer alone.</p>
