<h3>第十二章　荒野中的约翰</h3>
<p>约翰说，当他开始意识到智人的悲剧性徒劳时，忽然被“一种惊慌失措的末日感”攫住，同时还伴随着“一股孤独的激情”。同别人待在一起时，他反而比独处时更觉得孤独。与此同时，他自己的心灵里似乎也发生着某种奇怪的变化。最初他怀疑自己是不是疯了，但仍死死相信，也许自己终究只是在长大。无论如何，他确信自己必须彻底斩断一切联系，不受干扰地独自面对内心这场剧变。仿佛一条幼虫预感到自己即将经历溶解与再生，于是有意识地开始给自己织茧。</p>
<p>此外，如果我理解得不错，他觉得自己在精神上已经被同智人文明的接触污染了。他认为，至少有一段时间，必须从自己身上剥掉这个文明留下的一切痕迹；必须彻底赤裸地面对宇宙，证明自己能够独立站立，无须以任何方式依赖那些统治着这颗行星的原始而堕落的生物。起先，我以为这种对简朴生活的饥渴，不过是替一场少年式冒险寻找借口；现在我才明白，这件事对他确实具有一种严肃的重要性，只是我所能理解的非常有限。</p>
<p>大致就是这些动机，把他赶进了这座岛最荒凉的地区。他执行计划时的彻底程度让我吃惊。他就这么从苏格兰高地的一座火车站走出来，在旅店里好好吃了一顿，随后大步穿过荒原，朝高山走去。等到判断自己已经不会再受人打扰时，他脱掉全部衣服，包括鞋子，把它们埋进岩石间的一个坑里。他仔细辨认方位，以便将来到了适当时候能把东西找回来，随后便赤身裸体地离开，在荒野里寻找食物和住处。</p>
<p>最初几天显然是一场可怕的考验。天气转冷，又下起雨。必须记住，约翰本来就是一种极其强健耐受的生物；为了这次冒险，他此前还专门进行过耐寒与暴露训练，并事先研究过：如果手里连一把刀、一根绳子都没有，怎样在苏格兰的山谷和荒原里取得食物。可命运起初并不站在他这一边。坏天气使住处成了必需品，而为了寻找庇护，他不得不耗费本来可以用来觅食的大量时间。</p>
<p>第一晚，他睡在一块伸出的岩石下面，用天气恶化前收集好的石南和草把自己裹住。第二天，他抓到一只青蛙，用尖石头肢解，生吃下去。他还吃了大量蒲公英叶和其他绿叶植物，这些都是他根据先前研究知道可以食用的。某些菌类也成了当天、乃至整个冒险期间的食物来源。到了第二天，他已经觉得“很不对劲”。第三天晚上，他发起高烧，咳嗽严重，而且腹泻。前一天，他预见到自己可能生病，已经大大改善了藏身处，并储存了一批自己认为最不伤胃的食物。接下来好几天——具体多久，他自己也不知道——他病得非常厉害，几乎连爬到溪边喝水的力气都没有。“有一阵我一定烧糊涂了，”他说，“因为我仿佛看见帕克斯来看我。后来我清醒过来，发现根本没有帕克斯；我以为自己就要死了，于是发疯似的爱惜自己，因为我知道，我的确是一件稀有而明亮的东西。就这么白白浪费掉，简直是一种酷刑。然后，那种不可言说的喜悦来了——仿佛借着上帝的眼睛来看一切，并发现它们归根到底都是对的，都恰当地处在整幅图景里。”接着是几天康复期。他说：“那几天，我仿佛完全同这次冒险原本的全部动机失去了联系。我只是躺在那里，纳闷自己为什么曾经是个那么自命不凡的蠢货。幸好，在我恢复到足以爬回文明世界以前，我强迫自己正面迎战这种精神上的衰败。因为即使在最卑微的状态里，我也模模糊糊知道，某个地方还有另一个‘我’，而且是一个更好的我。于是我咬紧牙关，下决心把这件事继续做完，哪怕它会要我的命。”</p>
<p>就在他作出这个决定不久，几个当地男孩带着一条狗上山，正好撞到他的藏身处。约翰一跃而起，转身就逃。他们一定瞥见了这个小小的裸体身影，因为立刻兴奋地大叫着追了上去。可他刚站起来就发现，自己的两条腿像水一样软。他一下子瘫倒在地。“可紧接着，”他说，“我突然像是从某个深层储备里抽出了一股生命力。我一下跳起来，拼命跑过一处山坡拐角，又一直跑到一片岩石地带。那里有一段相当难的岩壁，我爬进一个早就发现、也一直留作后手的洞里。然后我大概就昏过去了。事实上，我想自己差不多失去意识整整二十四小时，因为醒来时，太阳看上去又回到了清晨的位置。我冷得像死人，全身就是一整块疼痛，虚弱得连扭曲着躺成的那个姿势都动不了。”</p>
<p>当天晚些时候，他设法爬回原先的住处，又费尽力气把铺盖搬到一个更安全、但没那么舒服的位置。天气此时转为炎热晴朗。大约十天里，他几乎把所有时间都用来爬来爬去寻找青蛙、蜥蜴、蜗牛、鸟蛋和各种可食植物，或者干脆躺在太阳下恢复体力。有时，他能在河里的水潭中用手“挠”鱼，把鱼抓住。他整整花了一天，试图用石头相击，在一把干草上打出火星。最后总算成功了，他兴奋而骄傲地开始烹煮食物。突然，他注意到远处有个人，显然已经对火堆冒出的烟产生了兴趣。他立刻把火扑灭，并决定向荒野更深处迁移。</p>
<p>与此同时，尽管他在家里早已通过长期训练把脚底磨硬，现在双脚仍然伤得厉害，完全不适合继续“徒步旅行”。他用绞紧的草绳做成软鞋，缠在脚和脚踝上。它们一时能固定住，可不是不断松开，就是很快磨穿。经过许多天探索，又露宿了几个晚上——其中有两晚还下雨——他终于找到了后来攀岩者发现他的那个高处洞穴。“真是刚好赶上，”他说，“那时候我已经很惨了。脚肿得流血，咳得要命，又拉肚子。可一进那个洞，跟过去几周相比，我很快就觉得自己一辈子从没这么安逸过。我给自己铺了一张漂亮的床，又弄了个炉子，而且觉得相当不容易被打扰，因为我待的是一座很偏僻的山，何况能爬上那些岩壁的人本来就极少。下方不远有红松鸡和雷鸟，还有鹿。头一个早晨，我坐在洞顶晒太阳，简直幸福极了，看见一群鹿穿过荒原。它们步子那么轻巧，耳朵张开，头昂得高高的。”</p>
<p>有一阵，鹿似乎成了他最主要的兴趣。他被它们的美与自由迷住了。不错，如今这些鹿的生存要依赖一个奢华文明；可同样，它们早在任何文明出现以前就已经存在。更何况，杀死一头雄鹿之后能得到的大量物资，也让他垂涎。除此之外，他显然还有一种古怪欲望，想用自己的力量和狡猾去挑战一头配得上的猎物。那一时期，他几乎甘愿彻底做一个原始猎人，“不过脑子很深的地方仍记着：这一切只是在把精神洗干净，为另一项完全不同的事业作准备。”</p>
<p>大约十天里，他几乎只干两件事：设计捕鸟、捕野兔的办法；剩下时间则用来休息、恢复，并反复想着那些鹿。他在许多次失败以后，终于捕到第一只野兔：他在兔子常走的路径上设置陷阱，用一根细木棍勉强支住一块巨石，兔子一碰掉木棍，巨石便落下，折断了它的脊背。可当晚，一只狐狸吃掉了绝大部分兔肉。不过，约翰仍用兔皮做出一根粗糙的弓弦，又给自己的鞋做了鞋底和皮带。他劈开兔子的腿骨，在岩石上磨削，制成几把脆弱的小刀，帮助处理食物；还磨出一些极小而锋利的箭头。靠着各种陷阱、玩具似的弓箭，以及惊人的耐心和天赋，他终于捕获了足够多的猎物，把身体恢复到正常强度。几乎全部时间都花在打猎、设陷阱、做饭，以及用骨、木或石头制作小工具。每晚，他都累得精疲力竭，卷进草铺里，却十分安宁。有时，他会把铺盖搬到洞外，睡在悬崖的一块岩架上，头顶是群星和疾驰的云。</p>
<p>但鹿始终在那里；在鹿的背后，则是那道精神问题——它才是这次冒险真正的动机，而他至今还完全没有自觉地正面处理它。很明显，如果不大幅改善自己的生活方式，他根本不会有时间去进行自己极其需要的集中冥想与精神操练。于是，杀死那头雄鹿逐渐成了一个象征。这个念头激起他一些从未有过的感情。“仿佛过去所有时代的猎人都在向我挑战，”他说，“又仿佛——仿佛——嗯，像是上帝的天使命令我先做成这件小小而伟大的事，好为将来更伟大的事业作准备。我会梦见雄鹿，梦见它们的美、力量和速度。我设计一个又一个方案，又把它们统统否决。我空着手跟踪鹿群，只为了研究它们的习性。有一天，我碰到几个猎鹿人，于是也跟踪起他们，直到他们打倒一头十叉雄鹿；那种轻而易举的屠杀让我鄙视透了。在我看来，他们不过是侵害我猎物的害虫。可刚想到这里，我就笑自己，因为我对那些动物并不比任何别人拥有更多权利。”</p>
<p>约翰最后猎得那头雄鹿的故事，在我看来几乎难以置信，可我又不能不信。他选中的猎物是鹿群中最出色的一头：一头八岁的王者，除眉叉、冰叉和中叉之外，右边鹿角顶部还有三叉，左边则有四叉。沉重的鹿角让它昂首时显得异常庄严。一天，约翰同这头雄鹿在荒原一处肩状坡后面迎面相遇。彼此相距二十步，足足站了三秒钟，互相凝视；雄鹿张大的鼻孔吸取着他的气味。随后，那动物一转身，从容小跑着离开。</p>
<p>约翰描述这次相遇时，那双古怪的眼睛仿佛燃起暗色火焰。我记得他说：“我在灵魂里向它致敬。然后我怜悯它，因为它注定要死，而且正值壮年。可我想起，我自己也同样注定要死。我忽然知道，自己永远到不了壮年。于是我为了它，也为了自己，大声笑了起来，因为生命短暂而狂野，而死亡也在这幅图景之中。”</p>
<p>约翰花了很久才决定怎样发动袭击。挖坑？用兽皮绳套住它？布置一块巨石把它砸倒？还是用骨尖箭射穿它？这些办法很少有真正可行的；除了最后一种以外，其余看起来都很难看，而最后一种又办不到。有一阵，他忙着制作各种短匕首：木头的、野兔脆骨的，还有从附近山上取得的锋利石片。经过耐心试验，他终于做出一柄荒唐的小刺刀：硬木刀身，尖端装骨刺，整件东西都在岩石上磨得十分流线。他打算凭这件怪异武器，加上自己的解剖学知识，从藏身处跳到雄鹿背上，刺穿它的心脏。经过许多天毫无结果的跟踪与守候，最终他真的这样做了。有一片小林间空地，鹿群偶尔会在那里吃草，旁边是一块大约十英尺高的岩石。一天清晨，他早早藏到岩顶；当天风向正好，不会把他的气味吹给鹿群。那头巨大的雄鹿绕过山肩走来，身边跟着三头母鹿。它们小心地嗅闻、窥探；最后终于低下头，安静吃起草来。约翰一小时又一小时地趴着，等待正确的那头鹿走到岩石下方。可那雄鹿仿佛故意避开危险点。最后四头鹿离开了空地。接下来又白等了两天。直到第四天，约翰才终于从岩石上跳下，扑到正在吃草的雄鹿背上，把它压倒，右侧身体着地。那动物还没来得及重新站起来，约翰已经用全身重量把原始武器深深刺进去。雄鹿半撑起身子，疯狂挥动鹿角，撕伤了约翰的手臂，随后便倒下。出乎约翰自己意料的是，他接下来表现得一点也不像个合格猎人：这是他一生第三次，不由自主地放声痛哭。</p>
<p>随后好几天里，他都在用那些简陋工具拼命肢解尸体。这件工作甚至比杀鹿本身还困难；不过最后，他总算得到大量肉食、一整张极其宝贵的鹿皮，以及鹿角。为了把鹿角变成刀和其他工具，他费尽力气用大石头把它砸成碎块，再贴着岩石反复磨削。</p>
<p>完成这一切时，他已经累得连手都几乎抬不起来，掌上全是磨破流血的水泡。可事情终于做成了。历代所有猎人都在向他致敬，因为他完成了一件他们谁也不可能完成的事。一个孩子，赤身裸体走进荒野，并且征服了它。天堂的天使朝他微笑，又招手叫他走向一场更高的冒险。</p>
<p>从此，约翰的生活方式改变了。维持生命、甚至过得相当舒适，对他而言已经并不困难。他照常布陷阱、射箭、采集绿叶植物，可这些都成了例行工作。他能够一边处理这些事务，一边把最好的注意力放到自己心灵中那些刚开始发生的奇异而令人不安的事件上。</p>
<p>显然，我绝不可能真实地叙述约翰在荒野中经历的精神层面冒险。可如果把它完全略去，就等于略去了约翰身上最独特的一切。我至少必须试着写下自己能够理解的部分；因为即便只有这么一点，在我看来，对我自己这个物种的成员也有真正意义。即使事实上我完全误解了他告诉我的话，这种误解至少给我本人带来过真正的启明。</p>
<p>有一阵，他似乎主要专注于艺术。他“同瀑布对唱”。他制作排箫，吹奏它，显然采用了一套自己发明的古怪音阶。他在湖岸、森林、山顶和岩洞家园里吹出奇异的主题与乐句。他在工具上刻下同其形状与用途相协调的角线和曲线。又在骨片和石片上，用象征形式记录自己同鱼、鸟以及那头雄鹿之间的冒险。他设计出一些奇怪的形状，对他而言，这些形状概括了智人的悲剧，以及自己物种的希望。与此同时，他也任由周围那些感知形式深深进入自己的心灵。他以极强的洞察力接受荒原、天空与峭壁本身的质地。从心底里，他感谢同物质现实之间这些细微的接触，并从中获得一种精神恢复；这种恢复我们其实也能获得，只是更混乱，也更不情愿。他还不断、而且总是出乎意料地，被自己猎杀的兽与鸟之美照亮——那种美同时意味着它们的力量与脆弱、生命力与迟钝。这些活生生的有机形式似乎比我所能理解的程度更深地撼动他。尤其是那头被他杀死、吃掉、而且如今每天都在使用其身体残余的雄鹿，对他似乎具有某种深刻象征意义，我只能依稀体会，也不打算强行描述。我记得他曾惊呼：“我多么真正认识了它，又多么赞美它！而它的死亡，正是它生命的王冠。”</p>
<p>我想，这句话概括了约翰当时正在获得的一种新启明：关于他自己，关于智人，甚至关于一切生命。我无法设想那种启明的真实性质，但其中某些模糊反光，我似乎还能捕捉到，因此必须试着记下来。</p>
<p>读者应该还记得，即使在婴儿时期，约翰在那些自己遭受痛苦的情形中，也表现出令人惊讶的超脱和一种奇异的兴味。现在谈起这一点，他说：“我一直能够欣赏自己疼痛与悲伤的‘真实感’和清晰轮廓，哪怕同时也痛恨它们。可是现在，我碰到了一种全新层次的恐怖，而且怎么也无法把它放进应有的位置。过去，我的痛苦不过是孤立的刺痛和暂时的受挫；可现在，我看见自己的整个未来：它会比我曾经设想的一切都更加鲜明，同时也更加痛苦地遭到挫败。你知道，到这时我已经非常清楚，自己是一个独一无二的存在，比其他人清醒得多。我正在开始理解自己，发现自身各种崭新而精妙的能力；与此同时，我也看得太清楚了：我面对的是一个野蛮物种，它永远不会容忍我和我的同类，迟早会凭借粗暴的重量把我们碾碎。于是我对自己说，这终究没什么要紧，我不过是个自命不凡的小微生物，为一点小事大惊小怪；可我身体里的某个东西却专横地喊起来：即使我本人无足轻重，我能够做出的那些事情、我能够创造的美，还有我刚刚开始理解的那种崇拜，却极端重要，必须得到实现。可我又看见，它们不会实现；那些本该由我去完成的精妙事物，永远不会被完成。这是一种我少年心灵过去从未体验过的痛苦，性质完全不同。”</p>
<p>当他还在同这种恐怖搏斗，而且尚未战胜它时，他忽然意识到：对正常物种的成员而言，身体和心灵的一切疼痛、一切悲苦，都带着这种无法跨越的丑恶性质；而他自己只在经验的最高层次上遇到过这种东西，并且即便在那里也决心征服它。这个发现像一次震惊性的启示：普通人根本无法在纯属个人层面的痛苦中仍保持超脱与兴味。他第一次清楚认识到，那些比野兽更敏感、更觉醒，却又没有敏感到足够程度、没有彻底觉醒的生物，几乎每走一步都可能落入怎样的酷刑。想到这个由噩梦缠身的半人构成的世界，所承受的痛苦把他压垮了；从前没有任何事情能像这样压垮他。</p>
<p>他对普通物种的态度正在发生巨大改变。逃进荒野时，他最主要的反应是厌恶。我们当中有一两个人，被他毫无道理地珍爱着；可作为一个物种，他憎恨我们。此前他看得太多，靠得太近，已经被弄脏、被毒害。他对人类世界的研究，对一个虽然本质更优、却仍不成熟而脆弱的心灵而言，破坏性实在太强。可荒野把他洗净了，治好了他，又让他恢复清醒。现在，他能够把智人放在一臂之外，冷静研究，也能够欣赏。于是他看见：这种生物固然绝非神圣，可归根到底仍是一种高贵、甚至可爱的动物——事实上，是所有动物中最高贵、最可爱的一种。更进一步说，它之所以令人厌恶，恰恰因为它已经不只是动物，却又没有超越得足够远。现在，约翰终于毫无不甘地承认：一个普通人确实是比任何兽类都更高阶的精神存在，尽管大体上依旧迟钝、无情，而且背叛着自己内心最好的部分。</p>
<p>认识到这一切，又第一次真正意识到智人没有能力平静接受自身的疼痛和悲伤以后，约翰忽然被怜悯淹没。这种激情，他此前从未强烈体验过；只有极个别场合例外，比如朱迪的狗被汽车撞死，或者帕克斯生病、承受剧痛的时候。可即使在那些时刻，他的怜悯也总被一个假设缓和：他以为任何人——哪怕小朱迪——都总能够像自己一样，“站到外面去看这件事，并且欣赏它”。</p>
<p>接下来许多天，约翰似乎一直在同这个刚刚意识到的问题搏斗：邪恶竟会显得如此绝对；而那些由愚蠢和卑劣编织而成的生物，却仍然值得怜悯，而且以自己的方式拥有美。他寻找的不是一个理智上的答案，而是一种情感上的启明。渐渐地，他似乎真的获得了它。我追问他，这种奇怪的启明究竟是什么，他说，无非是“用我从小看待磕碰、烫伤和失望的方式，看待我自己的命运，以及普通物种可怜的处境。就是去欣赏它们清楚的形状，欣赏它们同其余一切的统一，以及它们怎样——怎么说呢？——让宇宙变得更深，也更有生命。”我记得，说到这里约翰停了停，又重复道：“对，让宇宙更深，也更有生命——这才是重点。不过不是‘理解’它们确实产生这种作用，而是直接看见、感觉到它们就是如此。”</p>
<p>我问他，他的意思是不是某种与上帝面对面的经验。他笑了，说：“我对上帝知道什么？不比坎特伯雷大主教多，而那就是什么都不知道。”</p>
<p>他说，麦克惠斯特和诺顿在洞里发现他时，他“仍在拼命把这些东西想清楚”。他们的出现，让他一瞬间重新生出过去那种对这个物种的厌恶；可那种阶段实际上早就过去了。当他看见这两个人站在那里、一脸茫然时，他想起了自己第一次同那头雄鹿近距离对视的情形。突然间，雄鹿仿佛成了整个普通人类物种的象征：一种拥有自身伟大美感和尊严、拥有自身正确性的生物，只要别把它硬塞进超出它能力的处境里。可怜的智人偏偏跌进了一个过于困难的处境——也就是当下这个世界局势。想到智人努力操纵一个机械化文明，他忽然觉得既可笑又可怜，就像想象一头雄鹿坐在汽车驾驶座上一样。</p>
<p>我趁机问起那个给两位访客留下如此深刻印象的“奇迹”。他又笑了。“嗯，”他说，“那时我正在发现各种稀奇古怪的能力。比如，我发现自己可以通过一种类似心灵感应的方式联系帕克斯，同她交谈。这是真的。你可以去问她。有时，我还能感觉到你正在想什么，只不过你太迟钝，收不到我发出的信息，更别说回应了。我还做过一些奇怪的小旅行，回到自己过去生命里的事件。我会把它们重新活一遍，鲜明得就像那些事情正在‘现在’发生。还有，我通过某种心灵感应的方式，开始获得很像证据的东西，说明世界上终究并不只有我一个同类；事实上，有相当数量的我们，散布在不同国家。再有，当麦克惠斯特和诺顿走进洞穴时，我发现只要看着他们的脸，就能读出他们过去的整个一生，而且看见，在他们所属物种的限制以内，两个人其实都极为健全！我想我还看见了他们未来的一些东西，不过那部分我不告诉你。后来，在必须给他们留下深刻印象的时候，我忽然想到：不如把洞顶举起来，再把暴风雪清走，让我们看看星星。我非常清楚自己办得到，所以就做了。”</p>
<p>我带着疑虑望着约翰。“哦，是的，”他说，“我知道你觉得我疯了，而且认为我所做的不过是催眠了他们。好吧，那就说我连自己也一起催眠了，因为我跟他们一样清清楚楚看见了整件事。可是相信我，说‘我催眠了我们三个人’，既不比说‘我真的挪开了巨石和暴风雪’更真，也不比它更假。事情真正的真相，要比任何简简单单的物理奇迹都精微得多，也巨大得多。不过别管这个。重要的是，当我真正看见星星时——它们按照自身狂野天性的任性，疯狂地朝各个方向疾驰，可每一次运动又都在证实法则——那团一直折磨我的纠结恐怖，终于以它真实而美丽的形状呈现在我面前。我知道，自己童年最初那个盲目的阶段结束了。”</p>
<p>我确实感觉到约翰发生了变化。就连身体上，他在六个月的离开之后也显著不同：更坚硬，更紧密；脸上还出现一些线条，仿佛暗示他曾经历考验，而且已经胜利通过。在精神上，虽然他仍然能够表现出令人极度不安的顽童气质，却同时获得一种难以定义的安宁与力量；这在普通物种的少年身上完全不可能出现，即便成熟成年人也极少真正获得。约翰自己说，他“发现了纯粹的邪恶”，这反而使他坚固起来。我问：“怎么个坚固法？”他说：“亲爱的，真正面对过最坏的东西，而且感到它本身也是美的一项特征，这会给人极大的力量。从此以后，再没有什么能够动摇你。”</p>
<p>他说得对。我不知道他靠什么魔法做到这一点，但在此后的一生中，甚至当他最珍爱的所有东西最终都被摧毁时，他接受最坏的事物，也不仅仅是靠忍耐或顺从，而是带着一种奇怪的喜悦；这种喜悦对我们其余人来说，恐怕永远无法理解。</p>
<p>我还要提到我同约翰那次长谈里出现的另一个要点。读者应该记得，他施展那个“奇迹”之后，曾为它道歉。我追问这件事，他大意是这样说的：“享受运用自身能力，本来是健康的。孩子喜欢学习走路。艺术家喜欢画画。我还是婴儿时，会为自己能在数字上耍的花样得意；后来为自己的发明得意；最近又为杀死雄鹿得意。当然，充分发挥自身能力，确实是精神生活的一部分。可它只是一部分。有时候，尤其在刚发现新能力时，我们很容易把它误当作存在的全部目的。嗯，在苏格兰，当我开始获得刚才说的那些奇怪能力时，我也一度受到诱惑，以为施展它们才是我生命真正的目标。我对自己说：‘现在终于，我将通过这些奇妙方式真正推进精神。’可是，举起那块岩石所带来的短暂狂喜一过去，我立刻清楚看见：这种行为绝不是精神的目标，只是它真正生活中的一种旁枝——有趣，有时也有用，而且往往危险，却从来不是目标本身。”</p>
<p>“那你告诉我，”我或许有些激动地说，“目标究竟是什么？精神真正的生活是什么？”约翰突然像个十岁男孩一样咧嘴笑起来，又发出他那种该死的、令人不安的笑声。“恐怕我不能告诉你，记者先生，”他说，“你的采访该结束了。即使我真知道精神的真实生活是什么，我也没法把它说成英语，或者任何一种‘智人语言’。就算我说得出来，你也听不懂。”停了一下，他又说：“不过，也许至少可以比较安全地说这么多：它并不是去做某一种特定的事情，比如施展奇迹，甚至也不是做好事。它是去做所有来到面前、需要被完成的事情，而且不只用尽全部力量去做，还要带着——精神上的品味、辨别力，以及对自己正在做什么的充分自觉。对，就是这个。可它还更多。它是——赞美生命，赞美一切事物在其真实位置上的样子。”他又笑了，说：“说得什么鬼话！要描述精神生活，我们得从根基开始把语言整个重造一遍。”</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" lang="en" hidden>
<h3><span id="a342"></span>CHAPTER XII. JOHN IN THE WILDERNESS</h3>
<p><span class="t28">J</span><span class="t31">OHN</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">SAID</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">THAT</span><span class="t30"> </span>when he had begun to realize the tragic futility of Homo sapiens he was seized with “a panicky sense of doom,” and along with that “a passion of loneliness.” He felt more lonely in the presence of others than in isolation. At the same time, apparently, something strange was happening to his own mind. At first he thought perhaps he was going mad, but clung to the faith that he was after all merely growing up. Anyhow he was convinced that he must cut right adrift and face this upheaval in himself undisturbed. It was as though a grub were to feel premonitions of dissolution and regeneration, and to set purposefully about protecting itself with a cocoon.</p>
<p>Further, if I understood him, he felt spiritually contaminated by contact with the civilization of Homo sapiens. He felt he must for a while at least strip away every vestige of it from his own person, face the universe in absolute nakedness, prove that he could stand by himself, without depending in any way whatever on the primitive and debased creatures who dominated the planet. At first I thought this hunger for the simple life was merely an excuse for a boyish adventure, but now I realize that it did have for him a grave importance which I could only dimly comprehend.</p>
<p>Of some such kind were the motives that drove him into the wildest region of this island. The thoroughness with which he carried out his plan amazed me. He simply walked out of a Highland railway station, had a good meal in an inn, strode up on to the moors toward the high mountains, and, when he judged himself safe from interruption, took off all his clothes, including his shoes, and buried them in a hole among the rocks. He then took his bearings carefully, so as to be able to recover his property in due season, and moved away in his nakedness, seeking food and shelter in the wilderness.</p>
<p>His first days were evidently a terrible ordeal. The weather turned wet and cold. John, it must be remembered, was an extremely hardy creature, and he had prepared himself for this adventure by a course of exposure, and by studying beforehand all possible means of securing food in the valleys and moors of Scotland without so much as a knife or a piece of string to aid him. But fate was at first against him. The bad weather made shelter a necessity, and in seeking it he had to spend much time that might otherwise have been spent in the search for food.</p>
<p>He passed the first night under a projecting rock, wrapped in heather and grass that he had collected before the weather broke. Next day he caught a frog, dismembered it with a sharp stone, and ate it raw. He also ate large quantities of dandelion leaves, and other green stuff which from previous study he knew to be edible. Certain fungi, too, contributed to his diet on that day, and indeed throughout his adventure. On the second day he was feeling “pretty queer.” On the third evening he was in a high fever, with a bad cough and diarrhœa. On the previous day, foreseeing possible illness, he had greatly improved his shelter, and laid by a store of such food as he considered least indigestible. For some days, he didn’t know how long, he lay desperately sick, scarcely able to crawl to the stream for water. “I must have been delirious at one time,” he said, “because I seemed to have a visit from Pax. Then I came to and found there wasn’t any Pax, and I thought I was dying, and I loved myself desperately, knowing I was indeed a rare bright thing. And it was torture to be just wasted like that. And then that unspeakable joy came, that joy of seeing things as it were through God’s eyes, and finding them after all right, fitting, in the picture.” There followed a few days of convalescence, during which, he said, “I seemed to have lost touch completely with all the motives of my adventure. I just lay and wondered why I had been such a self-important fool. Fortunately, before I was strong enough to crawl back to civilization I lashed myself into facing this spiritual decay. For even in my most abject state I vaguely knew that somewhere there was another ‘I,’ and a better one. Well, I set my teeth and determined to go on with the job even if it killed me.”</p>
<p>Soon after he had come to this decision some local boys with a dog came up the hill right on to his hiding-place. He leapt up and fled. They must have caught a glimpse of his small naked figure, for they gave chase, hallooing excitedly. As soon as he was on his feet he realized that his legs were like water. He collapsed. “But then,” he said, “I suddenly managed to tap some deep reserve of vitality, so to speak. I simply jumped up and ran like hell round a corner of hillside, and farther, to a rocky place. There I climbed a pretty bad pitch into a hole I knew of and had counted on. Then I must have fainted. In fact, I think I must have lain unconscious for almost twenty-four hours, for when I came to, the sun seemed to have gone back to early morning. I was cold as death, and one huge ache, and so weak I couldn’t move from the twisted position I was in.”</p>
<p>Later in the day he managed to crawl back to his lair, and with great difficulty moved his bedding to a safer but less comfortable spot. The weather was now hot and bright. For ten days or so he spent nearly all his time creeping about in search of frogs, lizards, snails, birds’ eggs and green stuff, or just lying in the sun recovering his strength. Sometimes he managed to catch a few fish by “tickling” them in a pool in the river. The whole of one day he spent in trying to get a flame by striking sparks from stones on a handful of dry grass. At last he succeeded, and began to cook his meal, in an ecstasy of pride and anticipation. Suddenly he noticed a man, far away but obviously interested in the smoke of his fire. He put it out at once and decided to go much farther into the wilds.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, though his feet had been hardened with long practice at home, they were now terribly sore, and quite unfit for “a walking tour.” He made moccasins out of ropes of twisted grass which he bound round his feet and ankles. They kept in place for a while, but were always either coming undone or wearing through. After many days of exploration, and several nights without shelter, two of which were wet, he found the high cave where later the climbers discovered him. “It was only just in time,” he said. “I was in a pretty bad state. Feet swollen and bleeding, ghastly cough, diarrhœa. But in that cave I soon felt snugger than I had ever felt in my life, by contrast with the past few weeks. I made myself a lovely bed, and a fireplace, and I felt fairly safe from intrusion, because mine was a remote mountain, and anyhow very few people could climb those rocks. Not far below there were grouse and ptarmigan; and deer. On my first morning, sitting in the sun on my roof, positively happy, I watched a herd of them crossing a moor, stepping so finely, ears spread, heads high.”</p>
<p>The deer seem to have become his chief interest for a while. He was fascinated by their beauty and freedom. True, they now depended for their existence on a luxurious civilization; but equally they had existed before there was any civilization at all. Moreover, he coveted the huge material wealth that the slaughter of one stag would afford him. And he had apparently a queer lust to try his strength and cunning against a worthy quarry. For at this time he was content to be almost wholly the primitive hunter, “though with a recollection, away at the back of my mind, that all this was just a process of getting clean in spirit for a very different enterprise.”</p>
<p>For ten days or so he did little but devise means for catching birds and hares, and spent all spare time in resting, recuperating, and brooding over the deer. His first hare, caught after many failures, he took by arranging a trap in its runway. A huge stone was precariously held in position by a light stick, which the creature dislodged. Its back was broken. But a fox ate most of it in the night. From its skin, however, he made a rough bow-string, also soles and thongs for his foot-gear. By splitting its thigh bones and filing them on the rock he made some fragile knives to help him in preparing his food. Also he made some minute sharp arrowheads. With a diversity of traps, and his toy bow and arrows, and vast patience and aptitude, he managed to secure enough game to restore him to normal strength. Practically his whole time was spent in hunting, trapping, cooking, making little tools of bone or wood or stone. Every night he rolled himself in his grass bedding dead tired, but at peace. Sometimes he took his bedding outside the cave and slept on a ledge of the precipice, under stars and driving cloud.</p>
<p>But there were the deer; and beyond them the spiritual problem which was the real motive of his adventure, and had not yet been consciously faced at all. It was clear that if he did not greatly improve his way of life, he would have no time for that concentrated meditation and spiritual exercise which he so greatly needed. The killing of the stag became a symbol for him. The thought of it stirred unwonted feelings in him. “It was as though all the hunters of the past challenged me,” he said, “and as though, as though — well, as though the angels of God ordered me to do this little mighty deed in preparation for mightier deeds to come. I dreamt of stags, of their beauty and power and speed. I schemed and plotted, and rejected every plan. I stalked the herd, weaponless, intent only on learning their ways. One day I came upon some deer-stalkers, and I stalked them too, until they brought down a stag of ten; and how I despised them for their easy slaughter. To me they were just vermin preying upon my game. But when I had thought that thought, I laughed at myself; for I had no more right to the creatures than any one else.”</p>
<p>The story of how John finally took his stag seemed to me almost incredible, yet I could not but believe it. He had marked down as his quarry the finest beast of the herd, an eight-year-old monarch, bearing besides his brow, bay and tray, “three on top” on the right, and four on the left. The weight of antler gave his head a superb poise. One day John and the stag met one another face to face round a shoulder of moor. They stood for full three seconds, twenty paces apart, gazing at one another, the stag’s wide nostrils taking the scent of him. Then the beast swung round and cantered easefully away.</p>
<p>When John described that meeting, his strange eyes seemed to glow with dark fire. He said, I remember, “With my soul I saluted him. Then I pitied him, because he was doomed, and in the prime. But I remembered that I too was doomed. I suddenly knew that I should never reach my prime. And I laughed aloud, for him and for me, because life is brief and wild, and death too is in the picture.”</p>
<p>John took long to decide on the method of his attack. Should he dig a pit for him, or lasso him with a cord of hide, or set a mighty stone to fell him, or pierce him with a bone-pointed arrow? Few of these devices seemed practicable; all but the last seemed ugly, and that last was not practicable. For some time he busied himself making dirks of various kinds, of wood, of the fragile bones of hares, of keen stone splinters from a neighbouring mountain. Patient experiment produced at last a preposterous little stiletto of hard wood pointed with bone, the whole “stream-lined” by filing upon the rock. With this fantastic weapon and his knowledge of anatomy, he proposed to leap on the stag from a hiding-place and pierce its heart. And this in the end he did, after many days of fruitless stalking and waiting. There was a little glade where the deer sometimes grazed, and beside it a rock some ten feet high. On the top of this rock he secreted himself early one morning, when the wind was such that his scent would not betray him. The great stag came round the shoulder of hill, attended by three hinds. Cautiously they sniffed and peered; then, at last, lowered their heads and peacefully grazed. Hour after hour John lay, waiting for the right beast to stray below the rock. But it was as though the stag deliberately avoided the danger-spot. Finally the four deer left the glade. Two more days were spent in vain watching. Not till the fourth day did John leap from the rock upon the back of the grazing stag, bringing it down with its right flank to the ground. Before the creature could regain its feet John had thrust his primitive weapon home with all his weight. The stag half righted itself, wildly swung its antlers, tearing John’s arm, then collapsed. And John, to his own surprise, behaved in a style most unseemly in a hunter. For the third time in his life, he burst into spontaneous tears.</p>
<p>For days afterwards he struggled to dismember the carcass with his inadequate implements. This task proved even more difficult than the killing, but in the end he found himself with a large quantity of meat, an invaluable hide, and the antlers, which, with desperate efforts, he smashed in pieces with a great stone and worked up as knives and other tools, by scraping them on the rocks.</p>
<p>At the end he could hardly lift his hands with fatigue, and they were covered with bleeding blisters. But the deed was accomplished. The hunters of all the ages saluted him, for he had done what none of them could have done. A child, he had gone naked into the wilderness and conquered it. And the angels of heaven smiled at him, and beckoned him to a higher adventure.</p>
<p>John’s way of life now changed. It had become a fairly easy matter for him to keep himself alive, and even in comfort. He set his traps, and let fly his arrows, and gathered his green things; but all was now routine work. He was able to carry it out while giving his best attention to the strange and disturbing events which were beginning to occur within his own mind.</p>
<p>It is obviously quite impossible for me to give anything like a true account of the spiritual side of John’s adventure in the wilderness. Yet to ignore it would be to ignore all that was most distinctive in John. I must at least try to set down as much of it as I was able to understand, for that little seems to me to have real significance for beings of my own species. Even if as a matter of fact I have merely misunderstood what he told me, my misunderstanding afforded at least to me a real enlightenment.</p>
<p>For a time he seems to have been chiefly concerned with art. He “sang against the waterfall.” He made and played his pan-pipes, apparently adopting some weird scale of his own. He played strange themes and figures on the shores of the loch, in the woods, on the mountain-tops, and in his rock home. He decorated his tools with engraved angles and curves consonant with their form and use. On pieces of bone and stone he recorded symbolically his adventures with fish and birds; and with the stag. He devised strange shapes which epitomized for him the tragedy of Homo sapiens, and the promise of his own kind. At the same time he was allowing the perceptual forms with which he was surrounded to work themselves deeply into his mind. He accepted with insight the quality of moor and sky and crag. From the bottom of his heart he gave thanks for all these subtle contacts with material reality; and found in them a spiritual refreshment which we also find, though confusedly and grudgingly. He was also constantly, and ever surprisingly, illuminated by the beauty of the beasts and birds on which he preyed, a beauty significant of their power and their frailty, their vitality and their obtuseness. Such perceived organic forms seem to have moved him far more deeply than I could comprehend. The stag, in particular, that he had killed and devoured, and now daily used, seems to have had some deep symbolism for him which I could but dimly appreciate, and will not attempt to describe. I remember his exclaiming, “How I knew him and praised him! And his death was his life’s crown.”</p>
<p>This remark epitomized, I believe, some new enlightenment which John was now receiving about himself and about Homo sapiens and indeed about all living things. The actual nature of that enlightenment I find it impossible to conceive, but certain dim reflections of it I do seem able to detect, and must try to record.</p>
<p>It will be remembered that John had shown, even as an infant, a surprising detachment and strange relish in situations in which he himself was the sufferer. Referring to this, he now said, “I could always enjoy the ‘realness’ and the neatness of my own pains and griefs, even while I detested them. But now I found myself faced with something of quite a new order of horribleness, and one which I could not get into place. Hitherto my distresses had been merely isolated smarts and temporary frustrations, but now I saw my whole future as something at once much more vivid and much more painfully frustrated than anything I had conceived. You see, I knew so clearly by now that I was a unique being, far more awake than other people. I was beginning to understand myself and discover all sorts of new and exquisite capacities in myself; and at the same time I saw now all too clearly that I was up against a savage race which would never tolerate me or my kind, and would sooner or later smash me with its brute weight. And when I told myself that after all this didn’t really matter, and that I was just a little self-important microbe making a fuss over nothing, something in me cried out imperiously that, even if I was of no account, the things I could do, the beauty I could make, and the worship that I was now beginning to conceive, did most emphatically matter, and must be brought to fruition. And I saw that there would be no fruition, that the exquisite things that it was my office to do would never be done. This was a sort of agony altogether different from anything that my adolescent mind had ever known.”</p>
<p>While he was wrestling with this horror, and before he had triumphed over it, there came upon him the realization that for members of the normal species every pain, every distress of body and mind, had this character of insurmountable hideousness which he himself had found only in respect of the highest reach of his experience, and was determined to conquer even there. It came as a shocking revelation to him that normal human beings were quite incapable of detachment and zest even in sufferings upon the personal plane. In fact he realized clearly for the first time the torture that lies in wait at every turn for beings who are more sensitive and more awakened than the beasts and yet not sensitive enough, not fully awakened. The thought of the agony of this world of nightmare-ridden half-men crushed him as nothing else had ever done.</p>
<p>His attitude to the normal species was undergoing a great change. When he had fled into the wilderness his dominant reaction was disgust. One or two of us he unreasonably cherished, but as a species he loathed us. He had recently seen too much, lived at too close quarters, been fouled and poisoned. His researches into the world of men had been too devastating for a mind which, though superior in quality, was immature and delicate. But the wild had cleansed him, healed him, brought him to sanity again. He could now put Homo sapiens at arm’s length for study and appreciation. And he saw that, though no divinity, the creature was after all a noble and even a lovable beast, indeed the noblest and most lovable of them all; nay further, that its very repulsiveness lay in its being something more than beast, but not enough more. A normal human being, he now ungrudgingly admitted, was indeed a spirit of a higher order than any beast, though in the main obtuse, heartless, unfaithful to the best in himself.</p>
<p>Realizing all this, and realizing for the first time the incapacity of Homo sapiens to accept his pains and sorrows with equanimity, John was overwhelmed with pity, a passion which he had not hitherto experienced in any intensity; save on particular occasions, as when Judy’s dog was run over by a car, and when Pax was ill and in great pain. And even then his pity was always tempered by his assumption that every one, even little Judy, could always “look at it from outside and enjoy it,” as he himself could do.</p>
<p>For many days John seems to have been at grips with this newly realized problem of the absoluteness of evil, and the novel fact that beings that were tissues of folly and baseness could yet be pitiable and, in their kind, beautiful. What he sought was not an intellectual solution but an emotional enlightenment. And this, little by little, he seems to have gained. When I pressed him to tell me more of this strange enlightenment, he said it was just “seeing my own fate and the piteous plight of the normal species in the same way as I had always, since I was a kid, seen bumps and burns and disappointments. It was a case of delighting in their clear-cut form, and in their unity with the rest of things, and the way they — how shall I put it? — deepened and quickened the universe.” Here, I remember, John paused, then repeated, “Yes, deepened and quickened the universe, — that’s the main point. But it wasn’t a case of understanding that they did so, but just seeing and feeling that they did so.”</p>
<p>I asked him if what he meant was some kind of coming face to face with God. He laughed, and said, “What do I know about God? No more than the Archbishop of Canterbury, and that’s nothing whatever.”</p>
<p>He said that when McWhist and Norton came upon him in the cave he was “still desperately puzzling things out,” and that their presence filled him just for a moment with the old disgust at their species, but that he had really done with all that long ago, and when he saw them there, looking so blank, he remembered his first close meeting with the stag. And suddenly the stag seemed to symbolize the whole normal human species, as a thing with a great beauty and dignity of its own, and a rightness of its own, so long as it was not put into situations too difficult for it. Homo sapiens, poor thing, had floundered into a situation too difficult for him, namely the present world-situation. The thought of Homo sapiens trying to run a mechanized civilization suddenly seemed to him as ludicrous and pathetic as the thought of a stag in the driving-seat of a motor-car.</p>
<p>I took this opportunity of asking him about the “miracle” with which he had so impressed his visitors. He laughed again. “Well,” he said, “I had been discovering all sorts of odd powers. For instance I found that by a kind of telepathy I could get in touch with Pax and talk to her. It’s true. You can ask her about it. Also I could sometimes feel what you were thinking about, though you were too dull to catch my messages and respond to me. And I had made queer little visits to events in my own past life. I just lived them again, with full vividness, as though they were ‘now.’ And in a telepathic way I had begun to get something very like evidence that after all I was not the only one of my kind in the world, that there were in fact quite a number of us scattered about in different countries. And then again, when McWhist and Norton appeared in the cave I found that by looking at them I could read the whole of their past lives in their faces, and I saw how thoroughly sound they both were within the limitations of their kind! And I think I saw something about their future, something that I won’t tell you. Then, when it was necessary to impress them, I suddenly got the idea of lifting the roof and clearing away the blizzard so that we could see the stars. And I knew perfectly well that I could do it, so I did it.”</p>
<p>I looked at John with misgiving. “Oh yes,” he said, “I know you think I’m mad, and that all I did was to hypnotize them. Well, put it that I hypnotized myself too, for I saw the whole thing as clearly as they did. But, believe me, to say I hypnotized us all is no more true and no less true than to say I actually shifted the rock and the blizzard. The truth of the matter was something much more subtle and tremendous than any plain little physical miracle could ever be. But never mind that. The important thing was that, when I did see the stars (riotously darting in all directions according to the caprice of their own wild natures, yet in every movement confirming the law), the whole tangled horror that had tormented me finally presented itself to me in its true and beautiful shape. And I knew that the first, blind stage of my childhood had ended.”</p>
<p>I had indeed sensed a change in John. Even physically he had altered strikingly during his six months’ absence. He was harder, more close-knit; and there were lines on his face suggestive of ordeals triumphantly passed. Mentally, though still capable of a most disconcerting impishness, he had also acquired that indefinable peacefulness and strength which is quite impossible to the adolescent of the normal species, and is very seldom acquired even by the mature. He himself said that his “discovery of sheer evil” had fortified him. When I asked, “How fortified?” he said, “My dear, it is a great strength to have faced the worst and to have felt it a feature of beauty. Nothing ever after can shake one.”</p>
<p>He was right. By what magic he did it I do not know, but in all his future, and in the final destruction of all that he most cherished, he accepted the worst not with resignation, merely, but with a strange joy that must remain to the rest of us incomprehensible.</p>
<p>I will mention one other point that emerged in my long talk with John. It will be remembered that after performing his “miracle” he apologized for it. I questioned him on this matter, and he said something like this: “To enjoy exercising one’s powers is healthy. Children enjoy learning to walk. Artists enjoy painting pictures. As a baby I exulted in the tricks I could do with numbers, and later in my inventions, and recently in killing my stag. And of course the full exercise of one’s powers really is part of the life of the spirit. But it is only a part, and sometimes we are inclined to take it as the whole purpose of our existence, especially when we discover new powers. Well, in Scotland, when I began to come into all those queer powers that I mentioned just now, I was tempted to regard the exercise of them as the true end of my life. I said to myself, ‘Now at last, in these wonderful ways, I shall indeed advance the spirit.’ But after the momentary exaltation of lifting the rock I saw clearly that such acts were in no sense the goal of the spirit, but just a by-play of its true life, amusing, and sometimes useful, and often dangerous, but never themselves the goal.”</p>
<p>“Then tell me,” I said, perhaps rather excitedly, “what is the goal, the true life of the spirit?” John suddenly grinned like a boy of ten, and laughed that damnably disturbing laugh of his. “I’m afraid I can’t tell you, Mr. Journalist,” he said. “It is time your interview was concluded. Even if I knew what the true life of the spirit was, I couldn’t put it into English, or any ‘sapient’ language. And if I could, you wouldn’t understand.” After a pause he added, “Perhaps we might safely say this much about it anyhow. It’s not doing any one particular kind of thing, like miracles, or even good deeds. It’s doing everything that comes along to be done, and doing it not only with all one’s might but with — spiritual taste, discrimination, full consciousness of what one is doing. Yes, it’s that. And it’s more. It’s — praise of life, and of all things in their true setting.” Once more he laughed, and said, “What stuff! To describe the spiritual life, we should have to remake language from the foundations upwards.”</p>
