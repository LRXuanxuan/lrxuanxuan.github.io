<h2>9</h2>
<h2><em>A Robot is Stymied</em></h2>
<p>Baley said, ‘So the higher potential wins out again, Daneel. You will hurt me to keep me alive.’</p>
<p>‘I do not believe hurting you will be necessary. You know that I am superior to you in strength and you will not attempt a useless resistance. If it should become necessary, however, I shall be compelled to hurt you.’</p>
<p>‘I could blast you down where you stand,’ said Baley. ‘There is nothing in <em>my</em> potentials to prevent me.’</p>
<p>‘I had thought you might take this attitude at some time in our present relationship, Partner Elijah. Most particularly, the thought occurred to me during our trip to this mansion, when you grew momentarily violent in the ground-car. The destruction of myself is unimportant in comparison with your safety, but such destruction would cause you distress eventually and disturb the plans of my masters. It was one of my first cares, therefore, during your first sleeping period, to deprive your blaster of its charge.’</p>
<p>Baley’s lips tightened. He was left without a charged blaster! His hand dropped instantly to his holster. He drew his weapon and stared at the charge reading. It hugged zero.</p>
<p>For a moment he balanced the lump of useless metal as though to hurl it directly into Daneel’s face. What good? The robot would dodge efficiently.</p>
<p>Baley put the blaster back. It could be recharged in good time.</p>
<p>Slowly, thoughtfully, he said, ‘I’m not fooled by you, Daneel.’</p>
<p>‘In what way, Partner Elijah?’</p>
<p>‘You are too much the master. I am too completely stopped by you. Are you a robot?’</p>
<p>‘You have doubted me before,’ said Daneel.</p>
<p>‘On Earth last year, I doubted whether R. Daneel Olivaw was truly a robot. It turned out he was. I believe he still is. My question, however is this: Are you R. Daneel Olivaw?’</p>
<p>‘I am.’</p>
<p>‘Yes? Daneel was designed to imitate a Spacer closely. Why could not a Spacer be made up to imitate Daneel closely?’</p>
<p>‘For what reason?’</p>
<p>‘To carry on an investigation here with greater initiative and capacity than ever a robot could. And yet by assuming Daneel’s role, you could keep me safely under control by giving me a false consciousness of mastery. After all, you are working through me and I must be kept pliable.’</p>
<p>‘All this is not so, Partner Elijah.’</p>
<p>‘Then why do all the Solarians we meet assume you to be human? They are robotic experts. Are they so easily fooled? It occurs to me that I cannot be one right against many wrong. It is far more likely that I am one wrong against many right.’</p>
<p>‘Not at all, Partner Elijah.’</p>
<p>‘Prove it,’ said Baley, moving slowly towards an end table and lifting a scrap-disposal unit. ‘You can do that easily enough, if you <em>are</em> a robot. Show the metal beneath your skin.’</p>
<p>Daneel said, ‘I assure you—’</p>
<p>‘Show the metal,’ said Baley crisply. ‘That is an order. Or don’t you feel compelled to obey orders?’</p>
<p>Daneel unbuttoned his shirt. The smooth, bronze skin of his chest was sparsely covered with light hair. Daneel’s fingers exerted a firm pressure just under the right nipple, and flesh and skin split bloodlessly the length of the chest, with the gleam of metal showing beneath.</p>
<p>And as that happened, Baley’s fingers, resting on the end table, moved half an inch to the right and stabbed at a contact patch. Almost at once a robot entered.</p>
<p>‘Don’t move, Daneel,’ cried Baley. ‘That’s an order! Freeze!’</p>
<p>Daneel stood motionless, as though life, or the robotic imitation thereof, had departed from him.</p>
<p>Baley shouted to the robot. ‘Can you get two more of the staff in here without yourself leaving? If so, do it.’</p>
<p>The robot said, ‘Yes, master.’</p>
<p>Two more robots entered, answering a radioed call. The three lined up abreast.</p>
<p>‘Boys!’ said Baley. ‘Do you see this creature whom you thought a master?’</p>
<p>Six ruddy eyes had turned solemnly on Daneel. They said in unison, ‘We see him, master.’</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘Do you also see that this so-called master is actually a robot like yourself since it is metal within? It is only designed to look like a man.’</p>
<p>‘Yes, master.’</p>
<p>‘You are not required to obey any orders it gives you. Do you understand that?’</p>
<p>‘Yes, master.’</p>
<p>‘I, on the other hand,’ said Baley, ‘am a true man.’</p>
<p>For a moment the robots hesitated. Baley wondered if, having had it shown to them that a thing might seem a man yet be a robot, they would accept <em>anything</em> in human appearance as a man, anything at all.</p>
<p>But then one robot said, ‘You are a man, master,’ and Baley drew breath again.</p>
<p>He said, ‘Very well, Daneel. You may relax.’</p>
<p>Daneel moved into a more natural position and said calmly, ‘Your expressed doubt as to my identity, then, was merely a feint designed to exhibit my nature to these others, I take it.’</p>
<p>‘So it was,’ said Baley, and looked away. He thought: The thing is a machine, not a man. You can’t double-cross a machine.</p>
<p>And yet he couldn’t entirely repress a feeling of shame. Even as Daneel stood there, chest open, there seemed something so human about him, something capable of being betrayed.</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘Close your chest, Daneel, and listen to me. Physically, you are no match for three robots. You see that, don’t you?’</p>
<p>‘That is clear, Partner Elijah.’</p>
<p>‘Good! … Now you boys,’ and he turned to the other robots again. ‘You are to tell no one, human or master, that this creature is a robot. Never at any time, without further instructions from myself and myself alone.’</p>
<p>‘I thank you,’ interposed Daneel softly.</p>
<p>‘However,’ Baley went on, ‘this man-like robot is not to be allowed to interfere with my actions in any way. If it attempts any such interference, you will restrain it by force, taking care not to damage it unless absolutely necessary. Do not allow it to establish contact with humans other than myself, or with robots other than yourselves, either by seeing or by viewing. And do not leave it at any time. Keep it in this room and remain here yourselves. Your other duties are suspended until further notice. Is all this clear?’</p>
<p>‘Yes, master,’ they chorused.</p>
<p>Baley turned to Dancel again. ‘There is nothing you can do now, so don’t try to stop me.’</p>
<p>Daneel’s arms hung loosely at his side. He said, ‘I may not, through inaction, allow you to come to harm, Partner Elijah. Yet in the circumstances, nothing but inaction is possible. The logic is unassailable. I shall do nothing. I trust you will remain safe and in good health.’</p>
<p>There it was, thought Baley. Logic was logic and robots had nothing else. Logic told Daneel he was completely stymied. Reason might have told him that all factors are rarely predictable, that the opposition might make a mistake.</p>
<p>None of that. A robot is logical, only, not reasonable.</p>
<p>Again Baley felt a twinge of shame and could not forbear an attempt at consolation. He said, ‘Look, Daneel, even if I were walking into danger, <em>which I’m not</em>’ (he added that hurriedly, with a quick glance at the other robots) ‘it would only be my job. It is what I’m paid to do. It is as much my job to prevent harm to man-kind as a whole as yours is to prevent harm to a man as an individual. Do you see?’</p>
<p>‘I do not, Partner Elijah.’</p>
<p>‘Then that is because you’re not made to see. Take my word for it that if you were a man, you would see.’</p>
<p>Daneel bowed his head in acquiescence and remained standing, motionless, while Baley walked slowly towards the door of the room. The three robots parted to make room for him and kept their photo-electric eyes fixed firmly on Daneel.</p>
<p>Baley was walking to a kind of freedom and his heart beat rapidly in anticipation of the fact, then skipped a beat. Another robot was approaching the door from the other side.</p>
<p>Had something gone wrong?</p>
<p>‘What is it, boy?’ he snapped.</p>
<p>‘A message has been forwarded to you, master, from the office of Acting Head of Security Attlebish.’</p>
<p>Baley took the personal capsule handed to him and it opened at once. A finely inscribed strip of paper unrolled. He wasn’t startled. Solaria would have his fingerprints on file and the capsule would be adjusted to open at the touch of his particular convolutions.</p>
<p>He read the message and his long face mirrored satisfaction. It was his official permission to arrange ‘seeing’ interviews, subject to the wishes of the interviewees, who were nevertheless urged to give ‘Agents Baley and Olivaw’ every possible co-operation.</p>
<p>Attlebish had capitulated, even to the extent of putting the Earthman’s name first. It was an excellent omen with which to begin, finally, an investigation conducted as it should be conducted.</p>
<p>Baley was in an air-borne vessel again, as he had been on that trip from New York to Washington. This time, however, there was a difference. The vessel was not closed in. The windows were left transparent.</p>
<p>It was a clear, bright day and from where Baley sat the windows were so many patches of blue. Unrelieved, featureless. He tried not to huddle. He buried his head in his knees only when he could absolutely no longer help it.</p>
<p>The ordeal was of his own choosing. His state of triumph, his unusual sense of freedom at having beaten down first Attlebish and then Daneel, his feeling of having asserted the dignity of Earth against the Spacers, almost demanded it.</p>
<p>He had begun by stepping across open ground to the waiting plane with a kind of lightheaded dizziness that was almost enjoyable, and he had ordered the windows left unblanked in a kind of manic self-confidence.</p>
<p>I have to get used to it, he thought, and stared at the blue until his heart beat rapidly and the lump in his throat swelled beyond endurance.</p>
<p>He had to close his eyes and bury his head under the protective cover of his arms at shortening intervals. Slowly his confidence trickled away and even the touch of the holster of his freshly recharged blaster could not reverse the flow.</p>
<p>He tried to keep his mind on his plan of attack. First, learn the ways of the planet. Sketch in the background against which everything must be placed or fail to make sense.</p>
<p>Next – see a sociologist!</p>
<p>He had asked a robot for the name of the Solarian most eminent as a sociologist. And there was that comfort about robots; they asked no questions. The robot gave the name and vital statistics, and paused to remark that the sociologist would most probably be at lunch and would, therefore, possibly ask to delay contact.</p>
<p>‘Lunch!’ said Baley sharply. ‘Don’t be ridiculous. It’s not noon by two hours.’</p>
<p>The robot said, ‘I am using local time, master.’</p>
<p>Baley stared, then understood. On Earth, with its buried Cities, day and night, waking and sleeping, were man-made periods, adjusted to suit the needs of the community and the planet. On a planet such as this one, exposed nakedly to the sun, day and night were not a matter of choice at all, but were imposed on man willy-nilly.</p>
<p>Baley tried to picture a world as a sphere being lit and unlit as it turned. He found it hard to do and felt scornful of the so-superior Spacers who let such an essential thing as time be dictated to them by the vagaries of planetary movements.</p>
<p>He said, ‘Contact him, anyway.’</p>
<p>Robots were there to meet the plane when it landed and Baley, stepping out into the open again, found himself trembling badly. He muttered to the nearest of the robots, ‘Let me hold your arm, boy!’</p>
<p>The sociologist waited for him down the length of a hall, smiling tightly. ‘Good afternoon, Mr Baley.’</p>
<p>Baley nodded breathlessly. ‘Good evening, sir. Would you blank out the windows?’</p>
<p>The sociologist said, ‘They are blanked out already. I know something of the ways of Earth. Will you follow me?’</p>
<p>Baley managed it without robotic help, following at a considerable distance, across and through a maze of hallways. When he finally sat down in a large and elaborate room, he was glad of the opportunity to rest.</p>
<p>The walls of the room were set with curved, shallow alcoves. Statuary in pink and gold occupied each niche; abstract figures that pleased the eye without yielding instant meaning. A large, box-like affair with white and dangling cylindrical objects and numerous pedals suggested a musical instrument.</p>
<p>Baley looked at the sociologist standing before him. The Spacer looked precisely as he had when Baley had viewed him earlier that day. He was tall and thin and his hair was pure white.</p>
<p>His face was strikingly wedge-shaped, his nose prominent, his eyes deep-set and alive. His name was Anselmo Quemot.</p>
<p>The two stared at one another until Baley felt he could trust his voice to be reasonably normal; but his first remark had nothing to do with the investigation. In fact it was nothing he had planned. He said, ‘May I have a drink?’</p>
<p>‘A drink?’ The sociologist’s voice was a trifle too high-pitched to be entirely pleasant. He said, ‘You want water?’</p>
<p>‘I’d prefer something alcoholic.’</p>
<p>The sociologist’s look grew sharply uneasy, as though the obligations of hospitality were something with which he was unacquainted.</p>
<p>And that, thought Baley, was literally so. In a world where viewing was the thing, there would be no sharing of food and drink.</p>
<p>A robot brought him a small cup of smooth enamel. The drink was a light pink in colour. Baley sniffed at it cautiously and tasted it even more cautiously. The small sip of liquid evaporated warmly in his mouth and sent a pleasant message along the length of his œsophagus. His next sip was more substantial.</p>
<p>Quemot said, ‘If you want more—’</p>
<p>‘No, thank you, not now. It is good of you, sir, to agree to see me.’</p>
<p>Quemot tried a smile and failed rather markedly, ‘It has been a long time since I’ve done anything like this. Yes.’</p>
<p>He almost squirmed as he spoke.</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘I imagine you find this rather hard?</p>
<p>‘Quite.’ Quemot turned away sharply and retreated to a chair at the opposite end of the room. He angled the chair so that it faced more away from Baley than towards him and sat down. He clasped his gloved hands and his nostrils seemed to quiver.</p>
<p>Baley finished his drink and felt warmth in his limbs and even the return of something of his confidence.</p>
<p>He said, ‘Exactly how <em>does</em> it feel to have me here, Dr Quemot?’</p>
<p>The sociologist muttered, ‘That is an uncommonly personal question.’</p>
<p>‘I know it is. But I think I explained when I viewed you earlier that I was engaged in a murder investigation and that I would have to ask a great many questions, some of which were bound to be personal.’</p>
<p>‘I’ll help if I can,’ said Quemot. ‘I hope the questions will be decent ones.’ He kept looking away as he spoke. His eyes, when they struck Baley’s face, did not linger, but slipped away.</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘I don’t ask about your feelings out of curiosity only. This is essential to the investigation.’</p>
<p>‘I don’t see how.’</p>
<p>‘I’ve got to know as much as I can about this world. I must understand how Solarians feel about ordinary matters. Do you see that?’</p>
<p>Quemot did not look at Baley at all now. He said slowly, ‘Ten years ago, my wife died. Seeing her was never very easy, but, of course, it is something one learns to bear in time and she was not the intrusive sort. I have been assigned no new wife since I am past the age of – of’ – he looked at Baley as though requesting him to supply the phrase, and when Baley did not do so, he continued in a lower voice – ‘siring. Without even a wife, I have grown quite unused to this phenomenon of seeing.’</p>
<p>‘But how does it feel?’ insisted Baley. ‘Are you in panic?’ He thought of himself on the plane.</p>
<p>‘No. Not in panic.’ Quemot angled his head to catch a glimpse of Baley and almost instantly withdrew. ‘But I will be frank, Mr Baley. I imagine I can smell you.’</p>
<p>Baley automatically leaned back in his chair, painfully self-conscious. ‘Smell me?’</p>
<p>‘Quite imaginary, of course,’ said Quemot. ‘I cannot say whether you do have an odour or how strong it is, but even if you had a strong one, my nose filters would keep it from me. Yet, imagination …’ He shrugged his shoulders.</p>
<p>‘I understand.’</p>
<p>‘It’s worse. You’ll forgive me, Mr Baley, but in the actual presence of a human, I feel strongly as though something slimy were about to touch me. I keep shrinking away. It is most unpleasant.’</p>
<p>Baley rubbed his ear thoughtfully and fought to keep down annoyance. After all, it was the other’s neurotic reaction to a simple state of affairs.</p>
<p>He said, ‘If all this is so, I’m surprised you agreed to see me so readily. Surely you anticipated this unpleasantness.’</p>
<p>‘I did. But you know, I was curious. You’re an Earthman.’</p>
<p>Baley thought sardonically that that should have been another argument against seeing, but he said only, ‘What does that matter?’</p>
<p>A kind of jerky enthusiasm entered Quemot’s voice. ‘It’s not something I can explain easily. Not even to myself, really. But I’ve worked on sociology for ten years now. Really worked. I’ve developed propositions that are quite new and startling, and yet basically true. It is one of these propositions that makes me most extraordinarily interested in Earth and Earthmen. You see, if you were to consider Solaria’s society and way of life carefully, it would become obvious to you that the said society and way of life is modelled directly and closely on that of Earth itself.’</p>
