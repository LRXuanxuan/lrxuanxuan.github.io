<h3>第十三章　约翰寻找同类</h3>
<p>从荒野回来后的许多星期里，约翰有相当多时间都待在家里，或者附近那座城市中。看起来，他很满足于重新沉入普通少年的兴趣。他恢复了同斯蒂芬的友谊，也恢复了同朱迪的友谊。他常带着这个孩子去看电影、马戏，或者任何适合她年龄的娱乐节目。他买了一辆摩托车，买到手的当天，就带朱迪来了一次疯狂兜风。邻居们都说，这次假期让约翰变好了。他现在正常多了。甚至同哥哥姐姐相处时——虽然他们已经很少见面——他也变得更像一个兄弟。安妮如今已经结婚，汤姆则成了一名事业顺利的年轻建筑师。兄弟二人过去通常维持着一种克制的敌意，现在敌意似乎也软化成了相互容忍。有次全家团聚后，汤姆评论说：“咱们这个神童总算真的在长大。”医生对约翰新出现的亲近态度非常高兴，经常同他长谈。他们最主要的话题是约翰的前途。医生一心想劝他学医，成为“一个更伟大的李斯特”。约翰总会若有所思地听这些劝说，甚至显得几乎已经被说动了。有一次帕克斯也在场。她笑着、又略带责备地朝约翰摇头。“别信他，医生，”她说，“他在逗你呢。”顺便一提，这一时期约翰和帕克斯常常一起去剧院或音乐会。事实上，母子两人如今相处得很多。帕克斯对戏剧、对“人”的兴趣，似乎给他们提供了一个永远不会枯竭的共同基础。偶尔，他们甚至一起去伦敦过周末，“看演出”。</p>
<p>过了一阵，我开始对这段漫长的放松期究竟意味着什么产生好奇。约翰现在的行为几乎完全正常。诚然，其中仍有一个不寻常、但并不显眼的特点：无论在谈话还是别的活动中，他有时会突然明显地惊一下。接着，他也许会重复刚刚过去的那句话——无论那句话是他自己说的还是别人说的——随后带着好笑的兴趣环顾四周。我总觉得，每发生一次这种情况，在接下来一段时间里，他会比此前更加警觉。倒不是说先前他显得心不在焉；他一直都同周围环境配合得很好。只是每次这种奇怪的震动之后，他生命的电流仿佛都升到了更高的电压。</p>
<p>有天晚上，我陪温赖特一家三口去当地的保留剧目剧院。幕间休息时，我们在大厅喝咖啡、讨论那出戏，约翰忽然比平常更猛烈地一惊，咖啡都洒进了托盘。他笑了起来，带着惊讶的兴趣四处看。尴尬地沉默了片刻；帕克斯带着隐藏很深的担忧看着儿子。接着，约翰继续评论那出戏，可在我看来，他的洞察忽然深了一层。“我的意思就是，”他说，“这东西太像生活，所以反而一点也不活。它不是肖像，是死后面模。”</p>
<p>第二天，我问他昨晚洒咖啡时究竟发生了什么。我们当时在我的公寓里。约翰来问，邮件里有没有寄来某项专利的消息。我坐在写字台前；他站在窗边，越过空荡荡的海滨长廊，看向冬日的大海，一边啃着从我桌上果盘里随手拿的一只苹果。“对，”他说，“是时候告诉你了，就算你不会相信。现在我正在寻找一些或多或少跟我一样的人。为了做到这件事，我会变成某种分裂的人格。我的一部分留在身体所在的地方，而且表现完全正常；可另一部分——真正的我——会出去寻找他们。或者，如果你愿意，也可以说我始终待在原地，只是向外伸出去寻找他们。无论怎么说，当我回来，或者停止搜索时，重新接起日常生活的线头，总会有一点冲击。”</p>
<p>“可你看上去从来没把线头丢掉。”我说。</p>
<p>“没有，”他回答，“那个回来的‘我’，会立刻顺顺当当地接收那个留守者过去所有的经验，可以这么说。可从天知道什么地方一下跳回这里，终究还是会震一下。”</p>
<p>“那你离开的时候，”我问，“去了哪里？找到了什么？”</p>
<p>“嗯，”他说，“最好从头讲。我以前告诉过你，在苏格兰的时候，我常常发现自己能同别人发生心灵感应式的接触；其中有些人显得很古怪，或者说，他们在某种关键意义上比你更像我。回家以后，我一直在练习怎么准确调到我要找的人身上。不幸的是，接收熟人的思想比陌生人容易得多。心灵的总体形式——思想发生的那个母体，可以这么说——影响很大。要接上你或者帕克斯，我只要想到你们就行。我可以接触到你此刻实际的意识；只要愿意，我也能进入你更深的好几层。”</p>
<p>我顿时一阵恐惧，只能拿“不相信”来安慰自己。</p>
<p>“噢，是的，我能。”约翰说，“刚才我讲话的时候，你一半脑子在听，另一半一直在想昨晚你跟——吵的那场架。”我立刻抗议，打断了他。</p>
<p>“好啦，别激动。”约翰说，“你也没什么好羞愧的。再说我也不想窥探。不过刚才——嗯，你简直是在冲我大喊那件事，因为你一边听我说，一边就在想它。大概很快你就能学会什么时候想把我挡在外面，就什么时候挡住。”</p>
<p>我哼了一声。约翰继续说：“像我刚才说的，同不认识的人接触困难得多，而起初我一个要找的人都不认识。另一方面，我发现我这种人在心灵感应上制造的‘噪声’，要比其他人响得多——至少当他们愿意发出声音，或者根本不在乎时如此。可一旦他们不想被听见，就能把自己完全封闭起来。最后，我总算从普通物种制造的那一片心灵感应‘嗡嗡声’中，分离出几条特别突出的脉络或主题；它们身上似乎带着一点我一直在寻找的那种特殊性质。”</p>
<p>约翰停下来。我插嘴问：“什么性质？”</p>
<p>他沉默着看了我好几秒。信不信由你，那种持续的凝视真的让我感到恐惧。我并不是说其中有什么魔法。它造成的效果，本质上同任何普通面部表情可能产生的效果一样。可我太了解约翰，又记得他在苏格兰那个夏天发生的种种怪事，因此大概格外容易受影响。我只能用一个意象来描述当时的感觉：仿佛我面对着一张用半透明材料制成的面具，而面具内部有另一张完全不同、带着精神光辉的脸在发亮。外面的面具是一个怪诞儿童的脸：半像猴子，半像滴水嘴兽，却又彻头彻尾是个顽童；巨大猫眼，扁扁的小鼻子，故意挑衅的嘴唇。里面那张脸——显然根本无法描述，因为它每一个特征都同外面完全一样，却又彻底不同。我只能说，它似乎把佛陀庄严而冰冷的微笑，同受损的狮身人面像在黎明第一次照上脸时散发出的那种诡异严酷结合起来。不，这些比喻完全失败了。我无法描述那几秒钟里，约翰的五官强迫我感受到的象征意味。我只知道自己极想移开目光，却做不到，或者不敢。毫无理性的恐惧在我身体里涌起。牙医的钻头压下来时，人可以咬牙承受几秒钟真正的酷刑而不动；可秒数越积越多，就越来越难不躲、不喊。我被约翰看着时也是这样。不同只在于，我仿佛已经被绑住，完全不能动；而且已经越过了能够尖叫的那一点，连叫都叫不出来。我想，我的恐惧很大一部分来自一种疯狂的预感：约翰就要笑了，而他的笑声会把我彻底消灭。不过他没有笑。</p>
<p>突然之间，那种魔力断了。我一下跳起来，去给炉子添煤。约翰已经转头看向窗外，用平日友善的声音说：“嗯，那种特殊性质究竟是什么，我当然没法告诉你，对吧？这样想吧。它是看见每件东西、每件事情永恒的一面，而不是只把它看成带日期的东西；把它看成世界树伊格德拉西尔（Yggdrasill）上一片活着的叶子，叶脉里流着永恒的汁液，而不只是历史书中一枚被摘下、晒干、贴好日期的标本。”</p>
<p>沉默了很久，他才继续讲。“我第一次发现像我这样的心灵踪迹，可折腾了我很久。我只能偶尔瞥见那个人，而且怎么也不能让他注意到我。传过来的内容也极不连贯，乱得令人摸不着头脑。我不知道是我的技术有问题，还是他的心灵发展得太高，已经超出我理解能力。我试着弄清他究竟在哪里，好让我亲自去见他。很显然，他住在一栋很大的建筑里，房间很多，其他人也很多；可他几乎不同那些人交往。从窗户向外，他能看见树、房子和一条长满草的长山坡。他几乎总能听见火车和汽车交通的连续噪声——至少我认得那是什么，可对他来说似乎没什么意义。我想，很明显，他住的地方附近既有铁路干线，也有一条大路。无论如何，我得把那地方找出来。所以我买了摩托车。与此同时，我继续研究他。我完全接不到他的思想，只能接到他的感知，以及他面对那些感知时的感受。有一件事特别醒目：他的音乐。有时我找到他时，他正在屋外一块有坡度的草地上，草地与主路之间隔着树；他会吹一种管乐器，像竖笛，可八度的分割方式非常奇怪。我发现他每只手都有五根手指外加一个拇指。即使这样，我还是想不通他怎么弄出那些额外音阶。他吹的音乐对我有一种异乎寻常的吸引力。那音乐里面有某种东西——一种精神姿态——让我非常确信，这个人确实是我的同类。顺便，我还发现他有个没多大帮助的名字：詹姆斯·琼斯。有一次我接上他时，他正在院子里，旁边树丛恰好有个缺口，所以能看见公路。不久，一辆公共汽车飞驰而过。那是辆‘绿线’巴士，牌子上写着‘BRIGHTON’。让我惊讶的是，这个词对詹姆斯·琼斯显然毫无意义。可对我意义重大。我骑摩托车出去，沿着从布莱顿出发的绿线路线寻找。花了两天，我终于找到了正确的地点——那栋大楼，那条草坡等等。我停下来问人那是什么建筑。原来是一家精神病院。”</p>
<p>他的叙述被我如释重负的一声大笑打断。“挺好笑，”他说，“不过也不算完全意外。我托了不少关系，终于得到许可，可以去见詹姆斯·琼斯。我说他是我的一个亲戚。院里的人告诉我，我们看上去确实有家族相似；等我真正见到詹姆斯·琼斯时，我立刻明白他们的意思。他是个矮小的老头，脑袋很大，眼睛也大得出奇，跟我的差不多。他几乎全秃了，只在两只耳朵上方还剩几绺硬卷的白发。他的嘴按身材比例说比我的小，而且带着一种受苦后的温柔，尤其当他做出一个很有个人特色的动作——把嘴紧紧抿成一种奇怪的噘嘴——那种感觉尤其明显。见他之前，他们告诉过我一些情况。说他从来不惹麻烦，只是身体非常差，需要大量护理。他几乎不开口，就算开口也通常只说一个音节。他能理解自己熟悉范围内那些简单话语，但常常根本无法让他注意别人正在说什么。可奇怪的是，他又似乎对周围发生的一切都很感兴趣。有时他会极专注地听别人说话；不过显然不是听意义，而只是听声音的音乐性质。各种可感知的节奏似乎都能完全吸住他。他会盯着一块木头的纹理，一看就是几个小时；或者盯着鸭池上的涟漪。普通音乐——智人发明的大多数音乐——似乎既吸引他，又冒犯他。不过有个医生演奏巴赫的一小段时，他却非常严肃地听完，随后跑去用自己的怪笛子，吹出那段音乐被扭曲得很奇怪的变体。某些爵士乐曲会给他极强烈的刺激；听完一张唱片以后，他有时会瘫上好几天。那些音乐仿佛用某种欢喜与厌恶之间的冲突把他撕裂。当然，院方只把他自己的吹奏当成疯子的猫叫。”</p>
<p>“然后，我们被带到彼此面前。我们就那么站着互相看了很久，久到看护都觉得不自在。过了一会儿，詹姆斯·琼斯仍盯着我的眼睛，很轻、却很郑重又带点惊讶地说了一个词：‘朋友！’我笑着点头。接着，我感觉到他瞥见了我的心灵，他的脸突然因为极强烈的喜悦和惊讶亮了起来。他非常缓慢地、像是痛苦地一个词一个词搜寻着说：‘你——没——疯。没疯！我们两个，没疯！可这些——’他慢慢指向看护，还笑了一下，‘全疯了，彻底、彻底疯了。可是善良，又聪明。他照顾我。我不能照顾自己。太忙了，忙着——忙着——’句子就这样消失进沉默。他露出天使般的微笑，慢慢点头，一次又一次。随后，他走上前，短短一瞬把手放在我头顶。到这里就结束了。我说，是，我们是朋友，他和我看世界的方式一样；他又点了点头。可当他试图继续说话时，脸上却出现一种几乎滑稽的迷惑。我进入他的心灵，看见里面已经再次乱成一团。他能感知，可完全找不到自己感知到的东西在人间世界里的意义。他看见面前站着两个人，但已经不再把我的可见形象同‘一个人’、同那个他仍努力交流的心灵联系起来。他甚至不再把我们看成物体，只看成没有任何意义的颜色和形状。我请他吹笛子给我听。他听不懂。看护把笛子塞进他手里，又把他的手指合在笛身上。他茫然看着那东西。忽然，他像有所领悟一样一笑，把笛子贴到耳边，像孩子听贝壳一样。看护又把笛子拿走，吹了几个音，还是没有用。于是我接过来，吹了一小段旋律——那是我在找到他以前，曾经远远听他吹过的。他立刻被吸引。脸上的迷惑消失了。让我们吃惊的是，他开口说话了，缓慢，却毫不费力：‘对，约翰·温赖特。前几天你听见我吹这个。我知道有人在听。把我的笛子给我。’”</p>
<p>“他接过笛子，坐到桌沿上，一直盯着我的眼睛，开始吹。”</p>
<p>约翰突然发出一声又短又尖的笑，吓了我一跳。“老天，那才叫音乐。”他说，“要是你能听见就好了！我是说，要是你真能听见，而不是像一头牛那样只听到声音！它清澈透亮。它把我脑子里缠成一团的东西全都理直了。它极其准确地向我展示：一个成熟的人类精神面对自己的世界时，真正恰当的态度究竟是什么。于是他继续吹，我继续听，死死抓住每一个音，好把它记住。然后看护打断了，说这种噪声总会让其他病人不安。如果是真正的音乐也就罢了，可偏偏只是这种疯疯癫癫的东西。所以琼斯基本上只允许在户外吹。”</p>
<p>“音乐嘎的一声停了。琼斯带着一种友善却痛苦的微笑，看了看那个看护。然后，他又滑回疯狂里。他的精神崩解得那么彻底，甚至真的试图把笛子的吹口塞进嘴里吃掉。”</p>
<p>我相信自己看见约翰打了个寒战。他此时又站回窗前，沉默不语。我正想着该说什么，他突然喊道：“你的望远镜在哪儿？快！见鬼，那不是灰瓣蹼鹬吗？无价的小混蛋，对吧！”我们轮流望着那只银灰色小鸟，看它在水面来回游动寻找食物，对一波波拍打过来的浪头毫不在意。跟海鸥比起来，它像一艘游艇混在一排远洋巨轮之间。“对，”约翰说，回答了我没说出口的想法，“你看着那个小混蛋时的感觉——单纯观察，单纯欣喜，而且——嗯，奇怪地带着虔敬，却又保持距离——对，这正是起点，是琼斯在音乐里发展出来的东西的第一瞬间。要是你能永远保持这种状态，再给它填满整个世界的泛音，你就已经在朝‘我们’靠近了。”</p>
<p>约翰说“我们”时，声音里有一种新婚夫妻第一次说起“我们”时那种羞涩而大胆的意味。我这才逐渐明白：即便是在精神病院里发现自己的同类，对约翰来说也必然是一次极深的震动。我开始意识到，他同一群不过是动物的生物生活了近十八年以后，终于发现了一个人。</p>
<p>约翰叹了口气，继续讲。“当然，詹姆斯·琼斯没法成为建立新世界这项工作的伙伴。后来我又去看过他几次。他每次都会给我吹奏，而我离开时总会觉得头脑更清楚一点，也更长大一点。可他终究无可救药地疯着。于是我又开始‘监听’，心情其实挺阴沉，因为我怕他们最后全都是疯子。事实上，下一个几乎把我彻底治好了，让我不想再找。你看，我当时打算先接触离得近的人，因为方便些。我已经发现一股法国人的思维脉络，很可能属于我们；还有一个埃及人，一个中国人或者西藏人。可暂时我没去碰他们。下一个，是个差不多还算婴儿的家伙，外赫布里底群岛南尤伊斯特岛一个佃农的儿子。他残疾得可怕：没有腿，两条胳膊细得像蝾螈的前肢。嘴也有问题，所以不能说话。消化系统又不好，总在生病。说实在的，任何像样的社会都会在他出生时把他淹死。可母亲像母老虎一样爱他；与此同时又怕他怕得要命，也厌恶他。父母双方都完全不知道他——究竟是什么。他们只以为他是个普通的小残废。因为身体残疾，又因为他们完全用错误方式对待他，他正在酿出你能想象的最凶狠仇恨。我去看他的头五分钟里，他就认出我同其他人不一样。他用心灵感应抓住了我。我也抓住他，可他立刻把心灵彻底关死。你大概会以为，一个人一生第一次发现同类，应该是个值得感恩的时刻。可他完全不是这种反应。他显然立刻觉得，这颗星球上容不下我和他两个人。不过他一点也没有透露自己打算做什么。他的心灵闭得像牡蛎壳，脸也空白得像一张纸。我甚至开始怀疑自己是不是搞错了，也许他根本不是我们中的一个。可一切环境细节都同我此前通过心灵感应瞥见的完全一致：那间很小的房间，石板地面，泥炭火，还有他母亲的脸——一只眼比另一只稍大，嘴角还有一点胡须痕迹。顺便说，他父母年纪都很大了，两人头发都灰白。这让我好奇，因为那孩子看起来顶多三岁。我问婴儿多大了，他们似乎不太愿意说。我很有分寸地夸那孩子长着一张可怕地聪明的脸，完全不像婴儿。父亲脱口说，他十八岁了；母亲则发出一声尖细、歇斯底里的笑。渐渐地，我成功同他父母套上了近乎。（顺便，我告诉他们，我正跟一群朋友在邻近岛上钓鱼度假。）我奉承他们，说自己在书上看过，畸形儿童有时反而会成为伟大的天才。与此同时，我仍在试图突破那孩子的防御，看看他的心灵里面究竟是什么样。我没法清楚告诉你，他对我耍的那个杀人把戏究竟有多可怕。他一定在一见到我时就决定干掉我。于是他选了自己唯一有效的武器——而那武器简直恶魔一般。按我能讲清的程度，事情大概是这样。我从他父母那里转过身去，同他说话，试着表示友善。他只是面无表情地盯着我。我越来越用力地想把那只牡蛎撬开，正准备厌恶地放弃时——老天——牡蛎突然完全张开，而我——嗯，这就是没法描述的地方。我只能继续用这个比喻。那只精神牡蛎张大了口，想把我整个吞进去。而它的内部——就是地狱那口没有底的黑坑。当然，在你听来，这说法又愚蠢又浪漫。可感觉就是那样。我觉得自己笔直坠入一个最可怕的黑暗深渊，不只是视觉的黑，而是心灵和精神的黑。那里什么也没有，只有永远无法满足的漆黑仇恨；像一种潮湿的毒气，所有我曾经在意过的东西，一落进去就开始腐烂，变成恶心的东西。我解释不了，我解释不了。”</p>
<p>约翰一直坐在我写字台的一角。说到这里，他忽然站起来，走到窗边。“感谢上帝还有光。”他望着灰色天空说，“要是有谁真正能理解，我也许能把这一切完整说出来，然后就摆脱它。可只说一半，只会让它们重新全涌上来。还有人说不存在地狱！”</p>
<p>他沉默了好一阵，仍看着窗外。然后突然说：“看那只鸬鹚。它叼的那条康吉鳗比它自己脖子还粗。”我走到他身旁，我们一起看那条鱼扭动、抽打。有时鸟和猎物一起没入水下。有一次鳗鱼挣脱了，可很快又被抓回来。失败许多次后，鸬鹚终于叼住鱼头，一下把它吞了进去；最后什么也看不见，只剩鱼尾还露在外面，鸬鹚的脖子则鼓起一个巨大的包。</p>
<p>“现在，”约翰说，“它就要被消化了。我差点也是这样。我感觉自己的整个心灵正在被那个撒旦小软体动物的消化液分解。接下来发生什么，我不知道。我记得看见那孩子脸上浮现出一种彻底恶魔般的表情；然后我大概不知怎么把自己救出来了，因为再过一会儿，我发现自己独自躺在离房子有一段距离的草地上，浑身冷汗。远远看见那栋房子本身都让我发毛。我完全没法思考。那个婴儿般的仇恨笑容不停出现在眼前，每出现一次，我脑子就又变迟钝。过了一阵，我才意识到自己很冷，于是站起来，朝停船的小海湾走。慢慢地，我开始问自己：这个小撒旦究竟是哪一种魔鬼？他是‘我们’中的一个，还是完全不同的东西？不过实际上，我心里几乎没有怀疑。当然，他就是我们中的一个，而且很可能比琼斯和我都强大得多。只是从受孕那一刻起，他身上的一切都出了错。他的身体背叛了他，天天折磨他；他的心灵也像身体一样残废；而父母根本没有能力给他一个公平机会。所以，他唯一可能的自我表达，就只剩仇恨。而他确实把仇恨这一门钻研得相当彻底。可整件事里最奇怪的是：离开那次经历越远，我越清楚地感觉到，他那种仇恨的狂喜其实完全不是为了自我。他不是替自己而恨。他恨自己同恨我一样深。他恨一切，包括仇恨本身。而且，他用一种近乎神圣的狂热去恨这一切。为什么？因为，正像我渐渐发现的那样，在他地狱最深的坑底，有一颗极小、却燃烧得炽烈的崇拜之星。他和我一样清楚地从永恒的一面看见万物，甚至可能比我更清楚；只是——怎么说呢？——他把自己在这幅图景中的角色理解成魔鬼的角色，于是像一个伟大艺术家那样，以激情与超脱并存的方式去演，而且——如果你明白我的意思——是为了上帝的荣耀。他是对的。这是他唯一能做的事，而他做得很有风格。尽管如此，我仍得向他脱帽致敬。不过这确实相当可怕。想想他过的生活：跟婴儿一样，而他却有这样的力量！只要再活久一点，我敢说总有一天，他会想出某个把整颗行星炸掉的把戏。还有一件事。我必须一直严加提防，不然他会再次抓住我。无论我在澳大利亚还是巴塔哥尼亚，他都能碰到我。老天！我现在都能感觉到他！再给我一个苹果，我们聊点别的。”</p>
<p>约翰嘎吱嘎吱吃着第二只考克斯苹果，渐渐平静下来。不久，他又继续讲。“那件事以后，我没干多少事。花了一阵才重新把心灵理顺；随后我对能不能在世界上任何地方找到一个真正属于我这一类、同时又神志正常的人非常悲观。不过十来天后，我又开始搜索。我找到一个年老的吉卜赛女人，算是半生不熟的‘我们’之一。可她总会发作抽搐。她替人算命，也许真能瞥见一点未来。不过她老得跟山一样，除了算命和朗姆酒什么都不在乎。可在某种程度上，她确实明确属于我们；不是智力上，虽然人人都说她狡猾得要命，而是在洞察力上。她确实能够从永恒的一面看见事物，只是看得很不稳定。还有几个在精神病院里，完全没救。还有一个雌雄同体的少年，住在某种不治病人收容所里。还有一个因谋杀被判终身监禁的男人。我猜，要不是他小时候头骨被打坏了一块，他可能原本会是真正的成品。再有一个闪电心算家，可他似乎除此之外什么也不是。他根本不真正属于我们，只是构成他的东西里恰好有一个必要因素。嗯，这些岛上所有的‘高等人类’（Homo superior），就这么多。”</p>
<p>约翰开始在房间里踱步，走得又快又有规律，像笼子里的北极熊。突然，他停下，攥紧双拳，喊起来：“牲口！牲口！整整一个世界的牲口！老天，它们真臭！”他死死盯着墙。随后叹了口气，转向我说：“抱歉，菲多，老伙计！失态了。午饭前出去走一圈怎么样？”</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a343"></span>CHAPTER XIII. JOHN SEEKS HIS KIND</h3>
<p><span class="t28">F</span><span class="t31">OR</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">MANY</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">WEEKS</span><span class="t30"> </span>after his return from the wilderness John spent a good deal of his time at home, or in the neighbouring city. Apparently he was content to sink back into the interests of the normal adolescent. He resumed his friendship with Stephen, and with Judy. Often he took the child to a picture show, a circus, or any entertainment suited to her years. He acquired a motor-bicycle, on which, upon the very day of the purchase, Judy was treated to a wild ride. The neighbours said that John’s holiday had done him good. He was much more normal now. With his brother and sister too, on the rare occasions when he met them, John became more fraternal. Anne was now married. Tom was a successful young architect. The two brothers had generally maintained a relation of restrained hostility to one another, but now hostility seemed to have mellowed into mutual tolerance. After a family reunion Tom remarked, “Our infant prodigy’s positively growing up.” Doc was delighted by John’s new companionableness, and often talked to him at great length. Their main topic was John’s future. Doc was anxious to persuade him to take to medicine and become “a greater Lister.” John used to attend to these exhortations thoughtfully, seeming to be almost persuaded. Once Pax was present. She shook her head, smilingly but reprovingly at John. “Don’t believe him, Doc,” she said, “he’s pulling your leg.” In this period, by the way, John and Pax often went together to a theatre or concert. Indeed, mother and son were now seeing a great deal of each other. Pax’s interest in the drama, and in “persons,” seemed to afford him an unfailing common platform. Occasionally they even went up to London together for a week-end, “to see the shows.”</p>
<p>There came a time when I began to feel a certain curiosity as to the meaning of this prolonged period of relaxation. John’s behaviour seemed now almost completely normal. There was, indeed, one unusual but unobtrusive feature about it. In the midst of conversation or any other activity he would sometimes give a noticeable start of surprise. He would then perhaps repeat the immediately preceding remark, whether his own or the other person’s; and then he would look around him with amused interest. I fancied that for some time after such an incident he was more alert than before it. Not that in the earlier stage he had seemed at all absent-minded. He was at all times thoroughly adjusted to his surroundings. But after these curious jerks the current of his life seemed to reach a higher tension.</p>
<p>One evening I accompanied the three Wainwrights to the local Repertory Theatre. During an interval, while we were drinking coffee in the foyer and discussing the play, John gave a more violent start than usual, spilling his coffee into the saucer. He laughed, and looked about him with surprised interest. After a moment’s awkward silence, in which Pax regarded her boy with veiled solicitude, John continued his comments on the play, but (as it seemed to me) with new penetration. “My point is just this,” he said. “The thing’s too lifelike to be really alive. It’s not a portrait but a death-mask.”</p>
<p>Next day I asked him what had happened when he spilt his coffee. We were in my flat. John had come to inquire if the post had brought information about some patent or other. I was at my writing-table. He was standing at the window, looking out across the deserted promenade to the wintry sea. He was chewing an apple that he had picked up from a dish on my table. “Yes,” he said, “it’s time you were told, even if you can’t believe. At present I am looking for other people more or less like me, and to do it I become a sort of divided personality. Part of me remains where my body is, and behaves quite correctly, but the other, the essential I, goes off in search of them. Or if you like, I stay put all the time, but reach out in search of them. Anyhow, when I come back, or stop the search, I get a bit of a jolt, taking up the threads of ordinary life again.”</p>
<p>“You never seem to lose the threads,” I said.</p>
<p>“No,” he answered. “The incoming ‘I’ comes slick into possession of all the past experiences of the residential one, so to speak. But the sudden jump from God knows where to here gives a bit of a jar, all the same.”</p>
<p>“And when you’re away,” I asked, “where do you go, what do you find?”</p>
<p>“Well,” he said, “I had better begin at the beginning. I told you before that when I was in Scotland I used to find myself in telepathic touch with people, and that some of the people seemed queer people, or people in a significant way more like me than you. Since I came home I’ve been working up the technique for tuning in to the people I want. Unfortunately it’s much easier to pick up the thoughts of folk one knows well than of strangers. So much depends on the general form of the mind, the matrix in which the thoughts occur, so to speak. To get you or Pax I have only to think of you. I can get your actual consciousness, and if I want to, I can get a good bit of the deeper layers of you too.”</p>
<p>I was seized with horror, but I comforted myself with incredulity.</p>
<p>“Oh, yes, I can,” said John. “While I’ve been talking, half your mind was listening and the other half was thinking about a quarrel you had last night with — —” I cut him short with an expostulation.</p>
<p>“Righto, don’t get excited,” said John. “You haven’t much to be ashamed of. And anyhow I don’t want to pry. But just now, — well, you kept fairly shouting the stuff at me, because while you were attending to me you were thinking about it. You’ll probably soon learn how to shut me out at will.”</p>
<p>I grunted, and John continued: “As I was saying, it’s much harder to get in touch with people one doesn’t know, and at first I didn’t know any of the people I was looking for. On the other hand, I found that the people of my sort make, so to speak, a much bigger ‘noise’ telepathically than the rest. At least they do when they want to, or when they don’t care. But when they want not to, they can shut themselves off completely. Well, at last I managed to single out from the general buzz of telepathic ‘noise,’ made by the normal species, a few outstanding streaks or themes that seemed to have about them something or other of the special quality that I was looking for.”</p>
<p>John paused, and I interjected, “What sort of quality?”</p>
<p>He looked at me for some seconds in silence. Believe it or not, but that prolonged gaze had a really terrifying effect on me. I am not suggesting that there was something magical about it. The effect was of the same kind as any normal facial expression may have. But knowing John as I did, and remembering the strange events of his summer in Scotland, I was no doubt peculiarly susceptible. I can only describe what I felt by means of an image. It was as though I was confronted with a mask made of some semi-transparent substance, and illuminated from within by a different and a spiritually luminous face. The mask was that of a grotesque child, half monkey, half gargoyle, yet wholly urchin, with its huge cat’s eyes, its flat little nose, its teasing lips. The inner face, — obviously it cannot be described, for it was the same in every feature, yet wholly different. I can only say that it seemed to me to combine the august and frozen smile of a Buddha with the peculiar creepy grimness that the battered Sphinx can radiate when the dawn first touches its face. No, these images fail utterly. I cannot describe the symbolical intention that John’s features forced upon me in those seconds. I can only say that I longed to look away and could not, or dared not. Irrational terror welled up in me. When one is under the dentist’s drill, one may endure a few moments of real torture without flinching. But as the seconds pile up, it becomes increasingly difficult not to move, not to scream. And so with me, looked at by John. With this difference, that I was bound, and could not stir, that I had passed the screaming point and could not scream. I believe my terror was largely a wild dread that John was about to laugh, and that his laugh would annihilate me. But he did not laugh.</p>
<p>Suddenly the spell broke, and I leapt up to put more coal on the fire. John was gazing out of the window, and saying, in his normal friendly voice, “Well, of course I can’t tell you what that special quality is, can I? Think of it this way. It’s seeing each thing, each event, on its eternal side, instead of merely as a dated thing; seeing it as a living leaf on the tree Yggdrasil, flushed with the sap of eternity, and not merely as a plucked and dried and dated specimen in the book of history.”</p>
<p>There was a long silence, then he continued his report. “The first trace of mentality like my own gave me a lot of trouble. I could only catch occasional glimpses of this fellow, and I couldn’t make him take any notice of me. And the stuff that did come through to me was terribly incoherent and bewildering. I wondered whether this was the fault of my technique, or whether his was a mind too highly developed for me to understand. I tried to find out where he was, so that I could go and see him. He was evidently living in a large building with lots of rooms and many other people. But he had very little to do with the others. Looking out of his window, he saw trees and houses and a long grassy hill. He heard an almost continuous noise of trains and motor traffic. At least I recognized it as that, but it didn’t seem to mean much to him. Clearly, I thought, there’s a main line and a main road quite close to where he lives. Somehow I must find that place. So I bought the motor-bike. Meanwhile I kept on studying him. I couldn’t catch any of his thoughts, but only his perceptions, and the way he felt about them. One striking thing was his music. Sometimes when I found him he was outside the house in a sloping field with trees between it and the main road; and he would be playing a pipe, a sort of recorder, but with the octave very oddly divided. I discovered that each of his hands had five fingers and a thumb. Even so, I couldn’t make out how he managed all the extra notes. The kind of music he played was extraordinarily fascinating to me. Something about it, the mental pose of it, made me quite sure the man was really my sort. I discovered, by the way, that he had the not very helpful name of James Jones. Once when I got him he was out in the grounds and near a gap in the trees, so that he could see the road. Presently a bus flashed past. It was a ‘Green Line’ bus, and it was labelled ‘BRIGHTON.’ I noticed with surprise that these words apparently meant nothing to James Jones. But they meant a lot to me. I went off on the bike to search the Green Line routes out of Brighton. It took me a couple of days to find the right spot — the big building, the grassy hill, and so on. I stopped and asked someone what the building was. It was a lunatic asylum.”</p>
<p>John’s narrative was interrupted by my guffaw of relief. “Funny,” he said, “but not quite unexpected. After pulling lots of wires I got permission to see James Jones, who was a relative of mine, I said. They told me at the Asylum that there was a family likeness, and when I saw James Jones I knew what they meant. He’s a little old man with a big head and huge eyes, like mine. He’s quite bald, except for a few crisp white curls above the ears. His mouth was smaller than mine (for the size of him) and it had a sort of suffering sweetness about it, specially when he let it do a peculiar compressed pout, which was a characteristic mannerism of his. Before I saw him they had told me a bit about him. He gave no trouble, they said, except that his health was very bad, and they had to nurse him a lot. He hardly ever spoke, and then only in monosyllables. He could understand simple remarks about matters within his ken, but it was often impossible to get him to attend to what was said to him. Yet oddly enough, he seemed to have a lively interest in everything happening around him. Sometimes he would listen intently to people’s voices; but not, apparently, for their significance, simply for their musical quality. He seemed to have an absorbing interest in perceived rhythms of all sorts. He would study the grain of a piece of wood, poring over it by the hour; or the ripples on a duck-pond. Most music, ordinary music invented by Homo sapiens, seemed at once to interest and outrage him; though when one of the doctors played a certain bit of Bach, he was gravely attentive, and afterwards went off to play oddly twisted variants of it on his queer pipe. Certain jazz tunes had such a violent effect on him that after hearing one record he would sometimes be prostrate for days. They seemed to tear him with some kind of conflict of delight and disgust. Of course the authorities regarded his own pipe-playing as the caterwauling of a lunatic.</p>
<p>“Well, when we were brought face to face, we just stood and looked at one another for so long that the attendant found it uncomfortable. Presently James Jones, keeping his eyes on mine, said one word, with quiet emphasis and some surprise, ‘Friend!’ I smiled and nodded. Then I felt him catch a glimpse of my mind, and his face suddenly lit up with intense delight and surprise. Very slowly, as if painfully searching for each word, he said, ‘You — are — not — mad, NOT MAD! We two, NOT MAD! But these—’ (slowly pointing at the attendant and smiling) ‘All mad, quite, quite mad. But kind and clever. He cares for me. I cannot care for self. Too busy with — with — —’ The sentence trailed into silence. Smiling seraphically, he nodded slowly, again and again. Then he came forward and laid a hand for an instant on my head. That was the end. When I said yes, we were friends, and he and I saw things the same way, he nodded again; but when he tried to speak, an expression of almost comic perplexity came over his face. Looking into his mind, I saw that it was already a welter of confusion. He perceived but could not find any mundane significance in what he perceived. He saw the two human beings that confronted him, but he no longer connected my visible appearance with human personality, with the mind that he was still striving to communicate with. He didn’t even see us as physical objects at all, but just as colour and shape, without any meaning. I asked him to play to me. He could not understand. The attendant put the pipe into his hand, closing the fingers over it. He looked blankly at it. Then with a sudden smile of enlightenment he put it to his ear, like a child listening to a shell. The attendant took it again, and played a few notes on it, but in vain. Then I took it and played a little air that I had heard him play before I found him. His attention was held. Perplexity cleared from his face. To our surprise he spoke, slowly but without difficulty. ‘Yes, John Wainwright,’ he said, ‘you heard me play that the other day. I knew some person was listening. Give me my pipe.’</p>
<p>“He took it, seated himself on the edge of the table, and played, with his eyes fixed on mine.”</p>
<p>John startled me with one sharp gasp of laughter. “God! it was music,” he said. “If you could have heard it! I mean if you could have really heard it, and not merely as a cow might! It was lucid. It straightened out the tangles of my mind. It showed me just precisely the true, appropriate attitude of the adult human spirit to its world. Well, he played on, and I went on listening, hanging on to every note, to remember it. Then the attendant interrupted. He said this sort of noise always upset the other patients. It wasn’t as if it was real music, but such crazy stuff. That was why J. J. was really only allowed to play out of doors.</p>
<p>“The music stopped with a squawk. J. J. looked with a kindly but tortured smile at the attendant. Then he slid back into insanity. So complete was his disintegration that he actually tried to eat the mouthpiece.”</p>
<p>I believe I saw John shudder. He was now standing at the window once more, and he stood silent, while I wondered what to say. Then he exclaimed, “Where’s your field-glass, quick! Damned if that’s not a grey phalarope. Priceless little devil, isn’t he!” In turn we watched the small silvery bird as it swam hither and thither in search of food, heedless of the buffeting wave-crests. Beside the gulls it was a yacht amongst the liners. “Yes,” said John, answering my thought, “the way you feel when you watch that little blighter, just observant and delighted, and — well, curiously pious yet aloof, — yes, that’s the starting-point, the very first moment, of what J. J. was working out in his music. If you could hold that always, and fill it out with a whole world of overtones, you’d be well on the way to ‘us.’ ”</p>
<p>In the tone of John’s “us” there was something of the shy audacity with which a newly married couple first speak of “us.” It began to dawn on me that the discovery of his own kind, even in a lunatic asylum, must have been for John a deeply moving experience. I began to realize that, having lived for nearly eighteen years with mere animals, he had at last discovered a human being.</p>
<p>John sighed, and took up his narrative. “Well, of course James Jones was no good as a partner in the job of founding a new world. I’ve seen him several times since, and he always plays to me, and I come away a little clearer in my head, and a little more grown up. But he’s incurably mad, all the same. So I started ‘listening in’ again; rather gloomily, for I was afraid they might all turn out to be mad. And really the next one almost cured me of looking any more. You see, I was trying to get in touch with the near ones first, because they were handier. I had already spotted a strain of French thinking that must be one of us, and also an Egyptian, and a Chinese or Tibetan. But for the present I left these alone. Well, my next was an infant more or less, the son of a crofter in South Uist (Outer Hebrides). He’s a ghastly cripple; no legs, and arms like a newt’s arms. And there’s something wrong with his mouth, so that he can’t talk. And he’s always sick, because his digestion doesn’t work properly. In fact he’s the sort any decent society would drown at birth. But the mother loves him like a tigress; though she’s scared stiff of him too, and loathes him. Neither parent has any idea he’s — what he is. They think he’s just an ordinary little cripple. And because he’s a cripple, and because they treat him all wrong, he’s brewing the most murderous hate imaginable. Within the first five minutes of my visit he spotted me as different from the others. He got me telepathically. I got him too, but he shut his mind up immediately. Now you’d think that finding a kindred spirit for the first time ever would be an occasion for thanksgiving. But he didn’t take it that way at all. He evidently felt at once there wasn’t room for him and me together on the same planet. But he didn’t let on he was going to do anything about it. He kept his mind shut like an oyster, and his face as blank as a piece of paper. I began to think I had made a mistake, that he was not one of us after all. Yet all the circumstances corresponded with my earlier telepathic glimpses of him, — the minute room with a flagged floor, the peat fire, his mother’s face, with one eye slightly bigger than the other, and traces of a moustache at the corners of her lips. By the way, his parents were quite old people, both grey. This made me curious, because the kid looked about a three-year-old. I asked how old the baby was, but they seemed unwilling to say. I tactfully said the child had a terribly wise face, not like a baby’s. The father blurted out that he was eighteen years old, and the mother gave a high-pitched hysterical sort of laugh. Gradually I succeeded in making friends with his parents. (I had told them, by the way, that I was on a fishing holiday with a party on the neighbouring island.) I flattered them by telling them I had read in a book that deformed children sometimes turned out to be great geniuses. Meanwhile I was still trying to get behind the kid’s defences to see what his mind was like inside. It’s impossible to give you a clear idea of the murderous trick he played on me. He must have made up his mind as soon as he saw me that he’d do me in. He chose the only effective weapon he had, and it was a diabolic one. It happened this way, so far as I can tell you. I had turned from his parents and was talking to him, trying to make friends. He just stared at me blankly. I tried harder and harder to open the oyster, and was just about ready to give up in disgust when, my God, the oyster opened wide, and I — well, this is the indescribable thing. I can only carry on with the image. The mental oyster opened wide and tried to swallow me into itself. And itself was — just the bottomless black pit of Hell. Of course, that sounds silly and romantic to you. But that’s what it was like. I felt myself dropping plumb into the most appalling gulf of darkness, of mental and spiritual darkness, in which there was nothing whatever but eternally unsatisfied black hate; a sort of dank atmosphere of poison, in which everything that I had ever cared for seemed to moulder away into nastiness. I can’t explain, I can’t explain.”</p>
<p>John had been sitting on the corner of my writing-table. He got up suddenly and walked to the window. “Thank God for light,” he said, looking at the grey sky. “If there was some one who could understand, I could tell it all and be rid of it, perhaps. But half-telling it just makes it all come welling up again. And some say there’s no Hell!”</p>
<p>He remained silent for some time, looking out of the window. Then he said, “Look at that cormorant. He’s got a conger fatter than his own neck.” I came up beside him, and we watched the fish writhing and lashing. Sometimes bird and prey disappeared together under water. Once the conger got away, but was speedily recovered. After many failures, the cormorant caught it by the head, and swallowed it, slick, so that nothing was to be seen of it but its tail, and a huge swelling in the bird’s neck.</p>
<p>“And now,” said John, “he’ll be digested. That’s what nearly happened to me. I felt my whole mind being disintegrated by the digestive juices of that Satanic young mollusc. I don’t know what happened next. I remember seeing a perfectly diabolic expression on the kid’s face; and then I must have saved myself somehow, for presently I found myself lying on the grass some way from the house, alone and in a cold sweat. The very sight of the house in the distance gave me the creeps. I couldn’t think. I kept seeing that infantile grin of hate, and turning stupid again. After a while I realized I was cold, so I got up and walked toward the little bay where the boats were. Presently I began to ask myself what sort of a devil this baby Satan really was. Was he one of ‘us,’ or something quite different? But there was very little doubt in my mind, actually. Of course he was one of us, and probably a much mightier one than either J. J. or myself. But everything had gone wrong with him, from conception onwards. His body had failed him, and was tormenting him, and his mind was as crippled as his body, and his parents were quite unable to give him a fair chance. So the only self-expression possible to him was hate. And he had specialized in hate pretty thoroughly. But the oddest thing about it all was this. The further I got away from the experience, the more clearly it was borne in on me that his ecstasy of hate was really quite self-detached. He wasn’t hating for himself. He hated himself as much as me. He hated everything, including hate. And he hated it all with a sort of sacred fervour. And why? Because, as I begin to discover, there’s a sort of minute, blazing star of worship right down in the pit of his hell. He sees everything from the side of eternity just as clearly as I do, perhaps more clearly; but — how shall I put it? — he conceives his part in the picture to be the devil’s part, and he’s playing it with a combination of passion and detachment like a great artist, and for the glory of God, if you understand what I mean. And he’s right. It’s the only thing he can do, and he does it with style. I take off my hat to him, in spite of everything. But it’s pretty ghastly, really. Think of the life he’s living; just like an infant’s, and with his powers! I dare say he’ll manage to find some trick for blowing up the whole planet some day, if he lives much longer. And there’s another thing. I’ve got to keep a sharp look-out or he’ll catch me again. He can reach me anywhere, in Australia or Patagonia. God! I can feel him now! Give me another apple, and let’s talk about something else.”</p>
<p>Crunching his second Cox, John became calm again. Presently he went on with his narrative. “I haven’t done much since that affair. It took me some time to get my mind straight, and then I felt depressed about the chances of ever finding any one anywhere that was really my sort and yet also sane. But after ten days or so I began the search again. I found an old gipsy woman who was a sort of half-baked one of ‘us.’ But she’s always having fits. She tells fortunes, and perhaps has some sort of glimpses of the future. But she’s as old as the hills, and cares for nothing but fortune-telling and rum. Yet she’s quite definitely one of us, up to a point; not intellectually, though she has the reputation of being damnably cunning, but in insight. She sees things on their eternal side all right, though not very steadily. Then there are several others in asylums, quite hopeless. And a hermaphrodite adolescent in a sort of home for incurables. And a man doing a life-sentence for murder. I fancy he might have been the real thing if he hadn’t had a bit of his skull knocked in when he was a kid. Then there’s a lightning calculator, but he doesn’t seem to be anything else. He’s not really one of us at all, but he’s got just one of the essential factors in his make up. Well, that’s all there is of Homo superior in these islands.”</p>
<p>John began pacing the room, quickly, methodically, like a polar bear in its cage. Suddenly he stopped, and clenched his fists and cried out, “Cattle! Cattle! A whole world of cattle! My God, how they stink!” He stared at the wall. Then he sighed, and turning to me he said, “Sorry, Fido, old man! That was a lapse. What do you say to a walk before lunch?”</p>
