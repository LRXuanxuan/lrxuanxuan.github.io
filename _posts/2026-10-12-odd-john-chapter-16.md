<h3>第十六章　阿德兰</h3>
<p>约翰继续寻找。我陪着他。在这里，我暂且不打算详述他后来发现并说服其为那场伟大冒险作准备的几个合适的超常少年。其中有马赛的一个小姑娘、莫斯科一个年纪稍大的女孩、芬兰的一个男孩、瑞典的一个女孩、匈牙利的另一个女孩，以及土耳其的一个年轻男子。除此之外，约翰找到的尽是疯子、残疾人、病弱者和积习难改的老流浪汉；这些人原本更高等的心智能力，都已经在同正常人类长期接触的过程中遭到无可挽回的扭曲。</p>
<p>可是在埃及，约翰竟真正遇见了一个比自己更高等的人。这件事离奇得让我几乎不敢记录，甚至连我自己都不太敢相信。</p>
<p>很久以来，约翰一直确信，在黎凡特某处或尼罗河三角洲里隐藏着一个极其非凡的心灵。我们从土耳其乘船到亚历山大港。经过进一步探查后，又转往塞得港。我们在那里住了几个星期。对我而言，那几周完全是闲散时光。我无事可做，只能打网球、游泳，再进行一些无伤大雅的调情。约翰自己看起来似乎也在闲逛。他游泳，在港内划船，在城里四处转悠。他比平时更心不在焉，有时甚至近乎烦躁。</p>
<p>等到塞得港开始让我厌烦得难以忍受时，我建议我们去开罗试试。“你想去就自己去吧，”约翰说，“我留在这里。我正忙着呢。”于是我便当真照办，坐火车穿过三角洲。还离开罗很远时，大金字塔便已经出现在视野里，越过棕榈树和尚未看见的城市高高耸起。我永远忘不了第一次望见它们的情形，因为后来我觉得，那景象仿佛象征了约翰本人当时正在塞得港经历的事情。它们在蓝天里呈灰蓝色，奇异地简单、遥远而稳固。</p>
<p>我在谢泼德饭店订了房间，把自己彻底交给观光。大约在我离开塞得港三周后的一天，我收到约翰发来的电报，上面只有两个词：“回去。约翰。”我正求之不得，于是收拾行李，搭下一班火车返回塞得港。</p>
<p>我一到那里，约翰便让我立刻预订三个人去土伦的船位，搭乘几天后即将通过运河的一艘东方航线客轮。他说，队伍里的新成员正在从上埃及赶来，一有可能就会同我们会合。不过在介绍这位未来的同行者之前，我必须先尽量转述约翰告诉我的另一件事：在我去开罗期间，他曾同一个完全不同的存在建立接触。</p>
<p>“事情是这样的，”他说，“我一直在找的那个人——名字叫阿德兰——原来三十五年前就已经死了。他一直试图从他所在的过去联系我，可我一开始根本没意识到这一点。等我们终于建立起某种交流时，他设法让我看见他正在看的东西。我这才注意到，港里的轮船全是又矮又小的老式家伙，桅杆上还挂着横桁。而且本来应该有运河公司大楼的地方根本没有那栋楼。（你知道，就是那个有绿色圆顶的东西。）你可以想象这有多刺激。我花了很久，才学会不是把他拉到现在来，而是把我自己送进过去。”</p>
<p>约翰的故事只能压缩来说。为了在过去获得一个不那么摇摇欲坠的立足点，他按照阿德兰的指示，结识了一位中年的英国船舶用品商。这个人童年有很大一部分时间是在阿德兰生活的那个年代的塞得港度过的。这位英埃混血背景的英国人名叫哈里·鲁滨逊。要让他谈起儿时经历并不困难，而他也能描述阿德兰——小时候有一段时间，他几乎每天都会见到那个人。约翰很快便熟悉鲁滨逊的心灵，熟悉到足以沿着它向后追溯，把自己相当牢固地安置进童年哈里的意识，以及那个早已消失的塞得港之中。</p>
<p>透过哈里的眼睛看去，阿德兰原来是一个年迈而贫困的本地船夫。约翰说，他的脸像一具木乃伊：黝黑、干瘪、紧绷，却又充满生命，而且经常露出一种颇为严峻的笑容。他硕大的脑袋顶上戴着一顶菲斯帽，小得同他的头极不相称，显得十分滑稽。偶尔帽子掉下来时，可以看见他的头颅完全秃了。约翰说，那东西让他想到一块深色、磨得发亮而形状古怪的木头。他也有那种典型的大眼睛，其中一只布满血丝，还不断流出黄色黏液。和许多当地人一样，他曾患过眼炎。他赤裸的棕色小腿和双脚布满伤疤，脚趾甲也掉了好几片。</p>
<p>阿德兰靠摆渡谋生，把乘客从远洋客轮送到岸上，也把欧洲侨民送往或接离所谓的“浴室”——那是一些用角铁架在海面上的木制建筑。鲁滨逊一家每星期都会几次雇阿德兰和他的船，让他划着把他们送过港湾，到他们自己的“浴室”去。他得在那里等他们游完泳、吃完午饭，再把他们划回城里。正是在阿德兰坐在那艘长尖船头、涂着鲜艳颜色的小船里奋力拉桨时，在哈里同父母、姐姐，甚至同阿德兰本人叽叽喳喳说个不停时，约翰一边借哈里的眼睛观看眼前场景，一边同这个独一无二的埃及人进行心灵感应式的谈话。</p>
<p>约翰把自己的心灵投射进过去，最终回到了1896年。那时，阿德兰声称自己已经三百八十四岁。若是在见到雅克利娜之前，约翰大概不会那么容易相信；可到了这一步，他已经完全愿意接受这种事。这样算来，阿德兰出生于1512年，地点在苏丹某处。他生命的第一个世纪大部分时间都作为本部落的智者度过，但最后，他决定离开原始环境，去寻找更文明的世界。他顺尼罗河而下，定居开罗，并逐渐获得巫师的名声。十七世纪期间，他曾积极卷入埃及动荡不安的政治生活，有一段时间甚至成了王座背后的真正力量。然而，政治活动无法满足他。他之所以被卷进去，多少就像一个聪明的旁观者被几个笨蛋下的一盘棋吸引进去一样。他忍不住看出这盘棋怎样下才最有效，于是不知不觉间，自己也成了棋手。到了十八世纪末，他越来越专注于发展自己的“神秘”能力，尤其是最新掌握的一门本领——把自己投射进过去。</p>
<p>拿破仑远征埃及前几年，阿德兰伪造了一次自杀，从此彻底同政治生活断绝关系。此后几年，他仍住在开罗，却完全隐姓埋名，生活极其卑微。他靠当挑水夫谋生，赶着驴穿过尘土飞扬的街道，驴背上驮着鼓胀、滴水的皮囊。与此同时，他继续磨炼自己的超常能力，有时也会利用这种能力为同阶层的穷人做某种心理治疗。不过，他最大的兴趣还是探索过去。当时人们对古埃及的知识极为贫乏，而阿德兰最强烈的愿望，是直接体验那个久远而伟大的民族。此前，他的能力只能让自己后退几年，进入同现有环境相差不大的往事。后来，他决定把自己埋进某个不起眼的村庄，在三角洲亲自耕种土地，融入那些原始农民的生活；因为这些人的习俗与文化，很可能自法老时代以来就没有发生太大变化。几十年里，他挥着锄头，操纵着沙杜夫汲水器。终于，他对古代孟斐斯的熟悉程度几乎赶上了对近代开罗的熟悉。</p>
<p>然而到了十九世纪第二个四分之一时期，外表仍不过中年的阿德兰开始觉得有必要研究其他文明。为此，他迁居亚历山大港，又干起了从前的挑水夫工作。在这里，他进入古希腊世界的过程不如研究古埃及时那么轻松，也不那么成功；但他最终还是学会把自己投射到大图书馆存在的时代，甚至进入柏拉图时代的希腊本土。</p>
<p>一直到十九世纪最后四分之一，阿德兰才骑着驴沿曼扎拉湖与大海之间那条狭长的沙带前往塞得港，并在那里再次以挑水夫身份安顿下来。不过他并不只干这一行。有时，他会把驴租给上岸游览一天的欧洲旅客。那时，他就赤脚跟在那头高大的白驴后面跑，一边亲热地拍打它的屁股，一边喊“哈！哈！”有一次，他那头名叫“两只漂亮黑眼睛”的驴被偷了，他沿着潮湿海岸上的蹄印追了三十英里。最后追上小偷时，他狠狠揍了对方一顿，然后得意洋洋地骑着驴回来。有时，他会登上远洋客轮，用戒指、球和几只坐立不安的小黄鸡表演魔术，逗乘客开心。有时，他也向他们兜售丝绸或珠宝。</p>
<p>阿德兰搬到塞得港的目的，是让自己接触当代欧洲的生活与思想，同时尽可能同印度和中国也建立某种联系。那时的运河已经成了全世界最国际化的地点之一。黎凡特人、希腊人、俄国人、拉斯卡水手、中国司炉工、前往东方的欧洲人、前往伦敦与巴黎的亚洲人、去麦加朝圣的穆斯林——所有人都会经过塞得港。几十个民族、几十种语言、几十种宗教与文化，在这座血统混杂得最明目张胆的城市里彼此摩擦、拥挤。</p>
<p>阿德兰很快便学会怎样从这种新环境里榨取最大的收获。他的方法多种多样，却都主要依赖心灵感应和极端发达的智力。他一点一点在自己心里建立起一幅极其清晰的欧洲文化图景，甚至也包括印度和中国文化。当然，他并不能直接在塞得港接触到的那些人脑子里找到什么现成的“文化”；不论是长期居民还是过路旅客，绝大多数都庸俗得很。然而，他能从这些迁徙者零散、贫乏而互不连贯的思想痕迹中进行极其精彩的推理，由此重建出塑造他们的整个文化母体。除此以外，他还靠阅读来补充这种方法。一位喜欢文学的船务代理会借书给他。后来，他甚至学会把心灵感应的距离扩展得极远：比如说，只要在脑中尽可能完整地唤起他所知道的约翰·罗斯金，他就有可能直接同那位住在科尼斯顿湖畔遥远家中的说教式贤人取得接触。</p>
<p>不久以后，阿德兰越来越清楚地认识到，欧洲思想真正有趣的阶段还在未来。那么，他能不能像探索过去那样探索未来？事实证明，这困难得多。若不是极其幸运地发现了约翰——一个同自己大致处于相近智力层级的心灵——他恐怕根本无法有效做到这一点。于是，他产生了一个想法：教这个同样超常的人从未来向过去伸手来接触自己。这样一来，他便可以了解未来，而不必亲自承担把自己投射进未来所需要的那种危险、摇摆不定而又艰苦的劳动。</p>
<p>我听约翰说，我们抵达埃及虽然不过几个星期，可在这段时间里，他已经同阿德兰一起度过了好几个月，这让我非常惊讶。或者更准确地说，他同阿德兰的会面——都是借哈里的心灵实现的——分散在阿德兰生命中的好几个月里。日复一日，老人会把鲁滨逊一家划到他们的浴室去，一边稳定地拉着磨损的船桨，一边用厨房式阿拉伯语同哈里聊轮船和骆驼；与此同时，他又在心灵层面同约翰进行极其严肃而精细的讨论，谈相对论、量子理论，或者历史的经济决定作用。约翰很快便确信，自己遇到的这个心灵，无论是凭天赋上的优越，还是凭数百年的沉思，都已经远远超出自己，甚至连处理西欧文化的能力也是如此。可正因为阿德兰如此卓越，他选择的生活方式才显得更加令人费解。约翰一度颇有几分自得地告诉自己，假如自己也能活到阿德兰那么久，绝不至于老年还要为了从智人手里挣几文钱而辛苦劳作。不过在同阿德兰分别之前，他已经开始以更谦逊的眼光看待自己，也以更尊敬的态度看待阿德兰。</p>
<p>老人对约翰的生物学知识非常感兴趣，也尤其关注这些知识对他本人和约翰意味着什么。“是的，”他说，“我们和其他人差别很大。我八岁时就已经知道了。事实上，我们周围这些生物几乎算不上真正的人。不过，我的孩子，也许你把这种差异看得太重了。不，我不该这么说。我的意思是，对你而言，建立新物种这个计划确实是正确的道路；但对我而言，还有另一条道路。而我们每个人都必须按照真主要求自己的方式去侍奉真主。”</p>
<p>约翰解释说，阿德兰并不是给他的伟大冒险泼冷水。恰恰相反，老人以充分的同情参与讨论，还提出过许多很有帮助的建议。事实上，他最喜欢做的事之一，就是一边划桨，一边以近乎先知般的热情向约翰描绘“约翰的新人类”将会创造怎样的世界，以及那个世界会比智人的世界更有生命力、更幸福。这份热情无疑是真诚的；可是约翰说，在它背后始终有一丝极轻微的揶揄。那有点像成年人热情加入儿童游戏时的兴致。有一天，约翰故意向他发起挑战，把自己的计划称作人类可能面对的最伟大的冒险。当时，一艘奥地利劳埃德公司的轮船正在驶入运河，阿德兰便停桨等待横穿港湾。哈里的全部注意力都在那艘客轮上，可约翰设法诱使他转头去看老船夫。阿德兰正庄重地望着这个孩子。“我的孩子，我亲爱的孩子，”他说，“真主要求他的造物以两种方式侍奉他。一种，是为实现他在世间的积极目的而劳动；这正是你最放在心上的侍奉。另一种，是以理解来观看他的造物，并以有辨别力的喜悦赞颂其卓越形式。这便是我的侍奉：把这样一生的赞颂放到真主脚下，而这是任何人都无法给予他的，甚至连你，我极亲爱的孩子，也不能。真主塑造你的方式，使你最适合通过行动来侍奉他——当然，这行动必须永远由深入本质的沉思所启发。但他塑造我的方式，却使我必须直接通过沉思与赞颂来侍奉他；只不过，为了达到这个目的，我首先也必须经过行动这所学校。”</p>
<p>约翰反驳说，与其让少数孤立的高贵心灵生活在一整个次人类世界里，不如让新人类建立一个完整的世界，那样才能更好地实现“赞颂”的目的；因此，最紧迫的任务正是把这样一个世界创造出来。</p>
<p>可是阿德兰回答：“在你看来当然如此，因为你生来就是为了行动，而且你还年轻。并且，从某种意义上说，事情确实如此。像我这样的心灵很清楚，到了适当的时候，像你这样的心灵事实上会创造出那个新世界。但我们也知道，对我们而言还有另一项工作。甚至可能，我任务的一部分恰恰就是把目光投向极远的未来，好让我得以看见并赞颂那些伟大的事业——那些事业注定将由你，或由另一个人完成。”</p>
<p>约翰向我转述完这番话以后，说道：“随后老人中断了同我的交流，也停止同哈里闲聊。过了一会儿，他又用思想对我说话。他的心灵以一种庄严的温柔把我整个包围起来，然后他说：‘现在该离开我了，你这极亲爱、近乎神性的孩子。我已经看见了一些等待着你的未来。你当然承受得了这种预知，也不会因此偏离赞颂之道；但这件事不该由我告诉你。’第二天我又见到他，可他不太愿意交流。旅程结束时，鲁滨逊一家正从船上岸，他把哈里抱起来放到陆地上，用欧洲侨民口中那种勉强算阿拉伯语的行话说：‘小少爷，很好，非常好！’然后他在思想中对我说：‘今晚，也许明天，我就会死。因为过去、现在和近未来，我都已经凭真主赐予我的全部洞察力加以赞颂。而当我把目光投向更遥远的未来时，我只看见一些模糊而可怕的东西，那些东西不是我能够赞颂的。因此可以确定，我的工作已经完成，现在可以休息了。’”</p>
<p>第二天，送哈里和他父母去浴室的已经是另一条船。</p>

<h3><span id="a346"></span>CHAPTER XVI. ADLAN</h3>
<p><span class="t28">J</span><span class="t31">OHN</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">CONTINUED</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">HIS</span><span class="t30"> </span>search. I accompanied him. I shall not at this stage describe the few suitable supernormal youngsters whom he discovered and persuaded to prepare themselves for the great adventure. There was a young girl in Marseilles, an older girl in Moscow, a boy in Finland, a girl in Sweden, another in Hungary, and a young man in Turkey. Save for these, John found nothing but lunatics, cripples, invalids, and inveterate old vagabonds in whom the superior mentality had been hopelessly distorted by contact with the normal species.</p>
<p>But in Egypt John actually met his superior. This incident was so strange that I hesitate to record it, or even to believe it myself.</p>
<p>John had for long been convinced that a very remarkable mind was secreted somewhere in the Levant or the Nile Delta. From Turkey we took ship to Alexandria. Thence, after further investigation, we moved to Port Saïd. Here we spent some weeks. As far as I was concerned, they were weeks of idleness. There was nothing for me to do but to play tennis, bathe and indulge in mild flirtations. John himself seemed to be idling. He bathed, rowed in the harbour, wandered about the town. He was unusually absent-minded, and sometimes almost irritable.</p>
<p>When Port Saïd was beginning to bore me excessively, I suggested that we should try Cairo. “Go yourself,” said John, “if you want to, but I’m staying here. I’m busy.” I therefore took him at his word, and crossed the Delta by train. Long before we reached Cairo the Great Pyramids came into view, overtopping the palm trees and the unseen city. I shall not forget that first glimpse of them, for later it seemed to symbolize the experience that John himself was passing through in Port Saïd. They were grey-blue, in the blue sky. They were curiously simple, remote, secure.</p>
<p>I took a room at Shepheard’s Hotel, and gave myself over to sight-seeing. One day, about three weeks after I left Port Saïd, a telegram came from John. It said merely, “Home, John.” Nothing loath, I packed my traps and took the next tram for Port Saïd.</p>
<p>As soon as I arrived, John made me book accommodation for three to Toulon by an Orient boat that was due to pass through the Canal a few days later. The new member of the party, he said, was on his way from Upper Egypt, and would join us as soon as he could. Before giving details of our future fellow passenger I must try to report what John told me of the very different being with whom he was in contact during my absence in Cairo.</p>
<p>“You see,” he said, “the fellow I was after (Adlan, by name) turned out to have died thirty-five years ago. He was trying to get me from his place in the past, and at first I didn’t realize. When at last we effected some sort of communication, he managed to show me what he was seeing, and I noticed that the steamers in the harbour were all little low old things with yards on their masts. Also there wasn’t any Canal Company’s Building where it ought to have been. (You know, the green-domed thing.) You can imagine how exciting this was. It took me a long time to get myself into the past instead of his coming to me in the present.”</p>
<p>John’s story must be condensed. In order to secure a less precarious footing in the past, John, under Adlan’s direction, made the acquaintance of a middle-aged Englishman, a ship-chandler, who had spent much of his childhood in Port Saïd in Adlan’s time. This Anglo-Egyptian, Harry Robinson, was easily persuaded to talk about his early experiences, and to describe Adlan, whom he used at one time to meet almost daily. John soon made himself familiar with Robinson’s mind to such an extent that he was able to reach back and establish himself quite firmly in the child Harry and in the Port Saïd that had long since vanished.</p>
<p>Seen through Harry’s eyes. Adlan turned out to be an aged and poverty-stricken native boatman. His face, John said, was like a mummy’s, black and pinched and drawn, but very much alive, with a frequent and rather grim smile. His gigantic head bore upon its summit a fez which was ridiculously small for it. When, as occasionally happened, this covering fell off, his cranium was seen to be perfectly bald. John said it reminded him of a dark and polished and curiously moulded lump of wood. He had the typical great eyes, one of which was bloodshot, and running with yellow mucus. Like so many natives, he had suffered from ophthalmia. His bare brown legs and feet were covered with scars. Several toe-nails had been lost.</p>
<p>Adlan made his living by ferrying passengers between the liners and the shore, and by transporting European residents to and from the “bathhouses” — wooden erections built out over the sea on angle-irons. The Robinson family hired Adlan and his boat several times a week to row them across the harbour to their “bath house.” He had to wait while they bathed and lunched. Then he would row them back to the town. It was while Adlan was tugging at the oars in his long-prowed and gaily painted boat, and while Harry was prattling to his parents or his sister or even to Adlan himself, that John, regarding the scene through Harry’s eyes, carried on his telepathic conversations with the unique Egyptian.</p>
<p>John’s projection of his mind into the past took him back to the year 1896. At this time Adlan claimed that he was three hundred and eighty-four years old. John would have been less inclined to believe this before he met Jacqueline, but by now he was ready to accept it. Adlan, then, was born in 1512, somewhere in the Soudan. Most of his first century was spent as the wise man of his tribe, but in the end he resolved to exchange his primitive environment for something more civilized. He travelled down the Nile, and settled in Cairo, where in time he gained a reputation as a sorcerer. During the seventeenth century he played an active part in the turbulent political life of Egypt, and was at one time the power behind the throne. But political activities could not satisfy him. He was drawn into them much as an intelligent spectator might be drawn into a game of chess played by blockheads. He could not help seeing how the game might be played most effectively, and presently he found himself playing it. Toward the end of the eighteenth century, he became more and more absorbed in the development of his “occult” powers, and chiefly his most recent art, that of projecting himself into the past.</p>
<p>A few years before Napoleon’s Egyptian expedition Adlan broke with his political life entirely by faking a suicide. For some years he continued to live in Cairo, but in complete obscurity and very humble circumstances. He made his living as a water-carrier, driving his ass, laden with swollen and dripping skins, along the dusty streets. Meanwhile he continued to improve his supernormal powers, and would sometimes use them to practise psycho-therapy upon his fellow-proletarians. But his chief interest was exploration of the past. At this time the knowledge of Ancient Egypt was extremely scanty, and Adlan’s passion was to gain direct experience of the great race of long ago. Hitherto his powers had only enabled him to reach a few years back, to events which occurred in an environment similar to his own. But presently he determined to bury himself in some obscure village and till the soil of the Delta, entering into the life of the primitive agriculturalists whose customs and culture had probably changed little since the days of the Pharaohs. For many decades he wielded the hoe and the shadoof; and in due course he learned to be almost as familiar with ancient Memphis as with modern Cairo.</p>
<p>In the second quarter of the nineteenth century, however, when he was still in appearance no more than middle-aged, he conceived the need to explore other cultures. For this purpose he settled in Alexandria, and took up his old profession of water-carrier. Here, with less ease and less success than in his study of Ancient Egypt, he made his entry into Ancient Greece, learning to project himself into the era of the great Library, and even into Greece itself of the age of Plato.</p>
<p>Not till the last quarter of the nineteenth century did Adlan ride his donkey along the strip of sand between Lake Menzaleh and the sea, and settle in Port Saïd, once more as a water-carrier. He did not practise his old profession exclusively. Sometimes he would hire out his donkey to a European passenger, ashore for the day. Then he would run barefoot behind the tall white ass, affectionately whacking its hind-quarters, and crying “Haa! Haa!” Once, when his beast, which he called “Two Lovely Black Eyes,” was stolen, he ran thirty miles in chase of it, following its footprints on the moist shore. When at last he overtook the thief, he battered him, and returned in triumph on the ass. Sometimes he would board the liners and amuse the passengers with conjuring tricks with rings and balls and restless little yellow chicks. Sometimes he would sell them silk or jewellery.</p>
<p>Adlan’s object in moving to Port Saïd had been to put himself into touch with contemporary European life and thought, and if possible to make some kind of contact with India and China. The Canal was by now the most cosmopolitan spot in all the world. Levantines, Greeks, Russians, Lascars, Chinese firemen, Europeans on their way to the East, Asiatics on their way to London and Paris, Moslem pilgrims on their way to Mecca, — all passed through Port Saïd. Scores of races, scores of languages, scores of religions and cultures jostled one another in that most flagrantly mongrel town.</p>
<p>Adlan soon learned how to get the best out of his new environment. His methods were diverse, but all depended chiefly on telepathy and extreme intelligence. He constructed little by little in his own mind a very clear picture of European, and even Indian and Chinese culture. He did not, indeed, find any culture ready to hand in the minds of the beings with whom he made contact in Port Saïd, for they, residents and passengers alike, were nearly all quite philistine. But by a brilliant process of inference from the meagre and incoherent traces of thought in these migrants he was able to reconstruct the cultural matrix in which they had developed. This method he supplemented by reading books lent him by a shipping agent who had a liking for literature. He learned also to extend his telepathic reach to such an extent that, by conjuring up all that he knew of John Ruskin (let us say), he could make contact with that didactic sage in his remote home by Coniston Water.</p>
<p>Presently it became evident to Adlan that the really interesting period of European thought lay in the future. Could he, then, explore the future as he had explored the past? This proved a far more difficult task, and one which he could never have performed at all effectively had he not, by great good luck, discovered John, a mind of somewhat the same calibre as his own. He conceived the idea of teaching that fellow-supernormal to reach back into the past to him, so that he himself might learn about the future without the precarious and dangerous labour of projecting himself into it.</p>
<p>I was surprised to hear from John, that, though only a few weeks had passed since our arrival in Egypt, he had in that period spent many months with Adlan. Or perhaps I should say that his interviews with Adlan (through the mind of Harry) were distributed over a period of many months in Adlan’s life. Day after day the old man would ferry the Robinsons to their bathhouse, pulling steadily at his battered oars, and prattling in kitchen Arabic to Harry about ships and camels. And at the same time he would be carrying on a most earnest and subtle telepathic conversation with John about relativity or the quantum theory or the economic determination of history. John was soon convinced that he had encountered a mind which either through native superiority or through prolonged meditation was far in advance of his own, even in ability to cope with Western European culture. But Adlan’s brilliance made his way of life seem all the more perplexing. With some complacency John assured himself that if he were to live as long as Adlan he would not have to spend his old age toiling for a pittance from Homo sapiens. But before he parted from Adlan he began to take a humbler view of himself and a more respectful attitude to Adlan.</p>
<p>The old man was greatly interested in John’s biological knowledge, and its bearing upon himself and John. “Yes,” he said, “we are very different from other men. I have known it since I was eight. Indeed these creatures that surround us are scarcely men at all. But perhaps, my son, you take that difference too seriously. No, I should not say that. What I mean is that though for you this project of founding the new species is the true way, for me there is another way. And each of us must serve Allah in the way that Allah demands of him.”</p>
<p>It was not, John explained, that Adlan threw cold water on his great adventure. On the contrary he entered into it with sympathy and made many helpful suggestions. Indeed one of his favourite occupations, as he plied his oars, was to expound to John with prophetic enthusiasm the kind of world that “John’s New Men” would make, and how much more vital and more happy it would be than the world of Homo sapiens. This enthusiasm was undoubtedly sincere yet, said John, there was a delicate mockery behind it. It was not wholly unlike the zeal with which grown men enter into the games of children. One day John deliberately challenged him by referring to his project as the greatest adventure that man could ever face. Adlan was resting on his oars before crossing the harbour, for an Austrian Lloyd steamer was passing into the Canal. Harry was intent upon the liner, but John induced him to turn his eyes on the old boatman. Adlan was looking gravely at the lad. “My son, my dear son,” he said, “Allah wills of his creatures two kinds of service. One is that they should toil to fulfil his active purpose in the world, and that is the service which you have most at heart. The other is that they should observe with understanding and praise with discriminate delight the excellent form of his handiwork. And this is my service, to lay at Allah’s feet such a life of praise that no man, not even you, my very dear son, can give him. He has fashioned you in such a manner that you may serve him best in action, though in action inspired always by deep-searching contemplation. But me he has fashioned such that I must serve him directly through contemplation and praise, though for this end I had first to pass through the school of action.”</p>
<p>John protested that the end of praise would be far better served by a world of the New Men than by a few isolated lofty spirits in a world of subhuman creatures; and that, therefore, the most urgent of all tasks was to bring such a world into being.</p>
<p>But Adlan replied, “It seems so to you, because you are fashioned for action, and because you are young. And indeed it is so. Spirits of my kind know well that in due season spirits of your kind will in fact create the new world. But we know also that for us there is another task. It may even be that part of my task is actually to peer so far into the future that I may see and praise those great deeds which you, or some other, are destined to perform.”</p>
<p>When John had reported this speech to me he said, “Then the old man broke off his communication with me, and also ceased prattling to Harry. Presently he thought to me again. His mind embraced me with grave tenderness, and he said, ‘It is time for you to leave me, you very dear and godlike child. I have seen something of the future that lies before you. And though you could bear the foreknowledge without faltering from the way of praise, it is not for me to tell you.’ Next day I met him again, but he was uncommunicative. At the end of the trip, when the Robinsons were stepping out of the boat, he took Harry in his arms and set him on the land, saying in the lingo that passed as Arabic with European residents, ‘ ’L hwaga swoia, quaïs ketír!’ (the little master, very nice). To me he said in his thoughts, ‘To-night, or perhaps to-morrow, I will die. For I have praised the past and the present, and the near future too, with all the insight that Allah has given me. And peering into the farther future I have been able to see nothing but obscure and terrible things which it is not in me to praise. Therefore it is certain that I have fulfilled my task, and may now rest.’ ”</p>
<p>Next day another boat took Harry and his parents to the bathhouses.</p>
