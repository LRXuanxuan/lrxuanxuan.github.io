<h3>第九章　一个年轻人类学家的方法</h3>
<p>约翰从出生起就在研究他的世界；但从十四岁到十七岁，这项研究比过去严肃、系统得多，并逐渐发展成一次范围极广的考察：普通物种究竟具有怎样的本性，取得了什么成就，又陷入了怎样的现实困境。</p>
<p>这项庞大的事业必须秘密进行。约翰决意不让自己引起注意。他必须像一个研究危险野兽习性的博物学家那样行动：拿着望远镜和照相机悄悄跟踪；必要时甚至披上一张偷来的兽皮，染上假气味，混进兽群之中。</p>
<p>遗憾的是，我无法完整叙述约翰生涯的这一阶段，因为我只在其中扮演了很小的角色。他通常采用的伪装，仍是那个早熟却天真的“学生”形象——这副面具当初帮他成功接触了金融家；而他的接近方式，也往往是此前那套“闯门”战术的进一步发展。这些办法又同他近乎恶魔般熟练的魅惑技巧结合起来。无论面对什么猎物，他的方法总会精确调整到对方的心理结构上。这里我只举几个例子，让读者对他的程序有个概念；随后再记录一些他通过这些研究得出的、毫不留情的判断。</p>
<p>为了接近一名内阁大臣，他挑在大臣夫人正要进入私宅的时候，在门外突然发病。读者还记得，约翰对自己的生理反射有异乎寻常的控制力；腺体分泌、体温、消化过程、心跳速度、血液在身体中的分布等等，他都能在一定程度上加以干预。通过小心操纵这些功能，他可以制造出一种症状足够吓人、却不会留下严重后果的病态。于是，一个面色苍白、可怜兮兮的小病号被安置在沙发上，由大臣夫人像母亲一样照料；大臣本人则打电话叫家庭医生。医生还没来，约翰已经变成一个颇有意思的“小康复者”，忙着用怜悯和兴趣编织起细微的纽带，把大臣吸引到自己身边。那位医学权威尽力掩饰困惑，建议先让孩子留在原处休息，等找到父母再说。可约翰哀声说，父母一整天都不在家，房子要到晚上才有人回来。他能不能先留下，等他们回家后再坐出租车回去？等他终于离开那所房子时，他已经对主人心里的一些东西有了了解，还成功弄到了一张“以后再来”的邀请。</p>
<p>人工制造疾病效果如此出色，很快成了他最爱用的手段之一。例如，他靠这种方法接近过一位共产党领袖，同时补上一段悲惨家世：自从父亲“因为组织罢工被炒鱿鱼”以后，家里的生活条件就坏得不像话。同一套假病法再加上适当的宗教装饰，他还用在一位主教、一名天主教神父以及其他几位教士身上。它对一位女议员同样有效。</p>
<p>换一种路数的例子是：约翰给一位著名的天文学家兼物理学家写了一封典型的学生信，天真中又透出聪明，请求允许自己参观天文台。请求获准以后，约翰按约定时间出现，头戴学生帽，口袋里还装着一架小望远镜。这次会面又为他打开了其他门路，使他结识了更多物理学家、生物学家和生理学家。</p>
<p>写信的方法也用在一位著名的剑桥哲学家兼社会评论家身上。这一次，他特意改变笔迹；最终亲自拜访时，又染了头发，戴着深色眼镜，说一口伦敦工人区口音。他准备扮演一个同此前面对天文学家时截然不同的角色，而且极力避免让哲学家产生任何可能：眼前这个少年，会不会就是那位天文学家曾经遇见的孩子？</p>
<p>他用来建立联系的那封信，显然经过了精心设计。它把粗糙的笔迹、拙劣的拼写、对宗教的厌恶、几段惊人却粗陋的哲学分析，以及对那位哲学家著作的热情混合在一起。下面摘一段最有代表性的内容。原信拼写错误很多，我只能在中文里保留它那种稚拙、没受过多少正规教育的口气：</p>
<p>“我爸揍我，因为我说要是上帝造了这个世界，那他造得真够糟。我说你讲过打小孩很蠢，所以他又揍我，因为我居然知道你说过这话。我说能把一个人打倒并不能证明他错了。他说我顶撞父亲就是坏。我说那到底什么叫好什么叫坏，不就是我喜欢什么、不喜欢什么吗。他说这叫亵渎。请让我来找你，问几个关于脑子怎么运转、脑子到底是什么的问题。”</p>
<p>约翰已经去剑桥那位哲学家的住处拜访过好几次，后来忽然收到天文学家寄来的一封便条。这里我应该补充说明：伦敦郊区有一位年轻教师允许约翰把自己的公寓当作收信地址。天文学家请约翰来见“另一个脑子非常清醒的男孩”；那孩子也住在剑桥，而且恰好是那位哲学家的朋友。接下来，两位先生反复设法促成两个“少年天才”见面，而约翰一次次以极有创意、又明显乐在其中的方式破坏安排。这件事非常有趣地照亮了他性格的另一个侧面，可惜我没有篇幅细写。</p>
<p>他也用书信成功接近过一位著名的现代诗人。那一次，信件的风格，以及后来面谈时约翰扮演的人格，都同对付天文学家和哲学家时截然不同。而且，他所针对的并不完全是当时公众眼中、甚至诗人本人自觉意识到的那套精神面貌，而是诗人内心某种后来才逐渐支配其创作的情绪或态度。下面是约翰那封信中最醒目的一段：</p>
<p>“在家里，在学校里，以及我那些混乱而失败的、试图同现代世界达成和解的努力中，我的精神处处受阻。可在所有这些挫败里，最大的安慰和力量来源却是您的诗。我总在想，这究竟为什么。您明明仿佛只是在描写一个受折磨、正在退化的文明，可仅仅是这种描写本身，就赋予它一种尊严和意义，仿佛揭示出：这一切终究不只是徒劳受挫，而是某种辉煌启明到来之前必经的黑暗。”</p>
<p>约翰的调查目标当然不只包括知识界人士，以及政治、社会运动的领袖。他会采用合适的方式，同工程师、技术工人、职员、码头工人交朋友。他曾直接调查南威尔士矿工与达勒姆矿工在心理上的差异；有人偷偷把他带进工会会议；他在浸礼会小教堂里“得救”过；也在招魂会上收到过一个虚构的亡姐从阴间传来的信息。他还曾跟着一辆吉卜赛大篷车，在英格兰南部各郡游荡了几个星期。据说，他能混进那个群体，是因为证明自己很会小偷小摸，也很擅长修锅补盆。</p>
<p>有一项活动他一再重复，投入的时间多得让我觉得同它的重要性不相称。他同家附近一条渔船的船主成了很好的朋友，经常跟着这人和他的伙伴，在河口或外海一待就是一整天、一整夜。我问他为什么如此关注渔民群体，尤其是这两个人，他说：“这些渔夫真是好料子，亚伯和马克又是其中最好的。你看，只要智人面对的工作和生活本身没有真正超出他的能力，他其实挺不错。只有当文明塞给他的任务超过他的智力，或者超过他的想象力时，他才会失败。而一旦失败，这失败就会从里到外把他整个毒坏。”很久以后我才明白，他如此关注海洋其实另有目的。有一阵子，他同一艘沿海双桅帆船的船长关系极好，跟着他在近海航线上来回跑了好几趟。我早该想到，这些冒险的动机之一，就是学习怎样操纵一艘船。</p>
<p>这里还应提到另一件事。约翰对智人的研究已经扩展到欧洲大陆。作为“全家的恩公”，我被交付了一项任务：说服医生和帕克斯跟我一起去法国、德国、意大利和斯堪的纳维亚旅行。约翰总会同行，有时哥哥姐姐也去，有时不去。由于医生不能频繁、长期离开诊所，这种偶尔的家庭旅行还必须由其他不带父母的行程补充。我会宣布，自己“得去巴黎参加一个记者会议”，或者去柏林见一位报业老板，去布拉格报道一个哲学家大会，或者去莫斯科看看他们在教育方面都做些什么。然后我便请求父母让我带上约翰。他们几乎一定会同意；事实上，很多时候，在真正得到许可以前，我们已经把计划的细节安排好了。就这样，约翰得以把自己在不列颠群岛进行的研究延伸到国外。</p>
<p>同约翰一起出国旅行，往往是一次让人自尊受挫的经历。他不仅能在不可思议的短时间里学会一门新语言，而且说得与本地人几乎无法区分；他对外国风俗的掌握、对外国人的心理习惯的直觉，也快得惊人。因此，即使到了我原本很熟悉的国家，他抵达几天以后，我也会发现自己已经被这个同伴远远超过。</p>
<p>如果那门语言对他完全陌生，他的办法非常简单：先把一本语法书和一本词典从头读一遍，再向一两个母语者或者留声机唱片集中学习发音，然后直接前往那个国家。刚到那里时，本地人通常会把他看作一个在国外住过较长时间、因此对母语稍有生疏的本国孩子。对于大多数欧洲语言，一个星期左右以后，已经不会有人怀疑他曾经离开过这个国家。再往后，随着旅行范围扩展，他估计即便是日语这种东方语言，从登陆之日起算，两周内也足以彻底掌握。</p>
<p>陪约翰在欧洲大陆旅行时，我常常问自己：为什么我竟允许这个奇异的生物永远把我当作奴仆？我有很多时间思考，因为约翰经常独自离开，去追踪某个作家、科学家、神父、政治家或群众煽动家；要不就是坐三等甚至四等车厢接触工人，或者同筑路工聊天。进行这些活动时，他往往宁愿不带我。可是每隔一阵，我又会被需要，用来扮演监护人或者随行家庭教师。有时约翰特别想避免让别人看出他的独特优势，事前便会仔细训练我，告诉我应该问哪些问题、作哪些观察。</p>
<p>例如有一次，他说服我带他去见一位著名精神科医生。约翰本人扮演一个发育迟缓、神经质的孩子，我则同那位专业人士讨论他的“病例”。这次会面后来发展成一段针对约翰的正式治疗，还包括精神科医生偶尔同我单独见面、讨论“治疗进展”。可怜的医生始终不知道，他那个看上去完全沉浸在疯狂幻想里的小病人，其实一直在拿医生本人做实验；至于我提出的那些听起来颇聪明、却常常令人烦躁的问题，也无一不是那个“病人”预先想好，再塞进我嘴里的。</p>
<p>我为什么会任由约翰这样利用我？为什么允许他占去如此多的时间和注意力，甚至严重干扰我的记者生涯？很难说他是一个“可爱”的人。当然，对记者或传记作者来说，他是独一无二的材料；我也早已决定，总有一天要把自己知道的一切告诉世界。但很明显，即使在这个早期阶段，约翰那尚未长成羽翼的精神，对我的吸引力已经远不只是新奇。我想，那时我已经隐约感觉到：他正在摸索一种全新的精神方向，而它一旦成形，也许会把整个存在照亮成另一种样子。我希望自己也能分享到其中的一点光。直到很久以后，我才明白，他真正看见的东西，从本质上说已经超出了普通人心智所能达到的范围。</p>
<p>不过在这一阶段，真正降临到约翰身上的“启示”，似乎只有一个毁灭性的确信：普通物种是何等徒劳。面对这一发现，他有时只是轻蔑；有时会因人类世界注定遭逢的命运而恐惧，也会害怕自己竟被卷入其中。但另外一些时候，他表现出的却是怜悯；还有一些时候，则是尖刻的愉悦；再有一些时候，则是一种更加平静的喜悦，在那里，怜悯、恐怖以及冷峻的幸灾式快感，都被奇异地转化成了另一种东西。</p>

<h3><span id="a339"></span>CHAPTER IX. METHODS OF A YOUNG ANTHROPOLOGIST</h3>
<p><span class="t28">J</span><span class="t31">OHN</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">HAD</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">BEEN</span><span class="t30"> </span>engaged in studying his world ever since he was born; but from fourteen to seventeen this study became much more earnest and methodical than it had been, and took the form of a far-reaching examination of the normal species in respect of its nature, achievement and present plight.</p>
<p>This vast enterprise had to be carried on in secret. John was determined not to attract attention to himself. He had to behave as a naturalist who studies the habits of some dangerous brute by stalking it with field-glass and camera, and by actually insinuating himself among the herd under a stolen skin, and an assumed odour.</p>
<p>Unfortunately, I cannot give at all a full account of this phase of John’s career, for I played but a minor part in it. His disguise was always the precocious but naïve “schoolboy” character which had served him so well in making contact with financiers; and his approach was very often a development of the “gate-crashing” tactics which he had used in the same connexion. This technique was combined with his diabolically skilled vamping. Always his methods were nicely adjusted to the mentality of the particular quarry. I will mention only a few examples, to give the reader some idea of the procedure, and then I will pass on to record some of the ruthless judgements which his researches enabled him to make.</p>
<p>He effected contact with a Cabinet minister by being taken ill outside the great man’s private residence at the moment when the minister’s wife was entering the house. It will be remembered that John had remarkable control over his organic reflexes, and could influence his glandular secretions, his temperature, his digestive processes, the rate of his heart-beat, the distribution of blood in his body, and so on. By careful manipulation of these controls he was able to produce a disorder the symptoms of which were sufficiently alarming though its after-effects were not serious. A pale pathetic wreck, he was laid on a couch and mothered by the minister’s wife while the minister himself phoned for the family doctor. Before the physician arrived, John was already an intriguing little convalescent, and was busy attaching the minister to himself with subtle bonds of compassion and interest. The medical pundit did his best to conceal his bewilderment, and recommended that the boy should rest where he was till his parents were found. But John wailed that his parents were away for the day, and the house would be shut till the evening. Might he stay until their return, and then go home in a taxi? By the time he left the house he had already gained some insight into the mind of his host, and had secured an invitation to come again.</p>
<p>The artificial illness had proved so successful that it became one of his favourite methods. He used it, for instance, to make contact with a Communist leader, supplementing it with an account of his shocking home-conditions since his father “got the sack for organizing a strike.” Variants of the same method of artificial illness, with appropriate religious trimmings, were used also upon a bishop, a Catholic priest, and several other clerical gentlemen. It also proved effective with a woman M.P.</p>
<p>As an example of a different approach I may mention that John bagged an eminent astronomer-physicist by writing him a schoolboyish letter of the naïve-brilliant kind, begging to be shown over an observatory. The request was granted, and John turned up at the appointed rendezvous equipped with schoolcap and a pocket telescope. This meeting led to other connexions among physicists, biologists, physiologists.</p>
<p>The epistolary method was also used upon a well-known Cambridge philosopher and social writer. This time the handwriting was disguised; and, when finally John called on his man, he turned up with dyed hair, dark glasses and a Cockney accent. He intended to assume a very different character from that which had served with the astronomer; and he was anxious to avoid all possibility that the philosopher might identify him as the lad whom the astronomer had encountered.</p>
<p>The letter by which he effected this contact was nicely adapted to its purpose. It combined crude handwriting, bad spelling, dislike of religion, scraps of striking though crude philosophical analysis, and enthusiasm for the philosopher’s books. I quote a characteristic passage:</p>
<p>My father beet me for saying if god made the world he made a mess. I said you said it was silly to beet children, so he beet me again for knowing you said it. I said being abel to beet a fellow didn’t proove he was wrong. He said I was evil to answer back on my father. I said wots good and evil anyhow but just wot I like and dont like. He said it was blastphemy. Please let me call and ask you some questiuns about how a mind works and wot it is.</p>
<p>John had already made several visits to the philosopher’s rooms in Cambridge, when he received a note from the astronomer. I should have explained that a young schoolmaster in a London suburb was allowing John to use his flat as a postal address. The astronomer asked John to come and meet “another very wide-awake boy,” who lived in Cambridge and was a friend of the philosopher. The ingenuity and relish which John displayed in defeating the repeated efforts of both men to bring about this meeting afforded me an amusing sidelight on his character, but I have not space to describe it.</p>
<p>The epistolary approach was used with equal effect upon a well-known modern poet. In this case the style of the letter and the persona which John assumed in the subsequent interviews were very different from those which had served for the astronomer and the philosopher. They were adjusted, moreover, not precisely to the conscious mentality of the poet as he was then known to the public and to himself, but to a mood or attitude in him which was subsequently to dominate his work. I quote the most striking passage from John’s letter:</p>
<p>In all my hideous frustration of spirit, at home, at school, and in my confused attempts to come to terms with the modern world, the greatest comfort and source of strength is your poetry. How is it, I wonder, that, although you seem simply to describe a tortured and degenerate civilization, the very describing lends it dignity and significance, as though revealing it to be, after all, not mere frustration, but the necessary darkness before some glorious enlightenment.</p>
<p>John’s efforts were not directed solely upon the intelligentsia and the leaders of political and social movements. Using appropriate methods, he made friends with engineers, artisans, clerks, dock-labourers. He acquired first-hand information about the mental differences between South Wales and Durham coal-miners. He was smuggled into Trade Union meetings. He had his soul saved in Baptist chapels. He received messages from a mythical dead sister in spiritualists’ séances. He spent some weeks attached to a gipsy caravan, touring the southern counties. This post he apparently gained by showing his proficiency at petty theft and at repairing pots and pans.</p>
<p>One activity he repeated again and again, spending on it a length of time which seemed to me disproportionate to its significance. He became very friendly with the owner of a fishing smack near home, and would often spend days or nights with this man and his mate on the estuary or the open sea. When I asked John why he gave so much attention to the fishing community and these two men in particular, he said, “Well, they’re damned fine stuff, these fishermen, and Abe and Mark are two of the best. You see, when Homo sapiens is up against the sort of job and the sort of life that’s not really beyond him, he’s all right. It’s only when civilization gives him a job that’s too much for his intelligence or too much for his imagination that he fails. And then the failure poisons him through and through.” It was not till long afterwards that I realized his ulterior purpose in giving so much attention to the sea. At one time he became very friendly with the skipper of a coasting schooner, and made several voyages with him up and down the narrow seas. I ought to have realized that one motive of these adventures was the desire to learn how to handle a ship.</p>
<p>One other matter should be mentioned here. John’s study of Homo sapiens now extended to the European Continent. In my capacity of family benefactor I was charged with the task of persuading Doc and Pax to join me on excursions to France, Germany, Italy, Scandinavia. John always accompanied us, with or without his brother and sister. Since Doc could not leave his practice frequently or for long at a time, these occasional family holidays had to be supplemented by trips in which the parents did not participate. I would announce that I had to “run over to Paris to a journalists’ conference,” or to Berlin to see a newspaper proprietor, or to Prague to report on a conference of philosophers, or to Moscow to see what they were doing about education. Then I would ask the parents to let me take John. Consent was certain, and our plans were often laid in detail before it was given. In this way John was enabled to carry on abroad the researches that he was already pursuing in the British Isles.</p>
<p>Foreign travel in John’s company was apt to be a humiliating experience. Not only did he learn to speak a new language in an incredibly short time and in a manner indistinguishable from that of the native; he was also amazingly quick at learning foreign customs and intuiting foreign attitudes of mind. Consequently, even in countries with which I was familiar I found myself outclassed by my companion within a few days after his arrival.</p>
<p>When it was a case of learning a language entirely new to him, John simply read through a grammar and a dictionary, took concentrated courses of pronunciation from one or two natives or from gramophone records, and proceeded to the country. At this stage he would be regarded by natives as a native child who had been in foreign parts for some time and had lost touch somewhat with his own speech. At the end of a week or so, in the case of most European languages, no one would suspect that he had ever been out of the country. Later in his career, when his travels took him farther afield, he reckoned that even an Eastern language, such as Japanese, could be thoroughly mastered in a fortnight from his landing in the country.</p>
<p>Travelling with John on the European Continent I often asked myself why I allowed this strange being to hold me perpetually as his slave. I had much time for thought, for John was as often as not away hunting some writer or scientist or priest, some politician or popular agitator. Or else he was getting in touch with the workers by travelling in third or fourth class railway carriages, or talking to navvies. While he was thus engaged he often preferred to be without me. Every now and then, however, I was needed to act the part of guardian or travelling tutor. Sometimes, when John was particularly anxious to avoid giving any suggestion of his unique superiority, he would coach me carefully before the interview, priming me with questions to ask and observations to make.</p>
<p>On one occasion, for instance, he persuaded me to take him to an eminent psychiatrist. John himself played the part of a backward and neurotic child while I discussed his case with the professional man. This interview led to a course of treatment for John, and occasional meetings between the psychiatrist and myself to discuss progress. The poor man remained throughout ignorant that his small patient, seemingly so absorbed in his own crazy fantasies, was all the while experimenting on the physician, and that my own intelligent, though often provoking, questions had all originated in the mind of the patient himself.</p>
<p>Why did I let John use me thus? Why did I allow him to occupy so much of my time and attention, and to interfere so seriously with my career as a journalist? It could hardly be said that he was lovable. Of course, he was unique material for the journalist or the biographer, and I had already decided that some day I would tell the world all that I knew of him. But it is clear that even at this early stage the unfledged spirit of John exercised over me a fascination more subtle than that of novelty. I think I felt already that he was groping towards some kind of spiritual re-orientation which would put the whole of existence in a new light. And I hoped that I myself should catch some gleam of this illumination. Not till much later did I realize that his vision was essentially beyond the range of normal human minds.</p>
<p>For the present the only kind of illumination which came to John was apparently a devastating conviction of the futility of the normal species. To this discovery he reacted sometimes with mere contempt, sometimes with horror at the doom which awaited the human world, and with terror at his own entanglement in it. But on other occasions his mood was compassion, and on others again sardonic delight, and yet on others delight of a more serene kind in which compassion and horror and grim relish were strangely transmuted.</p>
