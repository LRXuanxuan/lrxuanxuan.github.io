<h2>i. 保罗承担自己的任务</h2>
<p>这一精神成就既已稳固完成，保罗便准备以新的眼光观察伦敦和整个世界。事实上，他如今已经很适合执行我为他预定的主要工作。</p>
<p>他自己本来完全可以满足于继续教书，只是比从前更有活力、更认真；其余时间，则以更深入的理解继续自己那场悠闲的心灵国度旅行。</p>
<p>可是，他很快就会发现，自己还被要求做更多事情。这个刚刚学会安静功课的人，现在反而必须开始一种比过去任何尝试都更加积极、更加坚决的生活。</p>
<p>新的目标最初只缓慢地在他心中破晓。随着轮廓逐渐清楚，也逐渐显得可怕，他既渴望它，又害怕它。</p>
<p>最终，他对这项任务突然获得准确而完整的认识，是在坎农街大型火车总站外面。当时他坐在一辆停着不动、却仍不断震动的公共汽车顶层，看一股股戴圆顶礼帽或软帽的商人匆匆涌向办公室。</p>
<p>就在这一刻，一阵猛烈饥渴突然冲遍全身——或者，那其实是一种神圣召唤？</p>
<p>他迫切想精确而有理解地把握整个现代世界这一现象；想把它同自己不久前获得的启示联系起来观察；想发现它更深层的意义。</p>
<p>这种新欲望强烈到如此程度，他甚至不得不克制自己，免得当场下车走进街道，逐个拦住人群中的每一个人，问他：</p>
<p>“你这一生到底在干什么？为什么？”</p>
<p>保罗被自己欲望的猛烈程度吓住，也被它那种怪异、甚至近乎疯狂的性质吓住。</p>
<p>他强迫自己冷静下来，开始反驳这种冲动。</p>
<p>这会妨碍自己的本职工作，也会破坏内心安宁。</p>
<p>他根本不适合进行这种“研究世界”的工作。</p>
<p>而且，一个人不应该把整个人生都用于单纯理解；他必须行动，必须服务。</p>
<p>即使理解得再深，世界也不会因此变好半点。</p>
<p>可是，随着日子过去，这种新欲望反而抓得越来越紧。</p>
<p>他感觉它正长成一种无法抵抗、近乎疯狂的执念。</p>
<p>他拼命想把它从心里驱逐出去，结果反而几乎精神崩溃。</p>
<p>很明显，如果我强迫他违背这种绝望抵抗来执行我的目的，他一定会把工作做得很糟。</p>
<p>因此我决定，为了得到他自愿合作，必须尝试坦率而精确地向他解释我同他的关系。</p>
<p>这会非常困难；如果不是一个经过谨慎挑选、又长期准备的人，根本不可能做到。</p>
<p>一天夜里，保罗躺在床上时，我在他的想象中构造出一个极其具体的自我形象——也就是，如果他用自己的眼睛看见我，我大概会是什么样子。</p>
<p>他看见一个巨大的灰色人形巨碑，长着蛇一样的眼睛。</p>
<p>随后，我通过他自己的想象同他说话。</p>
<p>我解释说：我是一个生活在极遥远未来的人类，居住于海王星；我的任务，是研究“人”在地球上的各个时代。</p>
<p>为了这项工作，我选择他作为工具。</p>
<p>如果必要，我可以通过内在强迫逼他替我工作；但如果他愿意主动参与，工作显然会进行得更好。</p>
<p>我又向他说明：通过帮助我把一部分地球人类历史转译进我们物种的意识，他不仅是在为我服务，也是在为自己的物种服务。因为我们的心智水平——我告诉他——远远超过你们。</p>
<p>第二天早晨，保罗惊讶地回忆起夜里的经验，却把它当成一场梦抛开了。</p>
<p>接下来几个夜晚，我再次影响他，向他透露更多有关我的世界，以及我对他能够施加何种力量的细节。</p>
<p>我还让他去找两本书。</p>
<p>其中一本已经出版，是J. W. 邓恩的《时间实验》（An Experiment with Time）。我告诉他：从海王星人的思想看来，这本书在哲学上相当幼稚；不过，它也许能帮助他理解未来事件怎样侵入现在。</p>
<p>另一本书则还没有问世，它将叫作《最后与最初的人》（Last and First Men）。</p>
<p>当保罗真的找到第一本书时，他受到极大震动；几个月以后，当他又读到第二本书的手稿时，震动更为强烈。</p>
<p>此时，他终于确信：我同他的交流是真实的。</p>
<p>有一阵子，这个事实令他既恐惧又反感：另一颗人类心灵竟能接触他的每一个念头，甚至还能诱使他去想、去渴望一些按照他本来天性根本不会想、不会渴望的东西。</p>
<p>自己的性格竟在很大程度上是另一颗心灵塑造的产物——起初在他看来，这简直是对个人隐私、甚至对身份本身的不可容忍侵犯。</p>
<p>因此，我不得不一夜又一夜耐心提醒他：即使没有我对他的力量，他也同样只是许多“非自身因素”的表现；凡是他觉得最具个性的东西，其实都不真正属于他。</p>
<p>我对他说：</p>
<p>“你忘了，你的天性本来就是无限复杂因果之网的产物：进化，人类历史东冲西撞的混乱运动，最后还有你的父母、凯瑟琳、战争以及你的学生。</p>
<p>“那么，现在不过是发现又有一种此前没有怀疑过的影响参与了你的塑造——而且这种影响还大幅提升了你的精神高度——你就要反抗吗？</p>
<p>“如果是这样，那么你不久前在高地上的启示还远没有达到应有深度。</p>
<p>“你并没有真正从心里明白：赤裸裸的私人个体性其实微不足道；真正光荣或卑劣的，是环境加在这个个体性上的形式。</p>
<p>“我自己和你没有本质区别。</p>
<p>“我也是庞大环境因果网络的产物；而我的心灵，很可能同样暴露在某些我不知道、比我更高等的存在面前，供它们审视，甚至供它们影响。</p>
<p>“和你相比，我的心智层次确实更高；但我天性的这种更丰富、更有意义，并不能算作我的功劳。</p>
<p>“那就像一首交响曲最后的和弦，竟把此前所有和弦注入它的意义归功于自己。</p>
<p>“当然，在你我身上，在所有人身上，在一切生命，甚至所有低于生命的存在中，都有同一个普遍奇迹：自发地‘做’。</p>
<p>“这就是一切存在最根本的生命。</p>
<p>“受环境帮助或限制，有些存在能够做得多，有些做得少。</p>
<p>“究竟是谁做多、谁做少，有什么重要？</p>
<p>“音乐要达到完整，需要一切音色。</p>
<p>“真正重要的只有音乐，而不是抬高这件或那件乐器。”</p>
<p>几天以后，保罗已经在相当程度上同自己的命运达成和解，以至于开始兴奋地考虑：怎样满足这股新激情——此前他一直只能很费力地压制它。</p>
<p>他投入任务时所怀的热情，把食欲般旺盛的饥渴与履行神圣责任的高昂混合在一起。</p>
<p>我又进一步准备他，让他认识到：最近高地上的那次体验，正是所有典型现代思想所缺乏的生命火花；是那一个失落、却关系一切的词，没有它，现代思想那堆混乱言辞永远不可能真正有意义；也是那道微小而安静的声音，只有它在每颗心里工作，现代世界才有可能获得和平。</p>
<p>当然，保罗知道自己的这种体验绝非独一无二。</p>
<p>其他时代的人，曾比他更深地进入这种境界。</p>
<p>即使在他自己的时代，这种体验偶尔也会发生。</p>
<p>可是，它几乎从未真正进入公共的思想生活。</p>
<p>少数经历过它的人，几乎总会退进自己的内在堡垒。</p>
<p>他们摆脱了使现代思想混浊的渣滓，却同时在不自知中抛弃了一笔此前人类从未获得过的财富。</p>
<p>他们满足于徒劳重复古老真理，却听不见一种正在人类心灵里结结巴巴尝试表达自己的新真理。</p>
<p>他们确实能非常清楚地看出现代精神最核心的错误：相信“现实”全部包含在感觉世界之中。</p>
<p>但他们反抗这种愚蠢时，又走向另一种错误：宣称这个世界不过是幻影和陷阱，并幼稚地设想，灵魂在狂喜中逃离幻觉以后，终于发现自己摆脱一切束缚，而且永生不灭。</p>
<p>我花了很大力气让保罗看清楚：对于那些只浅浅进入精神生活的人而言，这恰恰是所有陷阱中最阴险、最致命的一种。</p>
<p>高地启示后的第二天，保罗曾对自己说：他已经从“自我”这艘正在沉没的船上逃了出来。</p>
<p>世界各地成群结队的男女同胞只能在漩涡中从一道浪头挣扎向下一道浪头，偶尔才在某个浪峰上停一下，四下望去，意识到自己正在一片荒芜水域上航行；而且所有人最终都必然沉没。</p>
<p>在他看来，人们受“珍视自我”这种东西折磨；同时，又以更高贵的方式被个人之爱折磨、提升——尤其是在看着自己所爱的人受苦时，那种痛苦会倍增。</p>
<p>这的确是他们高于野兽的主要地方。</p>
<p>可也正是这种东西，把他们像正在溺死的蜉蝣一样拴在那片荒水上。</p>
<p>他们的关切几乎全部是个人性的：自爱、仇恨，或者爱。</p>
<p>但他们越来越清楚地看见，在存在这片荒水里，任何地方都不可能为个人精神提供永久家园；所以，任何人所爱的人也不可能得到永久安慰。</p>
<p>每个人，以及每个人所爱的人，都终将像漏水船只一样沉重地陷入老年。</p>
<p>要是他们至少能排成战斗队形，为某种伟大事业而沉没，那也好！</p>
<p>可他们的航行命令没有传达到手里；或者传来时已经被破坏得面目全非，根本无法指出明确方向。</p>
<p>这就是启示之后第二天，保罗给事情整理出的图景。</p>
<p>而他自己，似乎通过某种奇迹，从那艘正在沉没的“自我之船”上逃出来了。</p>
<p>他的逃脱仅仅依赖一次想象力的跳跃：他飞上高空，从那里无私地观看自己和一切自我的沉船；怀着一种奇异、安静的同情，却不再反抗。</p>
<p>然而后来，在我持续影响下，他逐渐看出：这个意象既错误又危险。</p>
<p>他根本没有逃脱。</p>
<p>没有任何自我能够逃脱。</p>
<p>而如今，他终于明白，逃脱也根本不值得追求。</p>
<p>因为他已经看见某种美；如果所有自我都能看见这种美，它们会甘愿为之受苦，甚至甘愿为之毁灭。</p>
<p>此刻，保罗再次把注意力转向那个自己负有观察任务的世界。</p>
<p>一个他长期以来模糊知道、却从未清楚说给自己听的真理，突然变得既清晰又紧迫。</p>
<p>他终于毫不含糊地从宇宙尺度看见自己物种的处境。</p>
<p>他看见，自己与同时代人究竟应该怎样处理他们的世界。</p>
<p>命运给了他们一个所有早期时代都没有得到的机会。</p>
<p>他们误打误撞发现了控制人类种族命运的力量。</p>
<p>他们已经在一定程度上掌握物质自然，而且看起来，未来还会获得远为巨大的控制力。</p>
<p>世界已经第一次真正成为“一个世界”。</p>
<p>而且，将来某一天，他们还必然会学会重新塑造人性本身——既可能向善，也可能向恶。</p>
<p>如果他们能够开始超越自身意志的局限，如果他们的感受能够越过自我关切、部落嫉妒以及那些不断反复出现的幼稚执念，那么他们不仅有可能建造一座由幸福个体组成的乌托邦，还能把整个地球塑造成一件统一而极其强大的精神乐器，奏出此前从未被想象过的音乐。</p>
<p>至于遥远未来的人类究竟应该怎样处理自己，没有人能够知道。</p>
<p>但是，保罗心中如今有一个信念格外确定：在那些使人类各部落相互冲突的琐碎而自相矛盾的目标之上，存在一个目标，应该成为今天所有人最高、不可侵犯的共同目标。</p>
<p>那就是：在每一个现存人类身上唤起尽可能完整的生命力，并让所有人能够彼此和谐合作，共同创造一种更高贵、更有生命的人性。</p>
<p>在整个西方世界，甚至东方世界，许多人的心中都正模糊地开始出现某种类似目标。</p>
<p>然而，他们虽然人数不少，在整个人类中却仍只是极微小的一部分；他们的视野并不清楚，意志也不坚定。</p>
<p>同他们对立的，不仅有许多强暴力量，还有习惯的巨大死重——这种死重不仅存在于外部世界，也存在于他们自己身上。</p>
<p>这样一小群摸索中的心灵，究竟有多大机会把整个庞大世界硬生生扭向一种新的生活方式？</p>
<p>保罗如今带着急切兴趣——不，带着激情、敬畏以及一种冷峻的兴奋——正式开始自己的任务：观察并衡量你们时代那场短促而剧烈的小戏剧。</p>
<p>为了不至于看丢这场戏更广阔的意义，他首先思考人类努力在宇宙中的意义——这种意义，人类自己也才刚刚第一次开始试探着察觉。</p>
<p>他这次思考最直接的产物，是下面这首诗：</p>
<p>人类是否是一种疾病，</p>
<p>一颗衰老恒星的血液</p>
<p>已经无力抵抗？</p>
<p>当星座凝视我们时，</p>
<p>远处那样锐利地注视着我们的，</p>
<p>是否是恐惧、厌恶、惊骇——</p>
<p>像面对一个染上瘟疫、</p>
<p>从美中堕落的兄弟？</p>
<p>不。</p>
<p>星辰不过是气体。</p>
<p>它们并不注视我们，</p>
<p>不审判我们，</p>
<p>什么也不关心。</p>
<p>我们独自在空洞天空之下。</p>
<p>那么，那些虽遥不可及的小饰物，</p>
<p>便是我们的：</p>
<p>尚未取用，</p>
<p>尚未损坏；</p>
<p>那些古老神灯里沉睡、</p>
<p>还无人命令的精灵，</p>
<p>也是我们的。</p>
<p>凭心智之权，</p>
<p>它们属于我们；</p>
<p>凭痛苦之权，</p>
<p>它们属于我们；</p>
<p>凭漫长自省之权，</p>
<p>它们属于我们；</p>
<p>凭我们终有一天</p>
<p>必须拿它们来完成的一切事业，</p>
<p>它们属于我们。</p>
<p>等我们的日子到来，</p>
<p>等我们熬过童年，</p>
<p>等我们终于摆脱</p>
<p>反复发作的老年疯狂。</p>
<p>到那时，终将是人，</p>
<p>而不再是争吵不休的猴群，</p>
<p>占据宇宙。</p>

<h2>i. PAUL UNDERTAKES HIS TASK</h2>
<p>WITH THIS SPIRITUAL achievement solidly accomplished, Paul was ready to observe London and the world with new eyes. In fact he was now well fitted to carry out the main work that I had purposed for him. He himself would have been content simply to carry on his teaching with new vitality and conscientiousness, and for the rest to pursue with deeper understanding his leisurely tour of the countries of the mind. But he was soon to discover that more was demanded of him, and that he who had so lately learned the lesson of quietness, must now embark on a life more active, more resolute, than anything that he had formerly attempted.</p>
<p>His new purpose dawned on him slowly, and as it became clear to him and formidable, he both lusted in it and feared it. His final and precise apprehension of it flashed upon him as he was waiting on the top of a stationary and throbbing motor-omnibus outside the great railway terminus of Cannon Street, watching the streams of bowlered or soft-hatted business men, hurrying to their offices. There surged through him suddenly a violent hunger, or was it a sacred call, to apprehend precisely and with understanding the whole phenomenon of the modern world, to see it in relation to his recent vision, to discover its deeper significance. So insistent was this new craving that he had to restrain himself from descending there and then into the street to ask each of the crowd in turn what he was really doing with his life, and why.</p>
<p>Paul was frightened by the violence of his own desire, and by its quality of freakishness, even of insanity. He pulled himself together and argued against it. It would interfere with his own work, and with his peace of mind. He was not fitted for any such world-study. Moreover, he ought not to give up his life to merely apprehending; he must do, must serve. However well he should understand, the world would be none the better.</p>
<p>As the days passed, however, this new craving took firmer hold of him. He felt it growing into an irresistible, an insane obsession. So violently did he strive to exorcize it, that he was threatened with breakdown. Clearly, if he were forced to pursue my purpose against this desperate reluctance, he would pursue it ill. I therefore decided that, in order to gain his willing co-operation, I must attempt to give him a frank and precise explanation of my relations with him. This would be a difficult undertaking, and one which would have been impossible to carry out on any but a carefully selected and prepared individual.</p>
<p>One night, as Paul was lying in bed, I constructed in his imagination a detailed image of myself as I should have appeared to his eyes. He saw me as a great grey human monolith, snake-eyed. I then spoke to him, through his own imagination. I explained that I was a human being of the remote future, living upon Neptune, and that it was my task to study the Terrestrial ages of Man. For this work, I said, I had chosen him as my instrument. If necessary I could by an inner compulsion force him to work for me, but the work would fare better if he would enter into it willingly. I explained that, by helping me to render something of Terrestrial history into the consciousness of my own species, which, I said, was mentally far more advanced than his own, he would be serving not only me but his own kind.</p>
<p>Next morning Paul recalled the night’s experience with amazement, but dismissed it as a dream. On succeeding nights I influenced him again, giving him more detail of my world and my power over himself. I referred him, moreover, to two books. One of these, already published, was An Experiment with Time, by J. W. Dunne, This work, I told him, though to the Neptunian mind it was philosophically naive, might help him to understand the incursion of future events into the present. The other book, which would be called Last and First Men, had yet to appear.</p>
<p>When Paul encountered the first of these books, he was profoundly disturbed, and still more so when, some months later, he read the other in manuscript. He now became convinced of the reality of my intercourse with him. For a while he was terrified and revolted at the knowledge that another human mind had access to his every thought, and could even induce him to think and desire as it was not in his own nature to think and desire. That his character should be largely the product of another mind seemed to him at first an intolerable violation of his privacy, nay, of his identity. I had therefore to remind him, patiently and night by night, that even apart from my power over him he was an expression of influences other than himself, that whatever was distinctive of him was not truly his at all. ‘You forget’, I said, ‘that your nature is the product of an infinite mesh of causes; of evolution, of the hither-thither turmoil of human history, and finally of your parents, of Katherine, of the war, and of your boys. Will you then rebel because yet another influence, hitherto unsuspected, is discovered to have part in you, an influence, too, which has very greatly increased your spiritual stature? If so, your recent illumination on the Down was less deep than it should have been. You have not realized in your heart that the bare private individuality is a negligible thing, that what is glorious or base is the form which circumstance imposes on it. I myself am in the same case with you. I am the product of a vast web of circumstance, and my mind is probably open to the critical inspection and even the influence of I know not what beings superior to myself. Compared with you I am indeed of a higher order of mentality, but no credit is due to me for this greater richness and significance of my nature. As well might the concluding chord of a symphony take credit for the significance poured into it by all its predecessors. There is of course in both of us, and in all men, and all living things, and all sub-vital beings, the one universal miracle of spontaneous doing, which is the essential life of all existence. Through the aid or the limitation of circumstance some may do much, others little. What matter which of us does which? All the tones of the music are needed for the music’s perfection. And it is the music alone that matters, not the glorification of this instrument or that.’</p>
<p>In a few days Paul was reconciled to his fate to such an extent that he began eagerly to debate with himself how he should gratify the new passion which hitherto he had only with difficulty restrained. The fervour with which he embarked on his task combined the lusty hunger of an appetite with the exaltation of a sacred duty.</p>
<p>I further prepared him by making him realize that his recent experience on the Down was the vital spark lacking to all typical modern thought, the missing and all-relevant word without which its jumble of verbiage could never make sense, the still small voice which alone, working in each heart, could ever bring peace to the modern world. Paul knew, of course, that this experience of his was by no means unique. Other ages had more profoundly entered into it. Even in his own age it sometimes occurred. But it had played little part in the common life of the mind; the few who had known it had nearly always withdrawn into their inner fortress. Disengaging themselves from the dross which confuses modern thought, they had at the same time unwittingly rejected a treasure which had never before come within man’s reach. Contenting themselves with vain repetitions of old truths, they were deaf to the new truth which was as yet but stammeringly expressing itself in men’s minds. They could see very clearly the central error of the modern spirit, namely, the belief that reality is wholly included in the world of sense. Revolting from this folly, they erroneously declared this world a phantom and a snare, and naively conceived that in their ecstasy the soul, escaping from illusion, found herself at last untrammelled and immortal. I was at pains to make Paul see unmistakably that, for those who have entered superficially into the life of the spirit, this is the most insidious, the most lethal of all snares.</p>
<p>On the day after his illumination upon the Down, Paul had told himself that he had escaped from the foundering vessel of himself. The swarms of his fellow men and women all over the world could do nothing but labour incessantly from wave to wave of the maelstrom, pausing only now and then on some crest to look around them and realize that they were ploughing a waste of waters; and that one and all must sink. Paul saw them tormented by self-prizing; though also more nobly tortured and exalted by personal love, and by the intensified pain of watching the beloved’s pain. This it was, indeed, that chiefly dignified them above the beasts. But this also it was that tethered them to the waste of waters, like foundering may-flies. Their interest was almost wholly personal, either in self-regard, or hate, or love; but increasingly they saw that in the waste of existence there could be no lasting home for personal spirits anywhere, and so no permanency of comfort for anyone’s beloved. Each of them, and each beloved, must settle heavily down into old age, like leaky vessels. If only they could have sunk in battle array fighting for some great cause! But their sailing orders had not come through to them, or had been so mutilated that they found no clear direction.</p>
<p>Thus Paul had figured things out on the day after his illumination. By some miracle he himself, seemingly, had escaped from the foundering vessel of himself. He had escaped simply by a leap of the imagination, a soaring flight into the upper air; whence, selfless, he could watch the shipwreck of himself and all selves with a strange, still compassion, but without revolt. Yet subsequently, under my continued influence, he saw that this image was false and dangerous. He had not escaped. No self could escape. And now at last he realized that escape was not desirable. For he had seen something of the beauty for which all selves, if they could but see it, would suffer gladly, and gladly be annihilated.</p>
<p>Paul now once more turned his attention to the world that it was his task to observe. Suddenly a truth, which he had long vaguely known, but had never before clearly stated to himself, became both clear and urgent. At last he saw the situation of his species unambiguously from the cosmical point of view. He saw what it was that he and his contemporaries should be doing with their world. Fate had given them an opportunity which had been withheld from every early age. They had stumbled on the power of controlling the destiny of the human race. They had already gained some mastery over physical nature, and a far greater mastery was seemingly in store for them. Already their world had become one world, as it had never been before. Moreover, they must some day learn how to remake human nature itself, for good or for ill. If they could begin to outgrow their limitations of will, if they could feel beyond their self-regard, their tribal jealousies and their constant puerile obsessions, then they could begin not only to construct a Utopia of happy individuals, but to make of their planet a single and most potent instrument of the spirit, capable of music hitherto unconceived.</p>
<p>What humanity should do with itself in the far future, no one could tell; but one conviction now stood out with certainty in Paul’s mind, namely, that over all the trivial and inconsistent purposes that kept the tribes of men in conflict with one another, there was one purpose which should be the supreme and inviolate purpose of all men today, namely, to evoke in every extant human being the fullest possible aliveness, and to enable all men to work; together harmoniously for the making of a nobler, a more alive human nature.</p>
<p>Some such purpose as this was obscurely dawning in many minds throughout the Western and even the Eastern world. But though they were many, they were a minute proportion of the whole; and their vision was unclear, their will unsure. Opposed to them were many violent powers, and the dead-weight of custom, not only in the world but in themselves also. What chance was there that these few groping minds would wrench the great world into a new way of living? Paul now with eager interest, nay with passion, with awe, with grim zest, took up his task of watching and assessing the intense little drama of your age.</p>
<p>Lest he should lose sight of the wider bearings of that drama, Paul first meditated on the cosmical significance of human endeavour, which now for the first time was beginning to be tentatively apprehended by man himself. The immediate outcome of his meditation was this poem:</p>
<p>Is man a disease</p>
<p>that the blood of a senile star</p>
<p>cannot resist?</p>
<p>And when the constellations regard us.</p>
<p>is it fear, disgust, horror.</p>
<p>(at a plague-stricken brother</p>
<p>derelict from beauty).</p>
<p>that stares yonder</p>
<p>so sharply?</p>
<p>Gas the stars are.</p>
<p>They regard us not.</p>
<p>they judge us not.</p>
<p>they care for nothing.</p>
<p>We are alone in the hollow sky.</p>
<p>Then ours, though out of reach, those trinkets.</p>
<p>withheld.</p>
<p>unspoiled;</p>
<p>ours the sleeping and uncommanded genii</p>
<p>of those old lamps;</p>
<p>ours by right of mentality;</p>
<p>by right of agony.</p>
<p>by right of long heart-searching.</p>
<p>by right of all we must do with them</p>
<p>when our day comes.</p>
<p>when we have outlived our childishness.</p>
<p>when we have outgrown</p>
<p>the recurrent insanity of senility.</p>
<p>Then surely at last man.</p>
<p>and not bickering monkeys.</p>
<p>shall occupy the universe.</p>
