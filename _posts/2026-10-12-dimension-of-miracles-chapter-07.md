<h2>第七章</h2>
<p>火山狂怒地咆哮、冒烟，喷出一股股烈焰，把耀眼的火球抛进漆黑的天空。接着它炸成一百万块炽亮的碎片；每一块碎片又继续分裂，再分裂，直到整个天穹辉煌如昼，连那三颗小太阳都显得苍白失色。</p>
<p>“嚯！”卡莫迪说。那景象活像复活节时墨西哥查普尔特佩克公园举行的烟火表演，卡莫迪确实被震住了。</p>
<p>就在他观看时，那些发光碎片落向地面，被一片专门为了接住它们而形成的海洋熄灭。五颜六色的烟带彼此缠绕、扭动；深水嘶嘶作响，化为蒸汽。蒸汽升起，形成形状奇异、仿佛精心雕刻的云层，随后又溶成雨。</p>
<p>“好家伙！”卡莫迪叫道。</p>
<p>雨斜斜落下，一阵风随之而起。风把下落的水聚拢起来，编织在一起，直到风与雨彼此交融，形成一个巨大的龙卷风。龙卷风躯干粗壮，通体漆黑，泛着银色反光，在震耳欲聋而富有节奏的雷鸣伴奏下，朝卡莫迪步步逼近。</p>
<p>“够了吧！”卡莫迪尖叫。</p>
<p>龙卷风几乎走到他脚边时，突然溶解。风和雨一起冲向天空，雷声也减弱为不祥的低沉轰鸣。接着响起号角和古琴般的弦乐声，还有风笛的哀号与竖琴甜美的低吟。乐器声越来越高昂，奏出一曲庆典与欢迎之歌，很像米高梅用 CinemaScope 宽银幕和 Todd-AO 拍摄的那种真正不惜血本的历史大片配乐——只不过还要更好。最后，声音、光、色彩、运动以及若干其他事物一同爆发到顶点，然后骤然沉寂。</p>
<p>卡莫迪在最后一刻闭上了眼睛。此刻他重新睁眼，正好看见声音、光、色彩、运动以及若干其他事物，凝聚成一个英雄般赤裸的男人形体。</p>
<p>“你好，”那男人说。“我是梅利克隆。你觉得我的登场怎么样？”</p>
<p>“我彻底被震撼了。”卡莫迪真心实意地说。</p>
<p>“真的？”梅利克隆问。“我是说，真的‘震撼’？不只是‘印象深刻’而已？说实话，别顾虑我的感受。”</p>
<p>“真的，”卡莫迪说，“我真的是彻底被震撼了。”</p>
<p>“哦，那可真让人高兴，”梅利克隆说。“你刚才看见的，是我最近才编排好的一小段《自我介绍》。我觉得——我是说，我真的觉得——它确实表达了某些关于我的东西，你不觉得吗？”</p>
<p>“当然表达了！”卡莫迪说。他努力想看清梅利克隆究竟长什么样；可眼前那个英雄式的人形通体乌黑，比例完美，却没有任何五官或特征。唯一可以辨认的特点是声音：文雅、焦虑，而且稍微有点哼哼唧唧。</p>
<p>“当然，这一切都挺荒唐，”梅利克隆说。“我是说，给自己搞这么一大套登场仪式之类的。不过话又说回来，这里毕竟是——我的行星。一个人在自己的行星上都不能稍微炫耀一下，那还能上哪儿炫耀？是不是？”</p>
<p>“这没法反驳。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“你真的这么想？”梅利克隆问。</p>
<p>“我确确实实、真心实意就是这个意思。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>梅利克隆沉思了一阵，忽然说道：“谢谢。我喜欢你。你是个聪明、敏感的生物，而且敢于直说自己的想法。”</p>
<p>“谢谢。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“不，我是真心的。”</p>
<p>“那我也真心谢谢你。”卡莫迪说，努力不让声音里露出那一点点绝望。</p>
<p>“而且我很高兴你来了，”梅利克隆说。“你知道吗，我是个直觉很强的生物——这一点我很自豪——而我觉得你能帮我。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪差点脱口而出：自己是来求助，不是来帮人的；更何况他连“找到回家的路”这么基础的事情都帮不了自己，实在没资格帮助任何人。不过为了避免冒犯梅利克隆，他决定暂时什么也不说。</p>
<p>“我的问题，”梅利克隆说，“内在于我的处境。而我的处境是独一无二、令人敬畏、奇异而富有意义的。你也许已经听说，这整颗行星都是我的；但事情远不止如此。我是唯一能在这里生存的生命。别的生命试过，有人建立过殖民地，有人放养动物、种植植物。当然，这一切都经过我的批准——也全都失败了。无一例外，所有不属于这颗行星的物质，最终都会化成一层细灰，再由我的风把它们吹进深空。你怎么看？”</p>
<p>“很奇怪。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“对，说得好！”梅利克隆说。“确实奇怪！可事实就是这样。除了我以及我的延伸体，没有任何生命能在这里维持。当我意识到这一点时，可真把我吓了一跳。”</p>
<p>“我想也是。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“从我自己以及任何人所能记得的时候起，我就一直在这里，”梅利克隆说。“漫长的年代里，我满足于简单地生活：做阿米巴，做地衣，做蕨类。那时候一切都很好，也都很直接。我生活在某种伊甸园里。”</p>
<p>“那一定很美妙。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“我很喜欢。”梅利克隆轻声说。“不过当然，它不可能永远持续。我发现了进化，于是让自己进化，同时改变整颗行星，以适应我新的各种人格。我变成过许多生物，其中有些并不讨喜。我开始注意自己世界之外的其他世界，并试验在那里观察到的形态。我曾以银河里各种高等生命形态的身份度过漫长一生——类人型、克塞里佐德型、奥利科德型，还有别的许多种。我逐渐意识到自己的独特性，而这种认识给我带来了一种我无法接受的孤独。所以我没有接受它。相反，我进入了一段持续数百万年的躁狂期。我把自己变成一个又一个完整种族，并且允许——不，是鼓励——我的各个种族互相战争。我差不多同时发现了性与艺术。我把两者都介绍给自己的种族，于是有一阵子过得相当愉快。我把自己分成男性和女性成分，每一个成分都是独立单位，同时又仍然是我的一部分；然后我繁殖，沉溺于各种变态行为，把自己绑在火刑柱上烧死，伏击自己，和自己签和平条约，和自己结婚又离婚，经历了无数次微型的自我死亡与自体出生。我的各个成分还从事艺术——其中有些确实很漂亮——也从事宗教。当然，他们崇拜我。这很合适，因为对他们而言，我就是万物的动力因。不过我甚至允许他们设想并赞美一些并非我的更高存在。因为在那些日子里，我是极端自由主义的。”</p>
<p>“你考虑得真周到。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“嗯，我一向努力周到，”梅利克隆说。“而且我有资格周到。就这颗行星而言，我就是上帝。没必要拐弯抹角：我超凡、永生、全能、全知。万物都寓居于我之中——甚至包括对我的异议。没有哪一片草叶不是我存在中微不足道的一小部分。连山岳与河流都由我塑造。我带来丰收，也带来饥荒；我是精子细胞中的生命，也是鼠疫杆菌里的死亡。没有一只麻雀能够在我不知道的情况下坠落，因为我是结合者与解缚者，是一切与万殊，是那永恒曾在者，也是那永恒将存者。”</p>
<p>“这可真不得了。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“是啊，是啊，”梅利克隆有点不好意思地笑道，“就像我的一个诗人说的，我是‘天堂自行车厂里的那个大轮子’。一切都辉煌极了。我的种族画画；我制造日落。我的人民写爱情；我发明爱情。啊，多么美好的日子！要是它能一直继续下去就好了！”</p>
<p>“为什么没有？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“因为我长大了。”梅利克隆悲伤地说。“无数亿万年来，我一直沉醉于创造；后来我开始质疑自己的创造物，也质疑自己。你看，我的祭司总在追问关于我的问题，还互相争论我的本性和属性。我像个傻瓜一样听他们争。听自己的祭司讨论自己，总是很让人受用；但也可能很危险。于是我开始琢磨自己的本性和属性。我沉思，我内省。我越想，事情就显得越困难。”</p>
<p>“可你为什么非得质疑自己？”卡莫迪问。“毕竟你就是上帝。”</p>
<p>“这正是问题的核心，”梅利克隆说。“从我的造物角度看，一点问题也没有。我是上帝，我以神秘莫测的方式行事；而我的职能，就是养育并惩戒一个既拥有自由意志、又仍然属于我本质的种族。在他们看来，我做什么大体都是对的，因为做那些事的是‘我’。换句话说，我的一切行为到最后都不可解释，连最简单、最显而易见的行为也是如此，因为‘我本身’就不可解释。或者换一种说法，我的行为是对总体实在所作的谜语式解释；而那总体实在，只有我凭借神性才能感知。我的几位杰出思想家就是这样表述的；他们还补充说，等他们进了天堂，就会蒙赐更完整的理解。”</p>
<p>“你还创造了天堂？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“当然。还有地狱。”梅利克隆笑了。“你真该看看我把他们复活到其中一个地方时，他们脸上的表情！连最虔诚的那些人，其实都从没真正相信有来世！”</p>
<p>“我想那一定很令人满足。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“一开始是挺不错，”梅利克隆说。“可过了一阵，我厌烦了。我当然和任何别的上帝一样虚荣；但那种永无止境、肉麻透顶的赞美，最后把我烦得要命。一个上帝如果只是在履行自己的神职，凭什么还要受赞美？那跟称赞一只蚂蚁盲目履行蚂蚁职责有什么区别？这种局面让我觉得很不满意。而且我依旧缺乏真正的自我认识；我只能透过自己造物那双有偏见的眼睛看自己。”</p>
<p>“那你后来怎么办？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“我把他们废除了。”梅利克隆说。“我清除了这颗行星上的所有生命——活的，以及其他形式的——同时也删除了来世。坦白说，我需要一点时间思考。”</p>
<p>“呃。”卡莫迪震惊地说。</p>
<p>“不过从另一个意义上说，我也没有毁掉任何东西或任何人，”梅利克隆赶紧补充。“我只是把自己的碎片重新收回自己里面。”梅利克隆忽然咧嘴一笑。“以前我手下有不少眼神狂热的家伙，整天嚷着要和我合而为一。现在他们可算真的实现了！”</p>
<p>“也许他们就喜欢这样。”卡莫迪建议。</p>
<p>“他们怎么知道？”梅利克隆说。“与我合一，就意味着成为我；那必然会失去那个能够检视‘自己是否与我合一’的意识。它和死亡完全一样，只不过说起来好听得多。”</p>
<p>“很有意思，”卡莫迪说。“不过我记得，你似乎想和我谈一个问题？”</p>
<p>“对，正是！我刚要说到那里。你看，我把自己的人民收起来，就像一个孩子把玩偶屋收起来。然后我坐下来——打个比方——把事情仔细想一想。当然，唯一值得思考的东西就是‘我’。而关于‘我’的真正问题是：我究竟该做什么？难道我存在的意义就只是当上帝？上帝这一行我已经试过，觉得限制太多。那是一份适合头脑简单的自大狂的工作。我一定还有别的事可做——某种更有意义、更能表达我真正自我的事情。我确信如此！这就是我的问题，也是我要问你的问题：我究竟该拿自己怎么办？”</p>
<p>“嗯……”卡莫迪说。“嗯，嗯。是，我明白你的问题了。”他清清嗓子，若有所思地揉揉鼻子。“这种问题需要大量思考。”</p>
<p>“时间对我并不重要，”梅利克隆说。“我的时间无穷无尽。不过很遗憾，你没有。”</p>
<p>“我没有？我还有多少时间？”</p>
<p>“按你的计算，大约十分钟。过了那以后不久，你身上很可能会发生一件相当不幸的事。”</p>
<p>“会发生什么？我能怎么办？”</p>
<p>“得了，公平一点，”梅利克隆说。“你先回答我的问题，然后我再回答你的。”</p>
<p>“可要是我只有十分钟——”</p>
<p>“限制能帮助你集中注意力，”梅利克隆说。“再说这是我的行星，所以事情按我的规矩来。我可以向你保证，如果这是你的行星，我也会按你的规矩办。这很合理吧？”</p>
<p>“嗯，我想是吧。”卡莫迪闷闷不乐地说。</p>
<p>“九分钟。”梅利克隆说。</p>
<p>一个人该怎么告诉上帝，他的职能究竟应该是什么？尤其当这个人像卡莫迪一样，是个无神论者？又该怎么找出一句真正有意义的话来说，尤其当你清楚知道，这位上帝的祭司和哲学家们早已在这片土地上耕耘了几个世纪？</p>
<p>“八分钟。”梅利克隆说。</p>
<p>卡莫迪张开嘴，开始说话。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 7</b></p>
<p>The volcano raged and fumed, spat gouts of flame and hurled dazzling fireballs into the black sky. It exploded into a million incandescent fragments, and then each fragment split again, and again, until the skies were lighted with glory and the three little suns had gone pale.</p>
<p>‘Boy!’ Carmody said. It was like a Mexican fireworks display in Chapultepec Park on Easter, and Carmody was sincerely impressed.</p>
<p>Even as he watched, the glowing fragments fell to earth and were extinguished in an ocean that formed to receive them. Multicoloured streamers of smoke twisted and writhed around each other, and the deep waters hissed and turned into steam, which rose in strangely sculptured clouds and then dissolved into rain.</p>
<p>‘Hooee!’ Carmody cried.</p>
<p>The rain fell slantingly, and there arose a wind which collected the descending waters and wove them together until wind and rain had intermingled and formed a vast tornado. Thick-trunked, black with silvery reflections, the tornado advanced upon Carmody to the rhythmic accompaniment of deafening thunderclaps.</p>
<p>‘Enough already!’ Carmody shrieked.</p>
<p>When it had marched almost to his feet, the tornado dissolved, the wind and rain rushed skywards, the thunder diminished to an ominous rumble. A sound of bugles and psalteries could then be heard, and also the wail of bagpipes and the sweet moan of harps. Higher and higher the instruments pealed, a song of celebration and welcome not unlike the musical accompaniment to a really high-budget MGM historical movie in Cinemascope and Todd-AO, but better. And then there was a last burst of sound, light, colour, movement, and various other things, and then there was silence.</p>
<p>Carmody had closed his eyes at the very end. He opened them now, just in time to see the sound, light, colour, movement, and various other things, turn into the heroic naked form of a man.</p>
<p>‘Hello,’ the man said. ‘I’m Melichrone. How did you like my entrance?’</p>
<p>‘I was overwhelmed,’ Carmody said in all sincerity.</p>
<p>‘Were you really?’ Melichrone asked. ‘I mean, <i>really</i> overwhelmed? I mean, more than just <i>impressed</i>? The truth now, and don’t spare my feelings.’</p>
<p>‘Really,’ Carmody said, ‘I was really overwhelmed.’</p>
<p>‘Well, that’s awfully nice,’ Melichrone said. ‘What you saw was a little Introduction to Myself that I worked out quite recently. I think – I really <i>do</i> think – that it says something about me, don’t you?’</p>
<p>‘It sure does!’ Carmody said. He was trying to see what Melichrone looked like; but the heroic figure in front of him was jet black, perfectly proportioned, and featureless. The only distinguishing characteristic was the voice, which was refined, anxious, and a little whiney.</p>
<p>‘It’s all absurd, of course,’ Melichrone said. ‘I mean, having a big introduction for oneself and all. But yet, it <i>is</i> – my planet. And if one can’t show off a bit on one’s own planet, where <i>can</i> one show off? Eh?’</p>
<p>‘There’s no arguing that,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Do you really think so?’ Melichrone asked.</p>
<p>‘I honestly do mean it in all sincerity,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>Melichrone brooded for a while over that, then said abruptly, ‘Thank you. I like you. You are an intelligent, sensitive creature and you are not afraid to say what you mean.’</p>
<p>‘Thank you,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘No, I really mean it.’</p>
<p>‘Well then, I <i>really</i> thank you,’ Carmody said, trying to keep a faint note of desperation out of his voice.</p>
<p>‘And I’m glad you came,’ Melichrone said. ‘Do you know, I am a very intuitive creature (I pride myself on that) and I think that you can help me.’</p>
<p>It was on the tip of Carmody’s tongue to say that he had come to ask help rather than to give it, and that furthermore, he was in no position to help anyone, being unable to assist himself in so fundamental a task as finding his way home. But he decided against saying anything at the moment for fear of offending Melichrone.</p>
<p>‘My problem,’ Melichrone said, ‘is inherent in my situation. And my situation is unique, awesome, strange, and meaningful. You have heard, perhaps, that this entire planet is mine; but it goes much further than that. I am the only living thing which <i>can</i> live here. Others have tried, settlements have been formed, animals have been turned loose and plants have been planted. All with my approval, of course, and all in vain. Without exception, all matter alien to this planet has fallen to a thin dust which my winds eventually blow out to deep space. What do you think of that?’</p>
<p>‘Strange,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Yes, well put!’ Melichrone said. ‘Strange indeed! But there it is. No life is viable here except me and my extensions. It gave me quite a turn when I realized that.’</p>
<p>‘I imagine it did,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I have been here as long as I or anyone else can remember,’ Melichrone said. ‘For ages I was content to live simply, as amoebae, as lichen, as ferns. Everything was fine and straightforward in those days. I lived in a sort of Garden of Eden.’</p>
<p>‘It must have been marvellous,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I liked it,’ Melichrone said quietly. ‘But it couldn’t last, of course. I discovered evolution and evolved myself, altering my planet to accommodate my new personae. I became many creatures, some not nice. I took cognizance of worlds exterior to my own and experimented with the forms I observed there. I lived out long lifetimes as various of the galaxy’s higher forms – humanoid, Chtherizoid, Olichord, and others. I became aware of my singularity, and this knowledge brought me a loneliness which I found unacceptable. So I did not accept it. Instead, I entered a manic phase which lasted for some millions of years. I transformed myself into entire races, and I permitted – no, encouraged – my races to war against each other. I learned about sex and art at almost the same time. I introduced both to my races, and for a while I had a very enjoyable time. I divided myself into masculine and feminine components, each component a discrete unit, though still a part of me; and I procreated, indulged in perversions, burned myself at the stake, ambushed myself, made peace treaties with myself, married and divorced myself, went through countless miniature self-deaths and auto-births. And my components indulged in art, some of it very pretty, and in religion. They worshipped me, of course; this was only proper, since I was the efficient cause of all things for them. But I even permitted them to postulate and to glorify superior beings which were <i>not</i> me. For in those days, I was extremely liberal.’</p>
<p>‘That was very thoughtful of you,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Well, I try to be thoughtful,’ Melichrone said. ‘I could afford to be thoughtful. As far as this planet was concerned, I was God, There’s no sense beating around the bush about it: I was supernal, immortal, omnipotent and omniscient. All things Were resident in me – even dissident opinions about myself. Not a blade of grass grew that was not some infinitesimal portion of my being. The very mountains and rivers were shaped by me. I caused the harvest, and the famine as well; I was the life in the sperm cells, and I was the death in the plague bacillus. Not a sparrow could fall without my knowledge, for I was the Binder and the Unbinder, the All and the Many, That Which Always Was and That Which Always Will Be.’</p>
<p>‘That’s really something,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Yes, yes,’ Melichrone said with a self-conscious smile, ‘I was the Big Wheel in the Heavenly Bicycle Factory, as one of my poets expressed it. It was all very splendid. My races made paintings; I made sunsets. My people wrote about love; I invented love. Ah, wonderful days! If it only could have gone on!’</p>
<p>‘Why didn’t it?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Because I grew up,’ Melichrone said sadly. ‘For untold aeons I had revelled in creation; now I began to question my creations and myself. My priests were always asking about me, you see, and disputing among themselves as to my nature and qualities. Like a fool, I listened to them. It is always flattering to hear one’s priests discuss one; but it can be dangerous. I began to wonder about my own nature and qualities. I brooded, I introspected. The more I thought about it, the more difficult it seemed.’</p>
<p>‘But why did you have to question yourself?’ Carmody asked. ‘After all, you were God.’</p>
<p>‘That was the crux of the problem,’ Melichrone said. ‘From the viewpoint of my creations, there was no problem. I was God, I moved in mysterious ways, but my function was to nurture and chastise a race of beings who would have free will while still being of my essence. As far as they were concerned, what I did was pretty much all right since it was Me that was doing it. That is to say, my actions were in the final analysis inexplicable, even the simplest and most obvious of them, because I Myself was inexplicable. Or, to put it another way, my actions were enigmatic explanations of a total reality which only I, by virtue of my Godhead, could perceive. That is how several of my outstanding thinkers put it; and they added that a more complete understanding would be vouchsafed them in heaven.’</p>
<p>‘Did you also create a heaven?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Certainly. Also a hell.’ Melichrone smiled. ‘You should have seen their faces when I resurrected them to one place or the other! Not even the most devout had <i>really</i> believed in a Hereafter!’</p>
<p>‘I suppose it was very gratifying,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘It was nice for a while,’ Melichrone said. ‘But after a time, it bored me. I am doubtless as vain as the next God; but the endless fulsome praise finally bored me to distraction. Why in God’s name should a God be praised if he is only performing his Godly function? You might as well praise an ant for doing his blind antly duties. This state of affairs struck me as unsatisfactory. And I was still lacking in self-knowledge except through the biased eyes of my creations.’</p>
<p>‘So what did you do?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘I abolished them,’ Melichrone said. ‘I did away with all life on my planet, living and otherwise, and I also deleted the Hereafter. Frankly, I needed time to think.’</p>
<p>‘Huh,’ Carmody said, shocked.</p>
<p>‘In another sense, though, I didn’t destroy anything or anyone,’ Melichrone said hastily. ‘I simply gathered the fragments of myself back into myself.’ Melichrone grinned suddenly. ‘I had quite a number of wild-eyed fellows who were always talking about attaining a oneness with Me. They’ve attained it now, that’s for sure!’</p>
<p>‘Perhaps they like it that way,’ Carmody suggested.</p>
<p>‘How can they know?’ Melichrone said. ‘Oneness with Me means Me; it necessarily involves loss of the consciousness which examines one’s oneness. It is exactly the same as death, though it sounds much nicer.’</p>
<p>‘That’s very interesting,’ Carmody said. ‘But I believe you wished to speak to me about a problem?’</p>
<p>‘Yes, precisely! I was just coming to that. You see, I put away my peoples much as a child puts away a doll’s house. And then I sat down – metaphorically – to think things over. The only thing to think over was Me, of course. And the real problem about Me was, What was I supposed to do? Was I meant to be nothing but God? I had tried the God business and found it too limited. It was a job for a simple-minded egomaniac. There had to be something else for me to do – something more meaningful, more expressive of my true self. I am convinced of it! That is my problem, and that is the question I ask of you: What am I to do with myself?’</p>
<p>‘Well,’ said Carmody. ‘Well, well. Yes, I see your problem.’ He cleared his throat and rubbed his nose thoughtfully. ‘A problem like that requires a great deal of thought.’</p>
<p>‘Time is unimportant to me,’ Melichrone said. ‘I have limitless quantities of it. Though you, I am sorry to say, do not.’</p>
<p>‘I don’t? How much time do I have?’</p>
<p>‘About ten minutes, as you would reckon it. Shortly thereafter, something rather unfortunate is likely to happen to you.’</p>
<p>‘What is going to happen to me? What can I do about it?’</p>
<p>‘Come, now, fair’s fair,’ Melichrone said. ‘First you answer my question and then I’ll answer yours.’</p>
<p>‘But if I have only ten minutes –’</p>
<p>‘The limitation will aid your concentration,’ Melichrone said. ‘And anyhow, since it’s my planet, we do things by my rules. I can assure you, if it were your planet, I would do things by your rules. That’s reasonable, isn’t it?’</p>
<p>‘Yeah, I suppose so,’ Carmody said unhappily.</p>
<p>‘Nine minutes,’ Melichrone said.</p>
<p>How do you tell a God what his function should be? Especially if, like Carmody, you are an atheist? How do you find something meaningful to say, especially when you are aware that the God’s priests and philosophers have spent centuries on this ground?</p>
<p>‘Eight minutes,’ Melichrone said.</p>
<p>Carmody opened his mouth and began to speak.</p>
