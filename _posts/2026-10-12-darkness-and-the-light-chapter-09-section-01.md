<h2>一、村落世界</h2>
<p>此时开启的时代，几乎可以说是一个爆炸式进步的时代——爆炸式，却又受到控制。它不同于本书读者所熟悉的那场工业革命，并不依赖于毫无节制的经济个人主义。当然，它的巨大动力仍在很大程度上来自有才能者渴望自我伸张的冲动；但如今，满足这种冲动的途径大都经过中央规划，而且具有社会用途。</p>
<p>至少从表面上说，我还能理解人类在这一时期取得的物质成就；然而从这时开始，他们的文化生活越来越超出我的理解范围，逐渐从我的视野中逸去。</p>
<p>尽管如此，描述这个新秩序的主要特征仍然值得一试。原因不仅在于它在此后极其漫长的时期内一直是人类社会的典型形态，也在于它本身的新颖性，以及它对我们这个时代所具有的意义。这个时代刚开始时，普通人的先天素质并没有明显高于我们。总体说来，他们并不比我们更聪明，慷慨的能力也不比我们强；然而，由于光明意志已经在全世界取得胜利，一种道德正直的新传统和一种更健康的经济制度已经建立，普通人的实际行为却好得多。他们通常能够生活在更接近自身能力上限的位置。环境不再不断把人拖低，反而不断使人振作，并使人更具人性。新世界的统治者并不满足于此。整个社会组织都受一种目标支配：不断把人类平均能力提升到远远超过原有水平的高度。</p>
<p>新世界的社会秩序同此前任何一种社会形态都很不同。它可以说既“超级现代”，又在某种意义上带有中世纪色彩。归根结底，它依赖于那种新机械能源的特殊性质。两种彼此相反却又相互协调的趋势同时发挥作用。一方面，机械化不断推进；另一方面，普通人的生活中却令人意外地恢复了手工技能和多才多艺。一方面，社会的统一与和谐终于实现；另一方面，个人的自给能力和全面性又得到发展。</p>
<p>这种均衡经济之所以能够建立，很大程度上得益于能源几乎可以在任何地方取得，而且可以从非常普通的材料中获得。毫无疑问，在我们这个时代，要建立这样的秩序困难得多；因为在我们的工业发展阶段，能源生产与制造业都要求庞大而深入的组织，同时把个人压缩成巨大机器里的专业化齿轮。但即使是我们，只要目光足够清醒，又真正具有求变的意志，至少也可以朝这个方向前进。</p>
<p>最初的发电装置极其笨重、娇贵；后来，世界范围内的合作研究带来一系列辉煌发明，使整个方法发生彻底变化。支撑这种新文明的标准发生器，就像各种内燃机支撑我们的文明一样，是一种精巧的小型机器，一间小谷仓便足以容纳。制造这种仪器需要最顶尖物理学家的全部本领；但成品虽然还不能说完全“傻瓜也用不坏”，却十分可靠、有力而且用途广泛。它不仅能够生产动力，还能完成元素嬗变，并合成种类极多的实用材料。作为动力装置，操作它所需的技能并不比我们驾驶汽车多多少；但若把它用来合成各种材料，从普通人到天才大师，各层次能力都有用武之地。有些元素与化合物，任何称职者都能轻易制造；有些需要特殊天赋和训练；有些只有最卓越的大师才敢尝试；还有一些则必须在大型电化学工厂中完成。</p>
<p>渐渐地，每个村庄都有了自己的动力站。即使孤立的住宅，也可以自行发电并制造较简单的材料。不过从总体上说，村庄才是新社会制度的基本单位。它之所以具有这样的地位，是因为标准发生器本身的能力范围和局限性：一台设备通过村庄工业和农业直接或间接养活、雇用的人数，大致在五十到五百之间。一个普通村庄的居民包括负责发电的电磁工程师，一批专门制造村庄所需不同材料的工匠，以及另一批把这些材料加工成各种用品的工匠。前一类人被称为“原子织工”。他们从当地土壤中提取各种成分作为原料，再用强大动力站射出的亚原子粒子进行轰击，由此制造出种类繁多的元素和化合物。这项工艺所要求的手感，与旧时代的手工纺纱和织布颇为相似；工匠们彼此竞赛，看谁能制造出更精细、更实用而且不含杂质的化合物与混合物。随后，另一类工匠再把这些材料制成餐具、家具、刀具、建筑材料、衣物等等。村里的纺织工为邻人制作种类丰富、朴素却悦目的织物。甚至独居的一家人，只凭较小型的设备，也能自行制取许多简单材料。另一方面，有些村庄在某一种工艺上精湛到远近闻名，产品为整个地区所追求。只有最难制造的材料和器物，才需要从地方工厂运来；而所谓地方工厂，本身也不过是一个规模较大、高度专业化的村庄，或者围绕大型动力站与合成站形成的一组村庄。</p>
<p>村庄的食物并不是利用亚原子能直接合成有机化合物得到的。农业仍然存在。不过旧式农业很快让位于另一种方法：在阳光下直接进行光合作用，制造食物所需的基本成分。村庄周围分布着私人菜园和公共农田。人们先让土壤浸透适当的化学物质，再撒下一种人工“有机分子”的孢子；它会吸收光能并自我繁殖，直到整片田地覆盖一层绿色生长物。随后，一台装有类似真空吸尘器装置的拖拉机会把它收集起来，洗净，再与用类似方法生产的其他材料加工成形形色色的食品。整个夏季，田野大约每隔一周就能收获一次。与旧式农业相比，这套系统最大的优点是：同样一块土地所能提供的食物营养价值，几乎达到过去的十倍。</p>
<p>某些奢侈食品仍必须从地方或国家工厂取得——而每个村民都要求自己也应享有一份奢侈——其中某些特别珍贵的品种甚至要从外国进口。不过，只要一个村庄还稍微讲究口味、又有一点地方自豪感，它总会做出具有本地特色的合成“肉类”“面包”“奶酪”“水果”和饮料。许多孤立农庄只要由聪明而富有艺术感的人掌管食品制作，也能拿出一顿简单却精致的饭菜，使最挑剔的旅行者满意。</p>
<p>这些新工艺一点一点改变了整个世界的经济。一种微型飞机利用空气中某种稀有元素提供的亚原子动力，使任何人都能够以我们看来已经绰绰有余的速度前往任何地方。若要进行特别漫长而快速的旅行，人们仍会乘坐大型客机或平流层客机；但富于冒险精神的青年——青年女性也一样——常常驾驶自己的微型飞机前往最遥远的国家。这些小型飞行器通常被称为“飞蝇”（fly），体积比我们最小的滑翔机还要小一些。驾驶者全身俯卧在棺材般的机身中，里面铺有软垫，像一张狭窄的床。</p>
<p>我们所熟悉的那种城市正在消失。人们已经没有必要再住进巨大的蜂窝式人群巢穴，而且普遍要求更宽敞的生活空间。由于“飞蝇”这种宝贵交通工具的存在，空间分散与频繁社会交往再也不矛盾。许多旧城市被拆除，或者大幅降低建筑密度，使那些数量虽少却珍贵的建筑遗产能够更好地显现出来。贫民窟早已改造成公园或农地，其间零星分布一些村庄。旧城市中，只有大型港口仍保持原来的功能，但港口本身也被改造。除了纽约这类土地特别受限制的地方，原有拥挤城区都逐渐变成许多村庄，彼此由公园、菜园、果园和农田分隔。地方自给程度大幅提高，本来似乎会消灭海运贸易；港口最初确实衰退了，但很快又出现新的趋势。亚原子能源释放出极其庞大的人类精力与技巧，于是许多民族重新开始专业化生产——不再是生产基本生活必需品，而是制作奢侈食品、精美手工艺品以及超精密机器和工具。各民族之间出现一种新的、极其激烈的竞争，都力图在某一种产品上做到全世界最好，例如光学仪器、纺织品、家具等等。这不是资本主义式竞争；它的动力纯粹来自工艺自豪感和一种开明的爱国主义。正因为出现这种新的工业专业化，海运和空运贸易，以及沿各大陆主要干道进行的货物运输，依然是重要社会服务。这个富裕新世界里的每一个村庄都要求：除了在基本用品上自给自足、为本地工艺感到骄傲之外，还应能够分享到各民族用剩余精力创造出来的最优质产品。</p>
<p>在新秩序中，一个普通人无论生活在哪个国家，大致不是掌握某种专业亚原子技术的村庄工匠，就是一种“高级版”的自给农民。他在自己那一英亩土地上，或在村庄公田里，生产足够家庭食用的粮食，或者参加村庄的共同生产。产量还会留出足够部分用于纳税、物物交换、对外贸易以及慷慨招待客人。除非他专门生产某种困难的奢侈品，否则新式农业并不会占满他的全部时间；因此，他往往也能熟练操作亚原子设备，自己制作不少家庭用品。他的妻子——也许有女儿协助——负责准备食物并维持家务。在新动力与各种省力装置帮助下，这些工作每天不过占两小时左右。女人因此也会参加农活，而且通常还会花不少时间制作全家的衣服。孩子同样会在农场帮忙，主要目的是接受教育，并学习将来要用的各种工艺。村庄农业者和村庄工匠之间的区别只是侧重点不同。两类人都同时从事农业和手艺：农业者以耕作为主、辅以简单手工艺；工匠则在空闲时间种田、园艺。</p>
<p>就像我们所称的中世纪一样，绝大多数人都多少参与农业，不过仍有少数人完全专业化，在工厂、实验室等地方工作。有些地区的专业化程度高于另一些地区。各国仍保留自己相当多的生活模式，但传统习俗都被重新塑造，使之符合新世界的一般结构和精神。有些国家的普通村庄既住着工匠，也住着当地农业者，后者每天乘“飞蝇”去公共或私人农田；另一些地方的村庄主要由工匠组成，农业人口则散居在乡野各处的农庄。在某些国家，专业人员很少；另一些国家则很多。有些地方的农业主要由个人经营，只是受国家或村庄严格监管；另一些地方则由村庄共同体集体经营。人口稀少的地区，成年儿子会到尚未开发的土地上建立新农场；人口稠密的地区，兄弟们可能商量由谁继承父亲的农场，也可能所有人都与妻儿继续住在旧宅，通过销售手工艺品补充农场收入。有时，一户农庄会扩展成宗族村。有时，住宅几乎只承担宿舍功能，全部社会生活都集中在村庄公共空间。有时，村庄本身在精神上又明显受到附近某个城镇或大都会支配。然而即使世界上最大的城市，如今也只是村庄的有机群落；每个村庄都以自己的方式为整座城市的生命作出贡献。</p>

<h2>i. A WORLD OF VILLAGES</h2>
<p>THE AGE THAT now dawned was one of almost explosive progress, explosive, yet controlled. Unlike the industrial revolution which is familiar to readers of this book, it was not dependent on licentious economic individualism. Its energy was derived, of course, very largely from the self-assertive itch of able individuals, but the means of satisfying this craving were now in the main centrally planned and socially useful.</p>
<p>Superficially at least I was able to grasp the material achievement of the race in this period, but its cultural life henceforth increasingly escaped me, outranging my comprehension.</p>
<p>Nevertheless it seems worth while to describe the main features of the new order, not only because it was characteristic of the human race for a very long time but also because of its novelty and its significance for our own age. At the outset the innate calibre of the average human being was not appreciably higher than our own. Men were on the whole no more intelligent, and had no more capacity for generosity than we have; but, owing to the world-wide victory of the will for the light and the founding of a new tradition of moral integrity and a more wholesome economy, average individuals behaved far better. They lived normally far nearer the upper limit of their capacity. Instead of being constantly degraded by their environment, they were constantly braced and humanized. The rulers of the new world were not content with this. The whole social organization was dominated by the aim of continuously raising the average human capacity far beyond its present level.</p>
<p>The social order of the new world was very different from any earlier form. It might be described as at once ‘super-modern’ and yet in a way medieval. At bottom it depended on the special characters of the new source of mechanical power. Two contrary but harmonized tendencies were at work. On the one hand mechanization was being steadily pushed forward; on the other there was a surprising recovery of manual skill and versatility in the life of the ordinary human being. On the one hand came the fulfilment of social unity and harmony, on the other the development of the individual’s self-sufficiency and all-roundness.</p>
<p>This balanced economy was greatly assisted by the fact that power came to be accessible almost anywhere and was derived from quite ordinary materials. In our own age, no doubt, such an order would be far more difficult to establish, since in our stage of industrial evolution, power and manufacture both demand far-reaching organization, and the reducing of individuals to specialized cogs in the great machine. But even we, had we clear sight and the will for change, could at least set our faces in this direction.</p>
<p>Though at first the generators had been exceedingly cumbersome and delicate, the method was later transformed by a series of brilliant inventions, resulting from world-wide co-operative research. The standard generator, which supported the new civilization as combustion engines of all sorts support our own, was a subtle little machine which could be housed in a small barn. All the skill of the most expert physicists was needed for the making of this instrument; but the finished article, if hot fool-proof, was reliable, potent, and versatile. It could be used not only for the production of power but also for the transmutation of the elements, and the synthesizing of a vast range of materials for use. As a power-unit it demanded little more skill than we use in motoring; but as an instrument for the varied synthesizing of materials it could employ every range of ability. Some elements and compounds could be produced easily by any competent person, some demanded rather special aptitude and training, some could be attempted only by the most brilliant masters, and some had to be undertaken in the great electro-chemical factories.</p>
<p>Little by little every village came to have its own power plant. Even isolated houses generated their own power and could produce the simpler materials. In the main, however, the village was the unit of the new social system. Its strength was due to the scope and limitations of the standard generator, which employed directly and indirectly in village industry and agriculture between fifty and five hundred persons. The population of the average village consisted of the electro-magnetic engineers who saw to the generating of power, a number of craftsmen specialized in the production of the different kinds of material needed by the village, and another set of craftsmen who worked up the materials into articles of use. The former class of craftsmen, who were called ‘atomic weavers’, used as their raw material ingredients in the local earth. These they bombarded with sub-atomic particles, fired out by their mighty power plant, and thus they produced a great range of elements and compounds. The process demanded the same kind of skill as that of the old-time hand-spinners and weavers, the craftsmen vying with each other to produce the subtlest and most serviceable compounds and mixtures free from all impurities. These products were then worked up by craftsmen of the other class into crockery, furniture, cutting tools, building materials, clothing, and so on. The village textile workers clothed their fellow villagers in a great variety of simple but pleasing fabrics. Even isolated households, with their smaller plant, could provide themselves with many of the simpler materials. On the other hand some villages excelled so much in a particular line of craftsmanship that their products were in demand throughout the countryside. Only the most difficult materials and articles had to be brought to the village from the local factory, itself but a large and highly specialized village or cluster of villages around a great power house and synthesis station.</p>
<p>The food of the village was not produced by the synthesis of organic compounds under sub-atomic power. Agriculture was still practised. But the old kind of agriculture was rapidly giving way to direct photo- synthesis of the essential food factors under sunlight. The village was surrounded by its private gardens and communal fields. The earth was impregnated with appropriate chemicals and sprinkled with the spore of an artificial ‘organic molecule’, which absorbed light and propagated itself till it covered the field with a green exfoliation. It was then gathered by a tractor armed with a sort of vacuum cleaner, washed, and worked up with other materials (similarly produced) into a great variety of food-stuffs. Throughout the summer the fields were harvested at intervals of about a week. The advantage of this system over the old-fashioned agriculture was that the land produced nearly ten times its former yield in food value.</p>
<p>Certain luxury foods, and every villager demanded his share of luxury, had to be procured from the local or national factory, and some specially choice articles from foreign lands. But any village with any pretension to taste and local pride could produce characteristic local variants of the essential synthetic ‘meats’, ‘breads’, ‘cheeses’, ‘fruits’, and drinks. Many an isolated homestead, if its food-making was managed with intelligence and artistry, could produce a simple but elegant meal to delight the most fastidious traveller.</p>
<p>Little by little the new processes transformed the whole economy of the world. A miniature aeroplane, driven by sub-atomic power derived from one of the rarer elements in the air, made it possible for everyone to travel anywhere at a speed which we should regard as more than adequate. For very long fast journeys people had to resort to air-liners and stratosphere-liners; but enterprising young men, and young women also, often went to the farthest countries in their own miniature planes. These little vehicles, commonly called ‘flies’, were rather smaller than our smallest gliders. The flyer lay full length on his stomach in the coffin-like fuselage, which was padded to form a sort of bed.</p>
<p>Towns such as we know were disappearing. It was no longer necessary for people to live in great warrens, and I there was a general demand for spaciousness. Owing to the invaluable fly, this was no longer incompatible with constant social intercourse. Many of the old towns were being demolished or thinned out so as to display to better advantage their few but valued architectural treasures. Slums had long since been turned into parks or agricultural land, with here and there a village. Of the old towns, the great ports alone fulfilled their old function, but these too were transformed. Save where ground space was restricted, as in New York, the congested area gave place to a host of villages separated by parks, market gardens, orchards, and fields. The great increase of local self-sufficiency might have been expected to kill sea-borne trade, but though at first the ports declined, a new tendency soon appeared. Sub-atomic power had released so great a fund of human energy and skill that many of the peoples began to specialize once more, not indeed in the production of basic necessities, but in luxury foodstuffs, luxury handicrafts, superfine machines and tools. A new and fierce competition arose between peoples that vied with each other to produce the very best articles of some particular type, such as optical instruments, textiles, furniture, and so on. This competition was not of the capitalist sort. Its motive was sheer pride of workmanship and enlightened patriotism. In consequence of all this I new industrial specialization, sea-borne and air-borne trade, and the transport of goods along the great arterial roads of the continents, were still important social services. Every village in this new and prosperous world demanded that, in addition to its self- sufficiency in essentials and its pride in local craftsmanship, it should have a share in the choicest products of the excess energy of all peoples.</p>
<p>The average individual in the new order, in whatever land he lived, was either a village craftsman in one of the specialized sub-atomic skills or a sort of glorified subsistence farmer. On his personal acre or in the communal village fields he produced enough food for his family or co-operated in the communal production of the village. Enough was left over for taxes, bartering, trade with foreign lands, and lavish hospitality. As he would not be fully occupied by the new agriculture, unless he specialized in some difficult luxury product, he might also be enough of a craftsman with the sub-atomic machinery to make many of his household goods. His wife, possibly aided by the daughters, would prepare the food and keep the house in order. With the new power and the new labour-saving devices this would occupy no more than a couple of hours a day. The women would therefore lend a hand on the farm and probably spend a good deal of time on the production of clothes for the household. The children also would help on the farm, chiefly for their education. They would learn crafts for future use. The difference between the village agriculturalists and the village craftsmen was only one of emphasis. Both classes practised both activities, but while the agriculturalists supplemented their main occupation with simple crafts, the craftsmen were tillers and gardeners in their spare time.</p>
<p>As in the period that we call the Middle Ages, the great majority of men were agriculturalists to some extent; though minorities specialized completely, working in the factories, laboratories, and so on. In some districts specialism was more common than elsewhere. The different countries retained much of their characteristic pattern of life, but native customs were transmuted to accord with the general pattern and spirit of the new world. In some lands the ordinary village included, along with the houses of the village craftsmen, those of the local agriculturalists, who went to the communal or private fields each day by fly. Elsewhere the villages were populated mainly by craftsmen. The agriculturalists lived in scattered farm- houses throughout the countryside. In some countries there were few specialists, in others many. In some, agriculture was mainly individualistic, though subject to strict control by the state or the village; in others it was carried on by communal village enterprise. In some, where population was sparse, the grown sons would set up new farms in the untamed land. In others, densely populated, the sons might either decide among themselves who was to take over the paternal farm, or all might stay on in the old home with their wives and families, supplementing its produce by trade in handicrafts. Sometimes the individual homestead expanded into a clan village. Sometimes a dwelling-house would be little more than a dormitory, all social activity being centred upon the village. Sometimes the villages them- selves tended to be mentally dominated by some neighbouring town or metropolis. But even the greatest cities of the’ world were now organic clusters of villages, each making its own special contribution to the city’s life.</p>
