<h2>一、日本革命</h2>
<p>对西藏的战争，使俄罗斯与中国的统治阶级得以方便地对各自人民施加极其严格的纪律。战争一结束，这种纪律原本赖以成立的借口便消失了。从战争转入和平，必然给许多人带来困苦。这种转型并非像个人主义制度下那样完全听任偶然，而是由最高资本家——国家——加以控制；但控制的目的不是提高普遍繁荣，而是强化统治阶级的地位。更糟的是，这种控制本身也十分笨拙。熟练工人被安排去做自己既不擅长、性情也无法忍受的非熟练劳动；整片人口因为和平时期的经济要求失去生计，却被任由饿死。与此同时，另一些地区的人口则在恶劣条件下被毫无节制地压榨劳动。</p>
<p>受害最严重的民族之一就是日本人。在东方工业化较早的阶段，这个拥挤在群岛上的民族曾扮演过一个活跃却不幸的角色。旧封建统治阶级明智地拒绝让欧洲金融资本来掠夺本国，于是亲自承担起日本西化的任务。不幸的是，日本人在模仿欧洲商业主义最恶劣的特征方面远比吸收欧洲文明最优秀的精神成功得多。冷酷的工业主义与同样冷酷的帝国主义，最终把他们推入那场漫长而灾难性的侵华战争。最后的失败带来市场丧失、失业与持续不断的社会动荡。此后，东方的经济主人不再是日本，而是中国。日本狂热积累起来的机器逐渐闲置，而作为这些机器人类附属品的劳动者则挨饿。拥挤的人口根本不可能仅靠本土粮食维持生存。原本就不高的生活水平下降到饥荒线。共产党人一次又一次被消灭，却又一次又一次重新出现，而且力量越来越大。与此同时，军方与金融寡头想不出任何更高明的办法，只能仿效法国臭名昭著的“二百家族”——正如从前他们仿效英国那些开创工业时代的家族一样。他们发动反布尔什维克十字军，同中华帝国接触，最终甚至交出了日本的独立。和更早的维希政权人物一样，日本统治者希望这样至少能替自己保留几块权力的残羹。当然，这种希望并未实现。唯一结果是中国警察接管全国，并对一切制造麻烦的人“杀一儆百”，无论这些人来自左派还是右派。饥荒、酷刑与深刻幻灭共同作用，使日本列岛人口大幅减少；与此同时，大批中国官员迁入日本，重新组织其整个经济，把这个国家改造成一个服务于中华帝国对西藏战争的奴隶国家。</p>
<p>西藏陷落、战时经济结束以后，日本人同世界其他民族一样，热切等待此前许诺的生活改善和纪律放松；但同世界其他地方一样，他们很快便失望了。没过多久，日本的绝望已经发展到自杀成为最常见死亡方式的程度。人民似乎被彻底驯服，于是中国占领军甚至缩减到只剩一副骨架。就在这个时刻，日本的光明意志以一种笨拙的方式重新抬头。日本人再一次模仿西方，而且仍旧带着他们惯有的彻底与不理解。共产党领导者巧妙利用俄罗斯的金钱，成功说服东京等地大批民众：与其温顺地自杀，不如为革命而死。他们还宣称，革命并非注定失败。西藏之所以覆灭，是因为受到了来自僧侣寡头的感伤资产阶级观念污染。这个错误绝不能再犯。日本革命的基础必须严格建立在唯物主义之上，它的情感动力必须来自对压迫者的仇恨，而不是任何形而上学幻想。</p>
<p>革命者完全不顾自己的生命，成千上万地向统治者的机枪阵地推进。在中国能够送来有效援助以前，旧政权已经被击溃，人民政府掌握了国家。当时中国统治者正忙于应付俄罗斯造成的危险，因此没有派远征军进攻日本，而只实施极其严厉的封锁。新日本政府随即开始屠杀一切被怀疑同旧政权有牵连的人，也屠杀一切不服从命令的人。粮食是压倒一切的问题。死的人越多，幸存者活下去的希望就越大。因此，哪怕最轻微的违法行为也会被判死刑；只要罪名稍微显得可信，死刑便立即执行。一切可能的办法都被用来刺激农业生产。农民在死亡威胁下被迫耕种大片贫瘠土地，而由于封锁，这些土地甚至缺少肥料。政府承诺说，来年虽然不可避免会发生饥荒，但再下一季必定会迎来丰收。宣传告诉人民：忠于日本乃至整个人类的未来，就要求这一代人作出最大牺牲。然而，新开垦土地只收获了可怜的粮食；人民已经被饥荒与疾病削弱，又长期遭受粗暴对待，而且完全没有西藏那种宗教信念所提供的精神支撑，于是逐渐失去行动能力，身体也虚弱到无法承担繁重农活。政权束手无策。形势越绝望，它就越多地杀人、越多地施加酷刑。新统治者十分清楚，任何纪律上的松动都会立刻导致自己灭亡；而且其中多数人仍真诚相信，他们自己的存续是国家生存的必要条件。最后，中国政府选择最有利的时机，平静地重新占领了日本列岛。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" class="stapledon-body" lang="en" hidden>
<h2>i. THE JAPANESE REVOLUTION</h2>
<p>THE WAR AGAINST Tibet had enabled the ruling classes of Russia and China to impose a conveniently strict discipline upon their respective peoples. When the war was over, the excuse for this discipline vanished. Inevitably the change from war to peace brought hardship to many. The transition was not simply haphazard, as it would have been under individualism; it was controlled by the supreme capitalist, the state. And it was controlled in such a way as to strengthen the ruling class, not to increase general prosperity. Further, it was clumsily controlled. Skilled workers were put to unskilled work for which they had neither the ability nor the temper. Whole populations, deprived of their livelihood by the exigencies of peace, were left to starve. Other populations, meanwhile, were over-worked mercilessly, and in bad conditions.</p>
<p>Among the worst sufferers were the Japanese. In an earlier phase of the industrialization of the East this swarming island people had played a vigorous but unhappy part. The old feudal ruling class, wisely refusing to allow European finance to exploit the country, had itself undertaken the westernization of Japan. Unfortunately the Japanese were far more successful in imitating the worst features of European commercialism than in absorbing the best spirit of European civilization. Ruthless industrialism and ruthless imperialism landed them in the long and disastrous attack on China. Their ultimate defeat brought loss of markets, unemployment, and constant social turmoil. Henceforth China, not Japan, was the economic master of the East. Japan’s feverishly accumulated machinery fell out of use, and its human adjuncts were starved. The crowded population could not possibly be kept alive on home-grown food. The standard of living, never high, sank to famine level. The communists, though repeatedly exterminated, repeatedly reappeared, and with increasing strength. Meanwhile the military and financial oligarchy could think of nothing better to do than copy the notorious ‘two hundred families’ of France, as it had formerly copied the pioneering industrial families of Britain. It preached an anti-bolshevik crusade, made overtures to the Chinese Empire, and finally surrendered Japan’s independence. Like the men of Vichy before them, the Japanese rulers hoped that at least a few crumbs of power would thus be secured to them. This, of course, did not happen. The only result was that the Chinese police took charge of the country, and ‘made an example of’ all those who caused trouble, whether on the left or the right. Through the combination of famine, torture, and profound disillusionment the population of the Japanese islands was greatly reduced, while immense numbers of Chinese officials were settled in the country to reorganize the whole economy of Japan as a slave state for the benefit of the Chinese Empire in its crusade against Tibet.</p>
<p>After the fall of Tibet and the end of war-time economy, the Japanese, like the rest of the world, eagerly awaited the promised improvement of conditions and relaxation of discipline. But like the rest of the world they were disappointed. Very soon desperation in Japan reached the pitch at which suicide becomes the commonest form of death. The population seemed to be so completely cowed that the Chinese army of occupation was reduced to a skeleton. At this point the will for the light in Japan blunderingly reasserted itself. Once more the Japanese copied the West, with their accustomed thoroughness and lack of understanding. The Communist leaders, skilfully using Russian gold, succeeded in persuading large numbers in Tokio and elsewhere that it was better to die for the Revolution than meekly commit suicide. They declared, moreover, that revolution was by no means doomed to failure. The fall of Tibet, they said, had been due to contamination from sentimental bourgeois ideas derived from the ecclesiastical oligarchy. That mistake must not be made again. The basis of the Japanese revolution must be strictly materialistic, and its emotional drive must come from hate of the oppressor, not from metaphysical delusions.</p>
<p>Entirely careless of their lives, the revolutionaries advanced in thousands on the machine-guns of their masters. Before effective help could come from China the régime was broken, and a people’s government was in command. The rulers of China were at this time much occupied with the danger from Russia. They refrained from sending an expeditionary force against Japan, and contented themselves with a very strict blockade. The new Japanese government set about slaughtering all who were suspected of implication in the former regime, and all who disobeyed its orders. Food was the supreme problem. The more people were killed, the more hope for the survivors. The death penalty was therefore inflicted for the most venial offences, and whenever guilt seemed at all plausible. Everything feasible was done to stimulate agriculture. The peasants were forced, under threat of death, to cultivate vast tracts of poor land, for which, owing to the blockade, fertilizer were lacking. It was promised, however, that though in the coming year famine was inevitable, next season would see a plentiful harvest. Loyalty towards the future of Japan and the human race, it was said, demanded the utmost sacrifice from the present generation. But the new land produced a miserable crop; and the people, enfeebled by famine and disease, harassed by brutal treatment, and utterly without the religious stiffening that had fortified Tibet, became incapable of effort, and too physically weak for hard agricultural work. The regime was impotent. The more desperate its plight, the more it killed and tortured. The new rulers knew well that any relaxation of discipline would have brought immediate destruction to themselves; and most of them still sincerely believed that their survival was necessary to the state. In the end the Chinese government, choosing its own time, quietly recovered possession of the Japanese islands.</p>
</section>
