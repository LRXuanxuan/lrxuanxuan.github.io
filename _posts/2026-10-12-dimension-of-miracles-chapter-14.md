<h2>第十四章</h2>
<p>莫兹利开始建造一台能把卡莫迪送回地球的机器，于是卡莫迪被丢在一旁，自行打发时间。他很快无聊得要命。莫兹利工作时必须绝对独处，而奖品显然又回去冬眠了。两名年轻工程师奥林和布鲁克赛德都是乏味的家伙，一心只想着工作，对别的事毫无兴趣。因此，卡莫迪连个说话的人也没有。</p>
<p>他尽可能给自己找事消磨时间。他参观了一座原子制造厂，还尽职尽责地听一个满脸通红的工头解释原子究竟是怎么造出来的。</p>
<p>“从前这些全靠手工。”工头告诉他。“现在改用机器了，不过工艺本质上没变。首先，我们挑一个质子，用莫兹利先生获得专利的能量结合技术给它接上一个中子。然后用标准微观离心机把电子旋到指定位置。之后，再按要求添加其他东西——μ介子、正电子之类的装饰件。就这些。”</p>
<p>“金原子或者铀原子的订单多吗？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“不多。太贵。我们主要生产氢原子。”</p>
<p>“反物质原子呢？”</p>
<p>“我个人一直看不出那玩意儿有什么意义。”工头说。“不过莫兹利先生把它当副业做。当然，反物质是在另一家工厂生产的。”</p>
<p>“当然。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“那东西一碰上普通原子就会爆炸。”</p>
<p>“是，我知道。包装起来肯定很麻烦。”</p>
<p>“不，其实不会。”工头向他保证。“我们装在中性纸箱里。”</p>
<p>他们继续从一台台巨型机器之间穿过。卡莫迪努力想找点别的话题。最后他问：“质子和电子也是你们自己造的吗？”</p>
<p>“不是。莫兹利先生从来不想折腾那么小的东西。亚原子粒子我们都是向分包商采购。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪笑了起来，工头怀疑地看了他一眼。他们继续走，直到卡莫迪的脚都开始发疼。</p>
<p>他觉得又疲倦又迟钝，而这种感觉让他恼火。他告诉自己：我本来应该被迷住才对。自己现在待的地方，可是真的在制造原子，而且还有专门设施用来生产反物质！那边那台巨型机器，会从未经加工的空间里提取宇宙射线，把它们净化，再装进沉重的绿色瓶子里。更远处是一根热探针，用来给老恒星做修补；再往左一点则是……</p>
<p>没有用。走在莫兹利的工厂里，卡莫迪产生的无聊感，和从前参加印第安纳州加里市某钢铁铸造厂的导览时一模一样。那股阴沉的疲惫，那种无声反抗的感觉——他从前怀着虔敬之心，在卢浮宫、普拉多博物馆和大英博物馆幽静的走廊里一连走上几个小时以后，也同样体验过。</p>
<p>他终于意识到：人的惊奇感能够用于欣赏的额度其实很有限。人总会无情地忠于自己，也忠于自己的兴趣。他们始终保持自己的性格，即便那个性格突然被搬到了廷巴克图，或者半人马座阿尔法星。卡莫迪残酷诚实地承认：比起观赏宇宙里大多数奇迹，他宁愿去斯托滑“俯冲”雪道，或者驾驶一艘塔希提式双桅帆船从地狱门大桥下穿过去。他对此感到惭愧，却也没有多少办法。</p>
<p>“我大概确实没什么浮士德精神。”他自言自语。“宇宙的秘密像旧报纸一样铺得到处都是，我脑子里却在想着佛蒙特二月一个晴朗的早晨——雪道还没被人滑得到处都是沟的时候。”</p>
<p>他为此难受了一阵，随后又开始生出反抗情绪：“再说了，就连浮士德也没被迫像参观古典大师画展似的，一路走着看这些玩意儿。要是我记得没错，他可是累死累活才弄到知识的。假如魔鬼让一切来得这么轻松，浮士德大概早就放弃知识，改行爬山之类的了。”</p>
<p>他想了一会儿，又说：“而且，宇宙秘密到底有什么了不起？它们和别的任何东西一样，都被吹得过头了。真说到底，没有任何东西真能像你事先想象的那么好。”</p>
<p>这些话即使并不是真的，至少也成功让卡莫迪舒服了一些。可他依旧很无聊，而莫兹利仍然没有从独处中出来。</p>
<p>* * *</p>
<p>时间显得过得格外缓慢。它真正流逝的速度根本无法判断；但卡莫迪总觉得时间没完没了地拖着，似乎完全可以切分成好几天、好几个星期，甚至可能整整一个月。他还产生了一种感觉——或者说预感——莫兹利想完成自己先前轻轻松松答应下来的事情，并没有那么容易。也许造一颗新行星，比找到一颗旧行星还简单。随着卡莫迪逐渐意识到这项任务的复杂性，以及其中不断冒出来的意外维度，他越来越灰心。</p>
<p>有一天——姑且继续使用这种传统说法——他看着奥林和布鲁克赛德建造一片森林。这片森林是科埃斯二号星的灵长类居民订购的，用来替换他们那片被陨石击毁的旧森林。新森林的全部费用都来自当地学童的捐款；他们筹到了足够的钱，买得起一项一等工程。</p>
<p>工程师和工人离开以后，卡莫迪独自在林中漫步。他惊讶地发现，只要莫兹利和他的团队真的认真起来，他们能把工作做得多么出色；因为这片森林简直是创造性与体贴周到的规划奇迹。</p>
<p>森林中自然形成了适合步行的林间空地，头顶枝叶交织成凉棚，脚下则是松软而斑驳的腐殖土——踩起来舒服，看起来也让眼睛放松。这里的树并不是地球物种，却都很相似。于是卡莫迪决定忽略那些差别，直接用自己熟悉的树名来称呼它们。</p>
<p>整片森林都是最上等的原始林，只长着恰到好处的灌木，让环境保持一点趣味。景观设计师在各处安排了明亮湍急的小溪，没有一条深过三英尺。还有一汪很浅、蓝得浓烈的小湖，湖边长着黄松——或者至少是黄松的等价物。另有一小片袖珍沼泽，红树林和柏树长得密密麻麻，间杂着黑胶树、木兰和柳树，还大方地点缀了不少椰子树。离水边更远、地势更干燥的地方，有一片果树林，里面长着野李、樱桃、栗子、美洲山核桃、橙子、柿子、海枣和无花果。那简直是完美的野餐地点。</p>
<p>森林作为树栖生活空间的潜力同样没有被忽视。年轻的灵长类可以在树干笔直的榆树和悬铃木上飞快爬上爬下；可以在枝杈繁多的橡树和月桂树间玩跟随领头者；也可以摇摇晃晃地穿过连接树冠的藤蔓与爬藤网络。成年居民的需求也没有被忘掉：工程师替他们安排了巨大的红杉，他们可以高高坐在上面打盹或打牌，远离下面尖叫的孩子。</p>
<p>而这还远远不止。即便卡莫迪这种完全没有受过训练的人，也看得出来，这片小森林拥有一套简单、愉快而目的明确的生态系统。这里有鸟、有兽，也有其他生物。有花，还有不会蜇人的蜜蜂替花朵异花授粉、采集花粉；又有一只只胖乎乎、乐呵呵的小熊来偷蜜蜂的蜂蜜。蛴螬啃食花朵，亮翅膀的鸟吃蛴螬，敏捷的红狐狸吃鸟，熊吃狐狸，灵长类则吃熊。</p>
<p>但科埃斯的灵长类同样会死，死后会被埋在森林里浅浅的无棺墓穴中；仪式充满敬意，却不会过分折腾。然后，他们的尸体会被蛴螬、鸟、狐狸、熊，甚至一两种花所取食。通过这种方式，科埃斯人在森林的生死循环里占据了一个完整位置；而他们对此非常满意，因为他们天生就喜欢参与其中。</p>
<p>卡莫迪观察着这一切，一个人走着，胳膊底下夹着仍然保持铜坩埚形态的奖品，一边怀着惴惴不安的心情想着自己失去的故乡。就在这时，他听见身后一根树枝沙沙作响。</p>
<p>没有风，而那些熊此刻全都在池塘里洗澡。卡莫迪慢慢转过身。他知道那里有什么东西，却真心希望什么都没有。</p>
<p>那里确实有东西。</p>
<p>一个生物穿着臃肿的灰色塑料宇航服，脚上是一双弗兰肯斯坦怪物式的大鞋，头顶透明泡形头盔，腰带上还挂着十几件工具、武器和仪器。</p>
<p>卡莫迪立刻认出这个异象是个地球人；不会有别的生物穿成这样。</p>
<p>在那个地球人的右后方，还站着一个身材更纤细、装备相同的人影。卡莫迪一眼就看出，那是个地球女人，而且相当漂亮。</p>
<p>“老天！”卡莫迪说。“你们怎么会偏偏跑到这种地方来？”</p>
<p>“小声点。”那个地球男人说。“我只感谢上帝我们还来得及。不过现在，恐怕最危险的部分才刚刚开始。”</p>
<p>“我们真的还有机会吗，爸爸？”女孩问。</p>
<p>“总会有机会。”男人冷冷一笑。“不过要我下注，我一分钱都不会押。也许马多克斯博士还能想出点办法。”</p>
<p>“他最擅长想办法了，对吧，爸爸？”女孩问。</p>
<p>“当然，玛丽。”男人温柔地回答。“马多克斯博士是有史以来最厉害的。不过这一次，他——我们所有人——可能都把手伸得太远了。”</p>
<p>“我相信我们一定能找到办法。”女孩说，带着一种令人心碎的平静。</p>
<p>“也许吧。”男人说。“不管怎样，我们得让他们看看，这副老脑喷气机里还剩着几磅推力。”他转向卡莫迪，脸色骤然严厉起来。“我只希望你值得我们这么干，老兄。”他说。“为了你，有三条命要押上赌桌。”</p>
<p>这句话实在很难回答，卡莫迪也根本没试。</p>
<p>“排成单列，快步回飞船。”男人说。“让马多克斯博士评估一下现在的局势。”</p>
<p>男人从腰带上拔出一把枪口鼓鼓的手枪，转身走进树林。女孩跟了上去，回头给了卡莫迪一个鼓励的眼神。卡莫迪排在她身后，也跟着走了。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 14</b></p>
<p>Carmody was left to his own devices while Maudsley began construction of a machine to take him back to Earth. He became very bored. Maudsley could only work in utter solitude, and the Prize had apparently gone back into hibernation. Orin and Brookside, the junior engineers, were dull fellows, preoccupied with their work and uninterested in anything else. So Carmody had no one to talk to.</p>
<p>He filled in his time as well as he could. He toured an atom-building factory and listened dutifully while a red-faced foreman explained how it was done.</p>
<p>‘This used to be all handwork,’ the foreman told him. ‘Now machines do it, but the process is really the same. First, we select a proton and attach a neutron to it, using Mr Maudsley’s patented energy-binding. Then, we spin the electrons into position with a standard microcosmic centrifuge. After that, we put in anything else that’s called for – mu mesons, positrons, that sort of gingerbread. And that’s all there is to it.’</p>
<p>‘Do you get much call for gold or uranium atoms?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Not much. Too expensive. Mostly, we turn out hydrogen atoms.’</p>
<p>‘What about antimatter atoms?’</p>
<p>‘I’ve never seen much sense in it, myself,’ the foreman said. ‘But Mr Maudsley carries it as a sideline. Antimatter is made in a separate factory, of course.’</p>
<p>‘Of course,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘That stuff explodes when it comes into contact with normal atoms.’</p>
<p>‘Yes, I know. It must be tricky stuff to package.’</p>
<p>‘No, not really,’ the foreman assured him. ‘We put it up in neutral cartons.’</p>
<p>They continued to walk among the huge machines, and Carmody tried to think of something else to say. Finally he asked, ‘Do you make your own protons and electrons?’</p>
<p>‘Nope, Mr Maudsley never wanted to fool around with that really small stuff. We get our subatomic particles from subcontractors.’</p>
<p>Carmody laughed and the foreman looked at him suspiciously. They continued to walk until Carmody’s feet began to hurt him.</p>
<p>He felt tired and dull, and this annoyed him. He ought to be fascinated, he told himself. Here he was, in a place that actually manufactured atoms, and had separate facilities for creating antimatter! Over there was a gigantic machine that extracted cosmic rays from raw space, and purified them, and bottled them in heavy green containers. Beyond that was a thermal probe, used for doctoring up old stars; and just to the left of it …</p>
<p>It was no use. Walking through Maudsley’s factory elicited in Carmody the same sensations of boredom he had experienced during a guided tour through a Gary, Indiana, steel foundry. And that wave of sullen fatigue, that sense of mute rebellion – he had felt just the same after walking for reverent hours through the hushed corridors of the Louvre, the Prado, the British Museum. One’s sense of wonder, he realized, is only capable of a small amount of appreciation. Men remain inexorably true to themselves and their interests. They stay in character, even if that character is suddenly transported to Timbuktu or Alpha Centauri. And, being ruthlessly honest about it, Carmody realized that he would rather ski the Nosedive at Stowe or sail a Tahiti ketch beneath Hell Gate Bridge than see most of the marvels of the Universe. He was ashamed of this, but there wasn’t much he could do about it.</p>
<p>‘I guess I’m just not particularly Faustian,’ he said to himself. ‘Here are the secrets of the Universe spread around me like old newspapers, and I’m dreaming about a nice February morning in Vermont before the snow has got carved up.’</p>
<p>He felt bad for a while, but then he began to feel rebellious: ‘After all, not even Faust had to walk through this stuff like it was an exhibition of Old Masters. He had to work his ass off, if I remember correctly. If the devil had made it too easy for him, Faust would have probably given up knowledge and taken up mountain-climbing or something.’</p>
<p>He thought for a while. Then he said, ‘Anyhow. What’s such a big deal about the secrets of the Universe? They’ve been overrated, just like everything else. When you come right down to it, nothing’s as good as you think it’s going to be.’</p>
<p>All of that, even if it were not true, at least served to make Carmody feel better. But he was still bored. And Maudsley still did not come out of his seclusion.</p>
<p>* * *</p>
<p>Time passed with apparent slowness. It was impossible to judge its true rate; but Carmody had the impression that it dragged on and on, and could have been subdivided into days and weeks, perhaps even a month. He also had the feeling – or the premonition – that Maudsley was not finding it easy to do what he had promised so lightly. Perhaps it was simpler to build a new planet than to find an old one. Becoming aware of the complexity of the task and its many unexpected dimensions, Carmody grew disheartened.</p>
<p>One day (to speak conventionally) he watched Orin and Brookside construct a forest. It had been ordered by the primates of Coeth II, to replace their old forest, which had been struck by a meteor. This new one had been paid for entirely out of schoolchildren’s donations; a sufficient sum had been raised to purchase a first-class job.</p>
<p>When the engineers and workmen had left, Carmody wandered alone through the trees. He marvelled at how good a job Maudsley and his team could do when they put their minds to it, for this forest was a marvel of creative and considerate planning.</p>
<p>There were natural glades for walking, with a leafy arbour above and a springy, dappled loam below – enticing to the foot and restful to the eye. The trees were not Earth species, but they were similar. So Carmody chose to ignore the differences and name them after the trees he had known.</p>
<p>That forest was all prime first-growth timberland, with just enough underbrush to keep it interesting. It was landscaped here and there with bright, rushing streams, none deeper than three feet. There was a shallow, intensely blue little lake, flanked by ponderosa pine or its equivalent. And there was a miniature swamp, dense with mangrove and cypress, studded with blackgums, magnolias and willows, and liberally sprinkled coconut palms. Farther back from the water’s edge, on drier land, was a grove in which could be found wild plum and cherry trees, and chestnuts, pecans, oranges, persimmons, dates and figs. It was a perfect place for a picnic.</p>
<p>Nor had the arboreal potentialities of the forest been overlooked. The young primates could race up and down the straight-backed elms and sycamores, play follow-the-leader in the many-branched oaks and laurels, or teeter precariously across the tangled network of vines and creepers that interconnected the treetops. Nor had the needs of their elders been ignored; there were giant redwoods for them, where they could doze or play cards, high above the screaming children.</p>
<p>But there was much more than this; even an untrained person like Carmody could see that the little forest had been given a simple, pleasant and purposeful ecology. There were birds, animals, and other creatures. There were flowers, and stingless bees to cross-fertilize them and gather the pollen, and jolly fat little bears to steal the bees’ honey. There were grubs to feast on the flowers, and bright-winged birds to feast off the grubs, and quick red foxes to eat the birds, and bears to eat the foxes, and primates to eat the bears.</p>
<p>But the primates of Coeth also die, and are buried in the forest in shallow, coffinless graves, reverently but without undue fuss, and are fed upon by grubs, birds, foxes, bears and even one or two species of flowers. In this way, the Coethians have an integral place in the forest cycle of life and death; and this pleases them very much since they are born participants.</p>
<p>Carmody observed all of this, walking alone with the Prize (still a cauldron) under his arm, and thinking tremulous thoughts about his lost homeland. Then he heard a branch rustle behind him.</p>
<p>There was no wind, and the bears were all bathing in the pond. Carmody turned around slowly, knowing something was there but wishing it weren’t.</p>
<p>There was indeed something there. There was a creature wearing a bulky, grey plastic space suit. Frankenstein-type shoes, a transparent bubble helmet and a belt from which dangled a dozen or more tools, weapons and instruments.</p>
<p>Carmody immediately recognized this apparition as an Earthman; no other creature could dress that way.</p>
<p>Behind and to the right of the Earthman was a slighter, similarly clad figure. Carmody saw at once that this was an Earthwoman, and a very attractive one.</p>
<p>‘Good lord!’ Carmody said. ‘How did you people happen to come here, of all places?’</p>
<p>‘Not so loud,’ the Earthman said. ‘I just thank God we arrived in time. But now, I’m afraid, the most dangerous part is ahead of us.’</p>
<p>‘Do we have any chance at all, Father?’ the girl asked.</p>
<p>‘There is always a chance,’ the man said, with a grim smile. ‘But I wouldn’t bet any money on it. Still, maybe Dr Maddox can figure something out.’</p>
<p>‘He’s very good at that, isn’t he, Dad?’ the girl asked.</p>
<p>‘Sure he is, Mary,’ the man replied in a gentle voice. ‘Doc Maddox is the finest there ever was. But he – all of us – may have overreached ourselves this time.’</p>
<p>‘I’m sure we’ll find a way,’ the girl said, with heartbreaking serenity.</p>
<p>‘Maybe,’ the man said. ‘Anyhow, we’ll show ’em there’s still a few pounds of thrust in the old brain-jets.’ He turned to Carmody, and his expression hardened. ‘I just hope you’re worth it, Mac,’ he said. ‘Three lives are going on the line for you.’</p>
<p>It was a difficult proposition to respond to, and Carmody didn’t even try.</p>
<p>‘Single file quick-step back to the ship,’ the man said. ‘We’ll get Doc Maddox’s assessment of the situation.’</p>
<p>Drawing a bulb-nosed gun from his belt, the man turned into the woods. The girl followed, giving Carmody an encouraging look over her shoulder. Carmody fell into line behind her.</p>
