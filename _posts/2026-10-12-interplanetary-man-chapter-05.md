<h3>五、这一切究竟为了什么？</h3>
<p>现在终于来到问题真正的核心：殖民其他行星，究竟有没有意义？我们到底想达到什么目的？这一切究竟为了什么？为什么不干脆待在自己的母星上，照旧凑合着过日子？</p>
<p>大体说来，人类处理其他行星时，可能受到三种不同动机支配。（我暂且排除科学好奇心。它对某些个人当然可能非常重要，但大概不会成为决定性因素。）</p>
<p>第一，人类也许只是想利用那些世界的物质资源，增加地球人的奢侈享受。这一目标可能需要在其他行星建立工业据点，却未必需要大规模殖民。政策非常简单：利用其他行星，让人类获得尽可能多的快乐，使所有人都尽可能富足；换句话说，创造一种社会，使每个人都拥有今天只有百万富翁才享有的特权。一切苦役都将消失。只需按按钮或扳开关，各种精美饮食和花样百出的娱乐便随时可得。不只是“电影”，还有“触感娱乐”“嗅感娱乐”“性爱娱乐”，会像阿道司·赫胥黎等人预见的那样，提供无穷无尽的享乐。之后如果出现任何厌倦或倦怠，也会立刻用新的兴趣或合适的药物加以纠正。再没有人需要做任何不愉快或无聊的事情。各种机器都会成为每个人不知疲倦、绝对服从的奴仆。所有人都会成为贵族——当然，是这个词较坏意义上的贵族，而不是较好的意义。所有人都会成为享乐成瘾者，习惯一切奢侈，成为被隔绝于生活更严峻可能性之外、因而永远长不大的娇惯儿童。当然，要达到这种严格享乐主义的乌托邦，许多人此前必须为了科学、探索和发明而勤奋、冒险、献身，才能让这种奢华世界成为可能。但是一旦新秩序彻底建立，它也许只需极少的人类指导，便能永远自动维持下去。</p>
<p>我想，我们大多数人都会同意：一定程度的奢侈无害，甚至具有文明化作用；但超过某一点以后，奢侈增加就会导致精神上的退化。人类凭借多才多能和智力而崛起。如果最终进入一种对恒定环境完美适应的稳定状态，人类独特的能力便会逐渐丧失，智力会萎缩。这样的世界社会也许能一成不变地存续几百万年；但迟早环境会提出新的挑战，而人类届时已经失去应付挑战的机智。因此我的结论是：如果英国星际学会所有献身努力最终结出的果实，不过是拿其他世界的财富把人类腐化掉，那么诸位最好都别再交会费了。</p>
<p>第二种可能的目标，是单纯增加人类控制环境的力量，并把这种力量扩展到新的环境上。</p>
<p>在欧洲和美国工业化的鼎盛时期，力量本身在许多人眼中似乎就是目的。某一类缺乏教养的心灵，有一种极强的冲动，想在环境上留下自己的印记——什么印记都行，只要是“我的印记”、是由我亲手造成的。人类这种动物喜欢把自己的姓名首字母刻在树木、岩石、课桌和公共纪念物上。他们仅仅为了享受支配的快感，就会损坏美好的东西。他们不假思索地满足原始的建造冲动，不顾更长远的后果；有时无害，有时却以灾难性的方式，使所谓建设反而比破坏更具破坏性。孩子无害地捏泥饼，或者玩机械拼装玩具；成年人则不那么无害地建立帝国，或创办庞大的商业事业。通常，这种狂热活动总能找到某种听起来颇合理的借口：泥饼是魔法城堡；帝国是文明的工具；商业事业则为人们提供舒适或奢侈。但在根底里，动机常常只是无法满足的瘙痒：非得在环境上留下些什么不可，随便什么都行，而且越大越好。这个冲动本身无害，甚至值得肯定；但我们必须学会用无害、最好还真正有用的方式留下印记。</p>
<p>更确切地说，想留下痕迹这种冲动相当健康，条件是：即使它没有真正服务于某种更高目标，至少也不能积极妨碍恰当的发展。决不能任它退化成一种强迫性的乱画乱动，无论规模多么宏大。对整个人类来说，危险在于：一旦解决眼下迫在眉睫的问题，人类便可能滑入这样一种默认前提，认为一切集体行动的目标，不过是在越来越大的环境中留下越来越大的印记。力量太容易变成自身的目的。从前，自我扩张型的人追求地方专制或世界帝国；往后，他们更可能通过金钱权力实现支配，或者在高度组织化的国家——也许是某种极权世界国家——里，以盛气凌人的方式掌握重要职能。有人说，权力使人腐化，绝对权力使人绝对腐化。无论世界国家是资本主义、共产主义还是社会民主主义的，只要掌权者不能坚定不移地以真正价值为方向，就会面临严重腐化的危险。他们会轻易把自己的权力欲理想化，并说服人类相信，人真正正确的目标只是支配环境。这样一来，地球最终也许会接近阿道司·赫胥黎笔下《美丽新世界》的样子：整个社会被组织到极端高效，其目的却只是尽可能多地生产。在那个可怕的世界里，工人得到无限而廉价的享乐，精英、官僚和技术人员则从指挥整个社会生活中获得满足。</p>
<p>如果未来地球社会按这种原则组织，那么其他行星也会被用于同一目的。表面上，目标是把它们当作原料来源，以增加这个星球或其他星球上人类的舒适和奢侈；但在这个正统经济动机背后，还隐藏着未经承认的赤裸权力欲。个体会被权力腐化，整个物种同样也会。人类也许会痴迷于仅仅在太阳系里留下一个巨大的印记。从原则上说，这和一个顽童放火烧荒地、往窗户扔石头，没有任何区别。</p>
<p>第三种控制其他行星的动机，是利用它们使人类得到“最大限度”的实现，或者说，使人类成为“最好的”自己；也就是让人类物种最发达的能力获得充分表达。这个目标虽然极其模糊，却更有希望。它真正意味着什么？大体可以有两种解释。第一种是纯粹的人文主义解释；第二种则牵涉某种超越人类本身的东西，尽管我们主要通过它在人类自身之中的不完善表现来认识它。必须面对的问题是：我们有理由把人类仅仅视为自身目的么？所谓“善”，是不是只等于人类所意欲之物？某件东西之所以称为“善”，难道仅仅因为、或者仅仅就在于人类想要它？还是说，人类其实更应该被视为达到某种目的的手段，一种用来实现某个在一定意义上独立于人类当前发展阶段实际本性的目的之工具？这些问题若不牵涉非常困难的哲学问题，就连清楚表述都做不到，更不用说回答。它们不能简单地用“是”或“否”作答。答案更可能是：“在某种意义上，人类是目的；在另一种意义上，人类不是目的，而是工具。”这就像物理学家可以合理地说：在某种意义上电子是粒子，而在另一种意义上，它不是粒子，而是一列波。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" lang="en" hidden>
<h3><a id="a1087"></a>V. What is it All For?</h3>
<p><span class="t28">T</span><span class="t31">HIS</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">BRINGS</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">ME</span><span class="t30"> </span>at last to the real crux of my subject. Would there be any point in colonizing the planets? What are we getting at? What is it all for? Why not just stay put on our native planet and muck along as before? Broadly, there seem to be three possible motives that might control man’s dealings with the planets. (I exclude scientific curiosity, which, though it might be an important motive with some individuals, is not likely to be the determining factor.)</p>
<p>First, the physical resources of those worlds might be exploited simply in order to increase the luxury of human beings on earth. This aim might involve the creation of industrial settlements on the planets, but it might not involve large-scale colonization. The policy would be simply to use the planets to afford to human beings as much pleasure as possible, to give to all of them the greatest possible affluence; in fact, to create a society in which every individual would have the privileges that only millionaires have to-day. Drudgery would be completely abolished. All manner of superfine food and drink, and all manner of ingenious amusements would be constantly available at the cost of merely pressing buttons or switches. Not only the “movies” but also the “feelies” and “smellies” and “sexies” would provide unending beatitude, in the manner foreseen by Aldous Huxley and others. Any subsequent boredom or lassitude would be at once corrected by means of fresh interests or appropriate drugs. No-one would ever do anything unpleasant or uninteresting. Machines of all sorts would be the tireless and obedient slaves of every man. All men would be aristocrats in the worse sense of the word, not the better. All would be pleasure-addicts, accustomed to every luxury; spoilt children who, through being shielded from the sterner possibilities of life, would simply never grow up. Of course, <span class="t24">before</span> the attainment of this strictly hedonistic Utopia, many men would have had to live laboriously and dangerously and devotedly, in the cause of science and exploration and invention, in order to make such a luxury-world possible. But once the new order had been thoroughly established, it might maintain itself perpetually with a minimum of direction of human action.</p>
<p>Most of us, I think, would agree that, though a certain amount of luxury is a harmless and even civilizing thing, there is a point beyond which the increase of luxury leads to spiritual degradation. Man has risen by versatility and intelligence. If he were to enter at last upon a stable condition of perfect adaptation to an unchanging environment, he would gradually lose his distinctive powers. Intelligence would atrophy. The world-society might perhaps survive unchanged for millions of years; but sooner or later man would be confronted with some new challenge from the environment, and would have lost the wit to cope with it. I conclude that if the fruit of all the devotion of the British Interplanetary Society is to be merely the debauching of mankind with the riches of other worlds, you had better all stop paying your subscriptions.</p>
<p>The second possible aim in relation to the planets is simply to increase man’s power over the environment, and to extend that power so as to tackle fresh environments.</p>
<p>In the heyday of the industrialization of Europe and America, power did seem to many people an end in itself. The craving to leave one’s mark on the environment, any sort of mark, so long as it is <span class="t24">my</span> mark, made actually by me, is very strong in a certain type of uncultured mind. Human animals carve their initials on trees, rocks, school desks and public monuments. They deface beautiful objects simply for the lust of power. They satisfy their primitive impulses for construction thoughtlessly, without regard to the remoter consequences; sometimes harmlessly, but sometimes disastrously in ways that are less <span class="t24">con</span>structive than <span class="t24">de</span>structive. Children harmlessly make mud pies or play with meccano sets. Adults less innocently build empires or great commercial ventures. Generally some fairly plausible excuse is found for all this feverish activity. The mud pies are magic castles; the empires, instruments of civilization; the commercial ventures, purveyors of comfort or luxury. But at bottom the motive is often simply the insatiable itch to make a mark on the environment, any old mark, but the bigger the better. In itself, the impulse is harmless, even worthy; but we have to learn to make our mark in inoffensive and if possible actually useful ways.</p>
<p>More precisely, the itch to leave a mark is quite wholesome, on condition that, even if it does not actually serve some higher aim, at least it does not positively hinder proper development. It must not be allowed to degenerate into mere obsessive doodling or meddling on however vast a scale. The danger for mankind as a whole is that, having solved its present urgent problems it will slip in to the assumption that the goal of all its corporate action is simply to make a bigger and bigger mark on a bigger and bigger environment. Power is all too apt to become an end in itself. In the old days self-assertive individuals aimed at local tyranny or world empire. Henceforth they will be more likely to dominate either through money power or through fulfilling important functions domineeringly in a highly organized state, perhaps a world-state of totalitarian type. It has been said that power corrupts, and absolute power corrupts absolutely. Those who control the world-state, whether it is capitalist or communist or social-democratic, will be in danger of grave corruption unless they are imperturbably orientated to the true values. They will readily idealize their own power lust, and persuade mankind that the right goal for man is simply dominance over the environment. And so in time the planet may approximate to Aldous Huxley’s <span class="t24">Brave New World</span>, in which society is organized to the extreme pitch of efficiency with the aim simply of producing as much as possible. In that horrid world the workers are given unlimited easy pleasure, while the elite, the bureaucrats and technicians, have the satisfaction of directing the whole life of society.</p>
<p>If the future terrestrial society is organized on these principles, then the other planets will be used for the same end. Ostensibly the aim will be to use them as sources of raw material for man’s comfort and luxury, on this planet or others; but behind this orthodox economic motive will lie the unacknowledged motive of sheer power. And if individuals can be corrupted by power, so can a whole species. Man may become obsessed with a passion merely to make a big mark on the solar system. In principle there is no difference between this aim and that of the urchin who sets fire to the heath or throws stones at windows.</p>
<p>The third possible motive for gaining control of the planets is that of using them to make the “most” of man, or the “best” of him; in fact, for the full expression of the most developed capacities of the human species. Here we have a goal which, though extremely vague, is more promising. What does it really imply? Broadly, it may be interpreted in two different ways, one purely humanistic, the other involving a reference to something over and above man, though it is known to us mainly through its imperfect manifestation in man himself. The question to be faced is this. Are we justified in regarding man <span class="t24">simply</span> as an end in himself? Is “good” <span class="t24">simply</span> whatever man wills? Is a thing to be called “good” merely <span class="t24">because</span> or <span class="t24">in that</span> man wills it? Or, on the other hand, is man to be regarded rather as a means to an end, an instrument for the fulfilling of an end that is in some sense independent of his actual nature in its present stage of development? These questions cannot be even clearly stated, let alone answered, without raising very difficult philosophical problems. They cannot be truly answered with a plain “yes” or “no.” Rather the answer must be of the type, “In one sense man is an end, in another sense not an end but an instrument.” In the same manner the physicists may justifiably say that in one sense an electron is a particle, and in another sense not a particle but a train of waves.</p>
