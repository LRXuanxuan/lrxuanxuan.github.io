<h2>二、村落文化</h2>
<p>有一种引人注目的制度几乎遍布全世界，那就是村庄“会议”：全村居民聚在一起，规划共同生活。不同地区的“会议”形式千差万别，但几乎总是围绕一座兼具村民会堂、教堂与酒馆性质的建筑展开。语言演变出现了某种古怪岔路，以至于所有国家都把这种建筑称作“普布”（poob）。村民每天晚上在这里闲谈、做游戏、唱歌，饮用合成酒浆，吸食合成烟草。这里也是公共餐厅，朋友可以边吃边聚；许多比较爱社交的村民每天都来这里用餐；村庄在这里招待访客，也在这里举办宴会。音乐会和讲座同样在此举行。按照固定周期，村民会召开正式“会议”，讨论公共事务、解决纠纷。婚礼、葬礼、公民成年礼，以及纪念地方、民族或全人类重大事件的仪式，也都在这里举行。</p>
<p>普布中陈列着村庄的体育奖杯、历史遗物和艺术珍品。这里还有村庄的“圣柜”，平日藏在帷幕后面，只有重大场合才公开展示。它同时是保存重要文件的保险柜、吉祥物、神圣象征和祭坛。圣柜是一只巨大的雕花箱子，上面常常安放象征性雕像或图画。有时它由本地工匠制作，有时则是从邻近城市或某个外国珍重购入的宝物。这些物品的艺术价值和象征力量差别极大。有少数圣柜会吸引来自整个行星的朝圣者；另一些虽然在本村人心中无比亲爱而神圣，却完全不会引起外界注意。这些象征物有时以高度程式化的方式表现村庄、民族或全人类历史中具有特殊意义的事件；有时则象征爱情、理性、家庭、人类统一，或人与宇宙的关系。遇到婚礼或者定期的“静观日”等庄严场合，圣柜便会揭幕，全体村民在它面前静坐几分钟。随后奏起合唱或器乐，村长、专门受托的村民或来客主持简短朴素的仪式；仪式可能使用一套固定的词句，也可能即兴进行，甚至只用无声手势。仪式结束后，圣柜重新盖上帷幕，村民则共同饮酒或进餐。</p>
<p>许多地方的普布干脆就是古老的村教堂或庙宇。在城市里，它可能是大教堂、市政厅，或者别的历史建筑。所有城市以及每个国家的首都，都会举行与村庄会议本质相同、只是仪式性更强的集会。北京、贝拿勒斯、莫斯科和旧金山这四大全球文化中心，会举办特别重要的会议。而最崇高的仪式，则是每年在神圣拉萨举行的纪念大会。</p>
<p>经济问题既已解决，公众注意力越来越转向整个人类的文化生活。教育不再受那种要求年轻人为个人主义经济“生存竞争”作准备的需要支配，也不再以训练高效而驯服的机器人为目标。职业训练仍是教育的重要部分，却不再吞噬青年全部的时间和注意力。所有儿童主要在自己的故乡村庄长大。寄宿学校已经不存在——再也没有大批少年被聚集起来，像修道院成员一样与现实世界的生活隔绝。一般说来，孩子都住在家里，在正常农庄生活中成长，并在成人多样的活动环境里自然掌握各种技能。村校有时也会因为低效率或过度宽松受到严厉批评，但总体上都受到人类新传统的鼓舞。每个国家都极为慎重地选择教师，并在大型寄宿制大学里对他们进行细致训练。有些国家会让二十到一百个相邻村庄联合起来，为整个地区较聪明的儿童建立一所共同学校；另一些国家则反对这种办法，认为它会在聪明与迟钝者之间制造阶级分隔。于是两类孩子都留在村校，不过能力明显更高的学生，只要能够跟上正常课程，就允许缺席课堂，把节省出来的时间用于发展特殊才能或兴趣。一套极其严格的职业甄选制度，会从即将离校的学生中挑出那些在特定职业上具有突出天赋的人；也会挑出一般智力很高、适合成为教师、科学研究者或技术哲学研究者的人；此外，还会选拔那些在组织和人际交往方面具有特殊才能、适合从事工业管理、大规模经济规划与政治领导的人。</p>
<p>具有艺术潜质的人同样会被挑选出来。他们可以进入大型艺术学院或大学寄宿学习，也可以领取维持生活的津贴，让自己的天赋自行寻找道路，再靠出售作品补充那份微薄收入。国家有意——而绝非出于吝啬——只向那些选择避开一切国家组织职业的青年男女提供最低限度的帮助，无论他们要冒险的领域是艺术、科学还是哲学。这样做，是希望淘汰那些其实并没有真正创造能力的人。另一方面，无论某位探索者的作品在公众看来多么荒唐或骇人，他至少总能获得最低津贴。只要作品确有一点真正价值——即使大多数人看不出来——甚至有时根本没有真正价值，也很可能卖得出去。因为除非作品在技术上实在拙劣，又极端怪诞得只有作者本人能够理解，否则在这个文化意识高度发达的新世界里，总能找到某种市场。绘画、雕塑、音乐和文学都有人需要：有的面向一个国家的公众，有的面向全球公众，有的则面向某个专门群体，而这些群体各自关注某一艺术领域或体裁。一个无人问津的年轻画家，仅凭卖出一件作品就从穷困突然跃入富裕和名望，并不罕见。当然，许多艺术家没有这种运气，只能一生靠维持津贴过活。其中有些人只是超前于时代，死后才闻名世界；但绝大多数不过是缺乏才能的热心爱好者。没有人会因为他们徒劳却无害的人生而心生怨恨，因为共同体完全负担得起。事实上，大多数农庄都对偶然经过的旅行者敞开大门，所有村庄也都为持续流动的访客提供食宿；因此，这些失败艺术家甚至可以一路靠各地招待吃住走遍地球，把自己的津贴全部花在衣物和额外舒适上。</p>

<h2>ii. VILLAGE CULTURE</h2>
<p>ONE REMARKABLE INSTITUTION was almost universal, namely the village ‘meeting’, a gathering of all the villagers for the planning of their communal life. The ‘meeting’ took a great variety of forms in different lands; but nearly always it centred on a building which combined many of the characters of a village hall, a church, and a public house. By some freak of the evolution of language it was known in all countries as the ‘poob’. In it the village met every evening to yarn, play games, sing, drink their synthetic elixirs, smoke their synthetic tobaccos. It was also the communal eating-house where friends could meet over a meal, where many of the more sociable villagers fed every day, where the guests of the village were entertained, where village banquets were held. In it also the villagers met for concerts and lectures. In it at regular intervals they held their formal ‘meetings’ to discuss communal business and settle disputes. There they also held their sacred ceremonies, such as marriages, funerals, initiations into citizenship, commemorations of great events, local, national, or cosmopolitan.</p>
<p>The poob housed the village sports trophies, historical relics, and art treasures. It contained also, normally behind curtains, but displayed on great occasions, the village ‘ark’. This was at once a safe where valuable documents were preserved, a mascot, a sacred symbol, and a shrine. The ark was a great carved chest, often surmounted by a symbolic statue or picture. Sometimes it was the work of local craftsmen, sometimes it was a much treasured import from the near-by city or some foreign land. These objects varied greatly in aesthetic value and in symbolic power. A few were visited by pilgrims from every part of the planet. Others, though dear and sacred to the hearts of their own villagers, drew no attention from elsewhere. These symbols sometimes represented in a stylized manner incidents of special significance in the life of the village or the nation or mankind. Sometimes they symbolized love or reason or family, or the unity of the human race, or man’s relation to the cosmos. On any solemn occasion, such as a marriage or one of the regular ‘days of contemplation’, the ark would be unveiled, and the assembled villagers would sit in silence for a few minutes before it. Music would follow, choral or instrumental, and then the brief and simple ceremony would be performed by the village headman or some specially deputed villager or stranger, either with some well-established form of words or impromptu, or perhaps with silent gesture. When the ceremony was over the ark would be once more veiled, and the villagers would drink or feed together.</p>
<p>Often the poob was simply the ancient village church or temple. In cities it might be the cathedral or the city hall or some other historic building. Meetings of essentially the same type as the village meetings, but more ritualistic, took place in all the cities and ill each national metropolis. Specially important meetings occurred in the four great cultural world-centres, Peking, Benares, Moscow, and San Francisco. But most exalted of all were the annual commemorations in sacred Lhasa.</p>
<p>Now that the economic problem had been solved, public attention was more and more directed to the cultural life of the race. Education was no longer dominated by the need to equip the young for the individualistic economic ‘battle of life’, nor yet by the demand for efficient and docile robots. Vocational training was still an important element in education, but it no longer devoured the whole time and attention of the young people. All children were brought up mainly in their native village. There were no boarding schools, great swarms of young things living in monastic isolation from the life of the world. Normally every child lived at home, and grew up in the normal environment of farm life, acquiring the various skills which were demanded by the varied life of adults. The village schools, though some were severely criticized for inefficiency or laxity, were in the main inspired by the new tradition of the race. In every country the teachers were jealously selected, and carefully trained in the great residential universities. In some countries a group of a score or a hundred neighbouring villages might combine to set up a common school for the brighter children of the whole district. Elsewhere this principle was rejected as tending to create a class division between the bright and the dull. Instead, both types were kept in the village school, but those who showed superior capacity were allowed to absent themselves from classes so long as they kept pace with the class work. The time thus gained they spent on developing their special powers or interests. A searching system of vocational selection skimmed off from the village schools those children of leaving age who had superior aptitude for particular occupations, and those who, through high general intelligence were fitted to become teachers or research workers in some branch of science or in technical philosophy, and also those whose special talents for organizing and social intercourse were needed for industrial management, large-scale economic planning, and political leadership.</p>
<p>Potential artists were also selected. These might either go into residence at one of the great art schools or universities; or else, living on the maintenance grant, they could allow their genius to pursue its own course, eking out their meagre grant by selling their works. Of set purpose, and not through mere niggardliness, the state allowed the young man or woman who chose to avoid all state-organized professions only a bare minimum of help, whether his field of adventure was art or science or philosophy. Thus it was hoped to weed out those who had not actually ‘got it in them’ to produce creative work. On the other hand, no matter how preposterous or shocking to the public his products might be, the adventurer was at least assured of his minimum grant. And if it had any real merit (unperceived by the majority), and indeed often if it had no real merit at all, he might well succeed in selling. For, unless his work was both technically feeble and quite extravagantly idiosyncratic, it was very likely to find some sort of market in the new culturally conscious world. For in this new world-society pictures, statues, music, and writing were in demand, in some cases by the national, in others by the world-wide public, and in yet others by one or other of the special publics, each interested in some particular sphere or genre of art. It. was not uncommon for a neglected young painter to leap from penury to affluence and fame on the sale of a single work. Many artists, however, had no such luck, and were forced to live on the maintenance grant alone throughout their lives. Some of these, ahead of their time, became world-famous after death, but the great majority were merely untalented enthusiasts. No one dreamed of grudging them their futile but harmless careers, since the community could well afford to maintain them. Indeed, since most farms kept open house for any stray travellers, and all villages provided meals and beds for a constant flow of visitors, these artistic failures could eat and sleep their way over the face of the earth and use their maintenance grant wholly for clothing and extra comforts.</p>
</section>
