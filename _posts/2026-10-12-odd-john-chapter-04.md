<h3>第四章　约翰与他的长辈</h3>
<p>我认为，同斯蒂芬的那场斗殴是约翰一生中最重要的几个界标之一；不过从外表看，生活仍大体照旧。唯一明显的变化，是他不再打架，而且独处的时间多了不少。</p>
<p>他和斯蒂芬恢复了友谊，但从此那成了一种不太自在的友谊。两个人似乎都努力表现得友好，却谁也无法真正放松。我想，斯蒂芬的精神受到了相当严重的打击。倒不是因为他害怕再挨一顿揍，而是他的自尊心受了伤。我找机会对他说，被约翰打败并不丢脸，因为约翰显然根本不是普通孩子。斯蒂芬立刻抓住这份安慰，声音里带着近乎歇斯底里的颤动，说：“我当时觉得——我也说不清自己觉得什么——就像一条狗咬了主人，然后挨了惩罚。我觉得……有点内疚，好像自己干了坏事。”</p>
<p>我想，这时约翰开始更清楚地意识到，自己同我们其余的人之间隔着怎样一道鸿沟。与此同时，他大概又强烈地需要伙伴——但那必须是水准远远高于普通人的伙伴。他仍然同过去的同伴一起玩，而且多数活动中，他依然是那个真正推动事情的人；可他总带着某种疏离感，像是在一边参与、一边暗自觉得好笑。虽然从外表看，他比整群孩子里任何人都更矮、更幼小，有时却使我想起一个满头白发的小老头，屈尊同一群小猩猩玩耍。某个疯闹的游戏进行到一半，他常常会突然退出，晃进花园，躺在草地上做起白日梦；或者在母亲身边转悠，一边看她做家务、收拾花园，或者——帕克斯很常做的一件事——什么也不做，只等下一件事情发生，一边同她讨论人生。</p>
<p>在某些方面，约翰同母亲的关系像一个人类弃婴同养大他的母狼；或者更准确些，像同一头养母牛。他显然把完全的信任和感情都给了她，甚至怀着一种深切却困惑的敬意；可是每当她跟不上他的思路，或者无法回答他关于宇宙的无数问题，他又会感到苦恼。</p>
<p>当然，“养母”这个比喻并不完善。在一个方面，它甚至完全错误。就智力而言，帕克斯远远不如他；但很明显，还有另一个领域，当时的她至少与他平等，甚至可能高于他。母子两人都有一种特殊的能力，能够品味经验本身；他们有一种异常敏锐的生活滋味感。我相信，从根本上说，这不过是一种极其独特而细腻的幽默感。我常常看见，当我们其他人完全不知道有什么好笑时，他们两人之间会悄悄交换一个心领神会、忍俊不禁的眼神。我猜，这种被掩藏的笑意多少同约翰刚刚苏醒的“对人的兴趣”有关，也同他越来越能洞察自身动机有关。但我们身上的哪些举止，在这两个人眼里究竟如此有趣，我始终没弄明白。</p>
<p>约翰同父亲的关系就完全不同。他充分利用医生那颗活跃的头脑；但两人之间并没有自发的亲近感，除了智力兴趣之外，几乎也没有共同爱好。我常常看见，约翰听父亲说话时，脸上会一闪而过讥讽，甚至厌恶的抽动。尤其当托马斯自以为正在向孩子发表某种关于人性或宇宙的深刻见解时，这种表情最容易出现。当然，激起约翰这种嘲笑或反感的绝不只有托马斯，我自己和许多人都难辞其咎。不过托马斯是首要“犯人”，也许因为他是最聪明的一个，因此也就成了展示本物种心智局限最醒目、最有说服力的样本。我怀疑，约翰经常是故意引导父亲这样暴露自己的。仿佛这孩子曾对自己说：“无论如何，我得理解这些占据着整个星球的奇异生物。这里正好有一个上等样本，我得拿他做实验。”</p>
<p>说到这里，我最好承认，我自己也越来越被“约翰”这个奇异生物吸引，而且在不知不觉间受到他的影响。回头看那段时期，我现在能看出，他其实已经把我列入未来要使用的人选，开始迈出捕获我的第一步。他最主要的办法，就是冷静地认定：虽然我是个中年男人，我却是他的奴隶；无论我怎样嘲笑他、训斥他，在内心深处，我都已经承认他是更高等的存在，并且本质上是他忠实的猎犬。眼下我尽可以拿“独立生活”自娱——那时我还是个并不怎么上心的自由记者——可迟早我总得乖乖回到主人脚边。</p>
<p>约翰实际年龄接近八岁半时，别人通常会把他当成一个非常奇特的五六岁孩子。他仍然玩儿童游戏，也仍然被其他孩子当作一个孩子，只不过有点怪。然而任何成年人的谈话，他都能参与。当然，他不是聪明得过头，就是对现实生活无知得过头，因此从来无法以真正“正常”的方式扮演自己的角色；但他绝不会仅仅显得低人一等。即便最天真的一句话，也常常带着令人吃惊的意味。</p>
<p>不过，约翰的天真正在迅速消失。这时他开始以难以置信的速度进行海量阅读。只要某本书讨论的东西没有超出他的经验范围，不论内容多么艰深，他最多几个小时就能彻底掌握；大多数书，他十五分钟就能充分吸收。但绝大部分书，他只翻上几眼，便把它当作毫无价值的东西扔到一边。</p>
<p>阅读过程中，他偶尔会要求父亲、母亲或我带他去看某种制造流程，或者下矿井、参观船只、游览某个历史地点，又或者去实验室观看某项实验。我们会尽力满足这些要求，可很多时候，我们没有必要的人脉和资格。另有不少计划好的出行，则被帕克斯对“孩子不必要地引起公众注意”的恐惧阻止。每当我们真的带他外出时，都得向相关人员装作约翰只是偶然跟来，而且他的兴趣不过是孩子气的、不懂行的好奇。</p>
<p>当然，约翰了解世界并不依赖长辈。他已经养成一种习惯，会主动同各种各样的人攀谈，“弄清楚他们在做什么，又怎么想事情”。无论是在街上、火车里还是乡间小路上，任何人若被这个有一双巨大眼睛、羊羔绒般头发、说话却像成年人的小男孩礼貌而巧妙地搭上话，多半都会在不知不觉间被他引着说出远超自己原先打算透露的东西。我确信，靠这种别开生面的调查研究，约翰一两个月里对人性和现代社会问题的了解，已经超过我们大多数人一辈子所能学到的。</p>
<p>我有幸亲眼旁观过其中一次访谈。那一次的“研究对象”，是邻近工业城市里一家大型百货商店的老板。出于稳妥，我们姑且称他为马格内特先生。计划是在他乘九点半火车去上班时搭话。约翰允许我在场，但有一个条件：我必须假装同他素不相识。</p>
<p>我们先让猎物通过检票口，在一等车厢里坐定。随后才去售票处；我颇不自在地买了“一张一等成人票和一张半票”。我们各自晃进马格内特先生的车厢。我进去时，约翰已经安坐在那位大人物正对面的角落里。马格内特先生偶尔会从报纸后面瞥一眼这个奇异的孩子：前额像峭壁，双眼像洞穴。我在约翰斜对面的角落里坐好不久，又有两个商人进来，各自摊开报纸。</p>
<p>约翰看上去正在专心读《漫画剪报》之类的刊物。虽然买这份东西原本纯粹是为了当舞台道具，但我相信，他当时确实也能够从中得到乐趣。因为在这个阶段，尽管天赋惊人，他骨子里仍然是个“粗俗的小男孩”。接下来的谈话中，他显然有意迎合商人对于“早熟而天真的孩子”的想象；可与此同时，他本来又确实是个天真的孩子，既有发育迟缓的一面，也有恶魔般聪明的一面。即便我对他已经相当熟悉，也无法判断那天他说的话究竟有多少是真心，又有多少只是表演。</p>
<p>火车开动后，约翰开始目不转睛地盯着猎物，盯得马格内特先生索性把自己藏到一堵报纸墙后面。过了一会儿，约翰那精确得奇怪的童声把所有人的目光都吸引过来。“马格内特先生，”他说，“我可以跟您说话吗？”报纸放了下来，主人努力让自己既不显得尴尬，也不显得居高临下。</p>
<p>“当然可以，小伙子。说吧。你叫什么？”</p>
<p>“哦，我叫约翰。我是个怪孩子，不过那无所谓。我们要谈的是您。”</p>
<p>我们全都笑了。马格内特先生在座位上动了一下，但仍努力保持那副大人物应有的姿态。</p>
<p>“嗯，”他说，“你确实是个怪孩子。”他朝几位成年同车人看了一眼，仿佛寻求确认。我们也配合地微笑。</p>
<p>“是啊，”约翰回答，“可您看，从我的角度，您也是个怪大人。”马格内特先生一时间悬在觉得好笑和感到冒犯之间；不过除了约翰以外我们都笑了，于是他决定把这当成一个有趣的玩笑，继续慈祥下去。</p>
<p>“我身上有什么值得大惊小怪的？”他说，“我不过是个商人。你为什么觉得我怪？”</p>
<p>“这个嘛，”约翰说，“大家觉得我怪，是因为我的脑子比大多数孩子好。有些人说，我的脑子好得超过了一个孩子应有的程度。您怪，是因为您的钱比大多数人多；而且——也有人说——多得超过了您应有的程度。”</p>
<p>我们又笑了一次，只是这回笑得有些紧张。</p>
<p>约翰继续说：“我还没弄明白该拿自己的脑子做什么，所以我很好奇，您有没有弄明白该拿自己的钱做什么。”</p>
<p>“我亲爱的小家伙，你可能不信，但事实是，我其实没有多少真正的选择。各种需要不断冒出来，我只好不停掏钱。”</p>
<p>“我明白了，”约翰说，“可您总不可能给一切可能的需要都掏钱。您一定得有某种大的计划或目标，帮您决定应该选哪一些。”</p>
<p>“嗯，这话该怎么说呢？我是詹姆斯·马格内特，有妻子、有家庭，还有一门相当复杂的生意，以及所有这些东西带来的整整一大堆义务。我所掌握的钱——或者几乎全部的钱——都得拿来让这些球继续滚下去，可以这么说。”</p>
<p>“我懂了。”约翰又说，“也就是黑格尔说的‘我的地位及其职责’，至于这一切究竟有什么意义，就不必操心。”</p>
<p>马格内特先生听到这句话的反应，像一条狗忽然闻到某种陌生而又颇有威胁的气味：先抽了抽鼻子，浑身警觉起来，喉咙里隐约发出低吼。</p>
<p>“操心！”他哼了一声，“我有的是事情可操心；但那都是每天实实在在的问题，比如怎么把货进得足够便宜，卖出去才能赚而不是赔。要是我开始操心什么‘这一切的意义’，生意很快就得垮掉。没那个时间。我发现自己手头正有一件国家需要有人干的大事，于是我就把它干好。”</p>
<p>沉默了一会儿，约翰说：“手上有一件真正需要完成的大事，而且还能把它做好，那一定很了不起！先生，您把它做得好吗？它真的需要做吗？不过您当然做得好，而且它一定有必要；否则国家也不会付钱给您。”</p>
<p>马格内特先生焦虑地逐个看了看同车的几个人，怀疑自己是不是正在被这孩子耍弄。不过，约翰那双天真而尊敬的眼睛让他重新安心。可孩子接下来一句话又叫他颇不自在。“觉得自己既安全又重要，一定很舒服吧。”</p>
<p>“嗯，这我可说不好。”那位大人物回答，“不过我给公众他们想要的东西，而且尽量卖得便宜；从中所得，足够让我一家人过上体面的舒适生活。”</p>
<p>“所以您挣钱，就是为了让家里人过得舒服吗？”</p>
<p>“那是一部分，还有别的。我用各种方式把钱花掉。你非想知道的话，我有相当一部分钱会给我认为最有能力治理国家的政党；还有一部分给我们这座大城市的医院和其他慈善事业。不过大头还是重新投进生意，让它变得更大、更好。”</p>
<p>“等一下，”约翰说，“您刚才一下提了好多有意思的点，我可不能漏掉。先说‘舒服’。您住在山上那幢很大的半木结构房子里，对吧？”</p>
<p>“对。那是仿伊丽莎白时代庄园建的。其实我不一定非得要它，可我妻子一心想住那样的房子。而且盖房子对本地建筑业也是件大好事。”</p>
<p>“您还有一辆劳斯莱斯和一辆沃尔斯利？”</p>
<p>“是的。”马格内特先生宽宏大量地补充道，“星期六到山上来吧，我让你坐坐劳斯莱斯。它跑到八十英里时，感觉跟三十一样。”</p>
<p>约翰的眼皮轻轻沉下去，又抬起来。我知道，这个动作表示一种带着笑意的轻蔑。可他为什么轻蔑？他自己也是个有点迷恋速度的人。比如，他从来不满意我那种谨慎开车法。难道他觉得，这句话只是一种胆怯的转移话题？访谈结束后我才知道，他其实早已经收买了马格内特家的司机，坐那辆车出去过好几次，甚至还学会了驾驶——因为腿太短够不到踏板，他就在背后垫上一堆坐垫。</p>
<p>“哦，谢谢您，我太想坐您的劳斯莱斯了。”他说，一脸感激地望着富人那双慈祥的灰眼睛。“当然，要是没有适当的舒适条件，您就不可能好好工作。而所谓适当的舒适，也就是一座大房子、两辆汽车、给妻子的皮草和珠宝、一等车票，还有孩子们上的那些体面学校。”他停了一下，马格内特先生疑惑地望着他。约翰又补充道：“不过，等您拿到爵士头衔以后，才算真正舒服吧。它怎么还没来？您已经付得够多了，不是吗？”</p>
<p>一个同车人“噗”地笑出了声。马格内特先生脸一下涨红，倒吸一口气，低声骂道：“讨厌的小混蛋！”随后重新躲到报纸后面。</p>
<p>“哦，先生，对不起。”约翰说，“我以为这完全是件体面的事。它不是跟卖罂粟花纪念章差不多吗？交了钱，拿到徽章，大家就都知道您尽了自己那一份责任。真正的舒服，不就是知道所有人都知道您是个正派人吗？”</p>
<p>报纸再次放下。主人带着温和却坚定的神情说：“听着，小伙子！别人告诉你的话不能全信，尤其是那些带诽谤性质的。你自己当然没恶意，可是——以后听到事情，要多加判断。”</p>
<p>“我真的特别抱歉。”约翰一脸痛苦和羞愧，“要弄明白什么能说、什么不能说，实在太难了。”</p>
<p>“是啊，当然。”马格内特先生和蔼地说，“也许我最好稍微给你解释一下。一个人如果处在我这样的地位，只要他还算有点用，就必须尽可能利用自己的机会为帝国服务。做到这一点，一部分靠把生意经营好，一部分靠个人影响力。而要产生影响，他不仅必须真正是一个有分量的人，还必须看起来像一个有分量的人。因此，他不得不花不少钱，维持某种生活排场。公众确实会更认真听取一个生活略显昂贵的人说话，而不是一个完全不讲排场的人。事实上，很多时候不这么过反而会更舒服。就像炎热天气里，一个法官在法庭上不穿法袍、不戴假发当然更舒服，可他不能那样。他必须牺牲舒适，维护尊严。去年圣诞节，我给妻子买了一条相当不错的钻石项链——南非产的，所以钱仍留在帝国内。每逢参加重要场合，比如市政厅宴会，她就必须戴上。她并不总愿意，说什么太重、硌得慌之类。我就对她说：‘亲爱的，这是表明你有分量的标志，是职务徽章。最好戴着。’至于爵士头衔，如果有人说我想花钱买一个，那纯粹是卑鄙的谎话。我尽自己所能资助我的政党，是因为凭我的经验，我非常清楚，那是一个代表常识和忠诚的政党。没有别的政党真正关心英国的繁荣和力量，也没有别的政党真正关心我们伟大的帝国，以及它领导世界的使命。既然如此，我当然必须用一切可行方式支持它。如果他们认为应该授予我爵士头衔，我会感到骄傲。我可不是那种装腔作势、对这种荣誉嗤之以鼻的人。我会高兴，一方面因为那意味着真正有分量的人确认我确实在为帝国服务，另一方面因为爵士头衔也会给我更多分量，让我继续为帝国服务。”</p>
<p>马格内特先生瞥了同车人一眼。我们都点头表示赞同。“谢谢您，先生。”约翰用庄重、尊敬的眼神说道。“所以这一切归根到底都离不开钱，对吧？如果我将来要做什么大事，无论如何都得弄到钱。我认识一个人，总说‘钱就是力量’。他有个妻子，老是又累又烦，还有五个孩子，长得难看，又都不聪明。他失业了，前几天还不得不把自行车卖掉。他说，自己落到这种地步，而——您却处在这种地位，很不公平。不过，其实全是他自己的错。要是他像您这么机灵、这么清醒，他就会像您一样富有。您有钱又不会让别人因此变穷，对吧？要是贫民窟里的人都跟您一样机灵，他们也都会有大房子、劳斯莱斯和钻石。他们就全都能对帝国有用，而不是只会成为负担。”</p>
<p>坐在我对面的男人窃笑起来。马格内特先生像一匹胆小的马那样斜眼瞥了他一下，随后振作起来，也笑了。</p>
<p>“小伙子，”他说，“你年纪太小，还不懂这些事情。我想我们再谈下去也不会有什么好处。”</p>
<p>“对不起。”约翰回答，看起来像受了沉重打击。“我还以为自己懂呢。”停了一会儿，他继续说：“您介不介意再说一小会儿？我还想问另一件事。”</p>
<p>“哦，好吧。什么事？”</p>
<p>“您平时都想些什么？”</p>
<p>“我想些什么？老天啊，孩子！各种事情。我的生意、我的家、我的妻子和孩子，还有——国家的状况。”</p>
<p>“国家的状况？您会想它什么？”</p>
<p>“这个嘛，”马格内特先生说，“那可说来话长。我会想，英国该怎么恢复对外贸易——这样才会有更多钱流进国家，人们也能过得更幸福、更充实。我会想，我们该怎样帮助政府，对付那些想煽动骚乱的蠢人，以及那些肆意攻击帝国的人。我还会想——”</p>
<p>约翰在这里打断了他：“什么东西能让生活充实又幸福？”</p>
<p>“你简直是个问题匣子！依我看，人要幸福，就得有足够多的工作，让他们没空惹事；也得有一点娱乐，让他们保持精神。”</p>
<p>“当然，”约翰插嘴道，“还得有足够的钱买娱乐。”</p>
<p>“对。”马格内特先生说，“不过不能太多。他们大多数人只会把钱浪费掉，或者拿它伤害自己。而且要是手里已经有很多钱，就不会再为了多挣一点而工作。”</p>
<p>“可您有很多钱，您还是工作。”</p>
<p>“对，但严格说来，我工作并不是为了钱。我工作，是因为我的生意本身是一场令人着迷的游戏，而且国家也需要它。我把自己看作某种公仆。”</p>
<p>“可是，”约翰说，“他们不也是公仆吗？他们的工作不也同样必要吗？”</p>
<p>“是的，孩子。不过他们通常不这样看。没人逼着，他们就不肯干活。”</p>
<p>“哦，我懂了！”约翰说，“他们跟您不是一种人。做您一定特别美妙。我真想知道，我以后会变成您这样，还是变成他们那样。”</p>
<p>“哦，我其实也没什么不同。”马格内特先生宽厚地说，“就算有区别，也只是环境把我塑造成这样。至于你，小伙子，我看你将来一定会走得很远。”</p>
<p>“我特别想走得远，”约翰说，“只是还不知道该往哪个方向。显然，无论做什么，我都必须有钱。不过告诉我，您为什么要为国家操心，又为什么要为别的人操心？”</p>
<p>“我想，”马格内特先生笑着说，“我为别人操心，是因为看见他们不幸福，我自己也会觉得不舒服。还有，”他语气庄重起来，“《圣经》告诉我们要爱自己的邻人。至于为什么为国家操心，我想一部分原因是，我必须有某种足够宏大的东西可以关心——某种比我自己更大的东西。”</p>
<p>“可您自己就很大啊。”约翰说，眼里全是英雄崇拜，连一点戏谑的闪光都没有。</p>
<p>马格内特先生连忙说：“不，不。我不过是为一个极其伟大的东西服务的一件卑微工具。”</p>
<p>“您说的是什么东西？”约翰问。</p>
<p>“当然是我们伟大的帝国啊，孩子。”</p>
<p>我们已经快到站了。马格内特先生起身，从行李架上取下帽子。“好了，小伙子，”他说，“我们聊得很有意思。星期六下午两点半左右来吧，我让司机开劳斯莱斯带你兜一刻钟。”</p>
<p>“谢谢您，先生！”约翰说，“我还能看看马格内特太太的项链吗？我很喜欢珠宝。”</p>
<p>“当然可以。”马格内特先生回答。</p>
<p>出了车站，我重新同约翰会合。他对这趟旅程唯一的评论，就是他那标志性的笑声。</p>

<h3><span id="a334"></span>CHAPTER IV. JOHN AND HIS ELDERS</h3>
<p><span class="t28">T</span><span class="t31">HOUGH</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">THE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">FIGHT</span><span class="t30"> </span>with Stephen was, I believe, one of the chief landmarks in John’s life, outwardly things went on much as before; save that he gave up fighting, and spent a good deal more time by himself.</p>
<p>Between him and Stephen, friendship was restored, but it was henceforth an uncomfortable friendship. Each seemed anxious to be amicable, but neither felt at ease with the other. Stephen’s nerve, I think, had been seriously shaken. It was not that he feared another licking, but that his self-respect had suffered. I took an opportunity to suggest that his defeat had been no disgrace, since John was clearly no ordinary child. Stephen jumped at this consolation. With a hysterical jerk in his voice he said, “I felt — I can’t say what I felt — like a dog biting its master and being punished. I felt — sort of guilty, wicked.”</p>
<p>John, I think, was now beginning to realize more clearly the gulf that separated him from the rest of us. At the same time, he was probably feeling a keen need for companionship, but companionship of a calibre beyond that of normal human beings. He continued to play with his old companions, and was indeed still the moving spirit in most of their activities; but always he played with a certain aloofness, as it were with his tongue in his cheek. Though in appearance he was by far the smallest and most infantile of the whole gang, he reminded me sometimes of a little old man with snowy hair condescending to play with young gorillas. Often he would break away in the middle of some wild game and drift into the garden to lie dreaming on the lawn. Or he would hang around his mother and discuss life with her, while she did her house-work, tidied the garden, or (a common occupation with Pax) just waited for the next thing to happen.</p>
<p>In some ways John with his mother suggested a human foundling with a wolf foster-mother; or, better, a cow foster-mother. He obviously gave her complete trust and affection, and even a deep though perplexed reverence; but he was troubled when she could not follow his thought or understand his innumerable questions about the universe.</p>
<p>The foster-mother image is not perfect. In one respect, indeed, it is entirely false. For though intellectually Pax was by far his inferior, there was evidently another field in which she was at this time his equal, perhaps even his superior. Both mother and son had a peculiar knack of appreciating experience, a peculiar relish which was at bottom, I believe, simply a very special and subtle sense of humour. Often have I seen a covert glance of understanding and amusement pass between them when the rest of us found nothing to tickle us. I guessed that this veiled merriment was in some way connected with John’s awakening interest in persons and his rapidly developing insight into his own motives. But what it was in our behaviour that these two found so piquant, I could never discover.</p>
<p>With his father John’s relation was very different. He made good use of the doctor’s active mind, but between them there was no spontaneous sympathy, and little community of taste save intellectual interest. I have often seen on John’s face while he was listening to his father a fleeting contortion of ridicule, even disgust. This happened especially at times when Thomas believed himself to be giving the boy some profound comment on human nature or the universe. Needless to say it was not only Thomas, but myself also and many another that roused in John this ridicule or revulsion. But Thomas was the chief offender, perhaps because he was the most brilliant, and the most impressive example of the mental limitations of his species. I suspect that John often deliberately incited his father to betray himself in this manner. It was as though the boy had said to himself, “I have somehow to understand these fantastic beings who occupy the planet. Here is a fine specimen. I must experiment on him.”</p>
<p>At this point I had better say that I myself was becoming increasingly intrigued by the fantastic being, John. I was also unwittingly coming under his influence. Looking back on this period, I can see that he had already marked me down for future use, and was undertaking the first steps of my capture. His chief method was the cool assumption that though I was a middle-aged man, I was his slave; that however much I might laugh at him and scold him, I secretly recognized him as a superior being, and was at heart his faithful hound. For the present I might amuse myself playing at an independent life (I was at this time a rather half-hearted free-lance journalist), but sooner or later I must come to heel.</p>
<p>When John was nearly eight and a half in actual years, he was as a rule taken for a very peculiar child of five or six. He still played childish games, and was accepted by other children as a child, though a bit of a freak. Yet he could take part in any adult conversation. Of course, he was always either far too brilliant or far too ignorant of life to play his part in anything like a normal manner; but he was never simply inferior. Even his most naïve remarks were apt to have a startling significance.</p>
<p>But John’s naïvety was rapidly disappearing. He was now reading an immense amount at an incredible rate. No book, on any subject which did not lie outside his experience, took him more than a couple of hours to master, however tough its matter. Most he could assimilate thoroughly in a quarter of an hour. But the majority of books he glanced at only for a few moments, then flung aside as worthless.</p>
<p>Now and then, in the course of his reading, he would demand to be taken (by his father or mother or myself) to watch some process of manufacture, or to go down a mine, or see over a ship, or visit some place of historic interest, or to observe experiments in some laboratory. Great efforts were made to fulfil these demands, but in many cases we had not the necessary influence. Many projected trips, moreover, were prevented by Pax’s dread of unnecessary publicity for the boy. Whenever we did undertake an expedition, we had to pretend to the authorities that John’s presence was accidental, and his interest childish and unintelligent.</p>
<p>John was by no means dependent on his elders for seeing the world. He had developed a habit of entering into conversation with all kinds of persons, “to find out what they were doing and what they thought about things.” Any one who was tactfully accosted in street or train or country road by this small boy with huge eyes, hair like lamb’s wool, and adult speech was likely to find himself led on to say much more than he intended. By such novel research John learned, I am convinced, more about human nature and our modern social problems in a month or two than most of us learn in a lifetime.</p>
<p>I was privileged to witness one of these interviews. On this occasion the subject was the proprietor of a big general store in the neighbouring industrial city. Mr. Magnate (it is safer not to reveal his name) was to be accosted while he was travelling to business by the 9:30 train. John consented to my presence, but only on condition that I should pretend to be a stranger.</p>
<p>We let the quarry pass through the turnstile and settle himself in his first-class compartment. Then we went to the booking office, where I rather self-consciously demanded “a first single and a half.” Independently we strayed into Mr. Magnate’s carriage. When I arrived, John was already settled in the corner opposite to the great man, who occasionally glanced from his paper at the queer child with a cliff for brow and caves for eyes. Soon after I had taken my post, in the corner diagonally opposite to John, two other business men entered, and settled themselves to read their papers.</p>
<p>John was apparently deep in Comic Cuts, or some such periodical. Though this had been bought merely to serve as stage property, I believe he was quite capable of enjoying it; for at this time, in spite of his wonderful gifts, he was still at heart “the little vulgar boy”. In the conversation which followed he was obviously to some extent playing up to the business man’s idea of a precocious yet naïve child. But also he was a naïve child, backward as well as diabolically intelligent. I myself, though I knew him well, could not decide how much of his talk on this occasion was sincere, and how much mere acting.</p>
<p>When the train had started, John began to watch his prey so intently that Mr. Magnate took cover behind a wall of newspaper. Presently John’s curiously precise treble gathered all eyes upon him. “Mr. Magnate,” he said, “may I talk to you?” The newspaper was lowered, and its owner endeavoured to look neither awkward nor condescending.</p>
<p>“Certainly, boy, go ahead. What’s your name?”</p>
<p>“Oh, my name’s John. I’m a queer child, but that doesn’t matter. It’s you we’re going to talk about.”</p>
<p>We all laughed. Mr. Magnate shifted in his seat, but continued to look his part.</p>
<p>“Well,” he said, “you certainly are a queer child.” He glanced at his adult fellow travellers for confirmation. We duly smiled.</p>
<p>“Yes,” replied John, “but you see from my point of view you are a queer man.” Mr. Magnate hung for a moment between amusement and annoyance; but since we had all laughed, except John, he chose to be tickled and benevolent.</p>
<p>“Surely,” he said, “there’s nothing remarkable about me. I’m just a business man. Why do you think I’m queer?”</p>
<p>“Well,” said John, “I’m thought queer because I have more brains than most children. Some say I have more brains than I ought to have. You’re queer because you have more money than most people; and (some say) more than you ought to have.”</p>
<p>Once more we laughed, rather anxiously.</p>
<p>John continued: “I haven’t found out yet what to do with my brains, and I’m wondering if you have found out what to do with your money.”</p>
<p>“My dear boy, you may not believe me, but the fact is I have no real choice. Needs of all sorts keep cropping up, and I have to fork out.”</p>
<p>“I see,” said John; “but then you can’t fork out for all the possible needs. You must have some sort of big plan or aim to help you to choose.”</p>
<p>“Well now, how shall I put it? I’m James Magnate, with a wife and family and a rather complicated business and a whole lot of obligations rising out of all that. All the money I control, or nearly all, goes in keeping all those balls rolling, so to speak.”</p>
<p>“I see,” said John again. “ ’My station and its duties,’ as Hegel said, and no need to worry about the sense of it all.”</p>
<p>Like a dog encountering an unfamiliar and rather formidable smell, Mr. Magnate sniffed this remark, bristled, and vaguely growled.</p>
<p>“Worry!” he snorted. “There’s plenty of that; but it’s practical day-to-day worry about how to get goods cheap enough to sell them at a profit instead of a loss. If I started worrying about ‘the sense of it all’ the business would soon go to pieces. No time for that. I find myself with a pretty big job that the country needs doing, and I just do it.”</p>
<p>There was a pause, then John remarked, “How splendid it must be to have a pretty big job that needs doing, and to do it well! Do you do it well, sir? And does it really need doing? But of course you do, and it must; else the country wouldn’t pay you for it.”</p>
<p>Mr. Magnate looked anxiously at all his fellow travellers in turn, wondering whether his leg was being pulled. He was reassured, however, by John’s innocent and respectful gaze. The boy’s next remark was rather disconcerting. “It must be so snug to feel both safe and important.”</p>
<p>“Well, I don’t know about that,” the great man replied. “But I give the public what it wants, and as cheaply as I can, and I get enough out of it to keep my family in reasonable comfort.”</p>
<p>“Is that what you make money for, to keep your family in comfort?”</p>
<p>“That and other things. I get rid of my money in all sorts of ways. If you must know, quite a lot goes to the political party that I think can govern the country best. Some goes to hospitals and other charities in our great city. But most goes into the business itself to make it bigger and better.”</p>
<p>“Wait a minute,” said John. “You’ve raised a lot of interesting points. I mustn’t lose any of them. First, about comfort. You live in that big half-timbered house on the hill, don’t you?”</p>
<p>“Yes. It’s a copy of an Elizabethan mansion. I could have done without it, but my wife had set her heart on it. And putting it up was a great thing for the local building trade.”</p>
<p>“And you have a Rolls, and a Wolseley?”</p>
<p>“Yes,” said the Magnate, adding with magnanimity, “Come up the hill on Saturday and I’ll give you a run in the Rolls. When she’s doing eighty it feels like thirty.”</p>
<p>John’s eyelids sank and rose again, a movement which I knew as an expression of amused contempt. But why was he contemptuous? He was a bit of a speed-hog himself. Never, for instance, was he satisfied with my cautious driving. Was it that he saw in this remark a cowardly attempt to side-track the conversation? After the interview I learned that he had already made several trips in the Magnate car, having suborned the chauffeur. He had even learned to drive it, with cushions behind him, so as to help his short legs to reach the pedals.</p>
<p>“Oh, thank you, I should love to go in your Rolls,” he said, looking gratefully into the benevolent grey eyes of the rich man. “Of course, you couldn’t work properly unless you had reasonable comfort. And that means a big house and two cars, and furs and jewels for your wife, and first-class railway fares, and swank schools for your children.” He paused, while Mr. Magnate looked suspiciously at him. Then he added, “But you won’t be really comfortable till you’ve got that knighthood. Why doesn’t it come? You’ve paid enough already, haven’t you?”</p>
<p>One of our fellow passengers sniggered. Mr. Magnate coloured, gasped, muttered, “Offensive little brat!” and retired behind his paper.</p>
<p>“Oh, sir, I’m sorry,” said John, “I thought it was all quite respectable. Surely it’s just like Poppy Day. Pay your money, and you get your badge, and everyone knows you have done your bit. And that’s true comfort, to know that everyone knows you’re all right.”</p>
<p>The paper dropped again, and its owner said, with mild firmness, “Look here, young man! You mustn’t believe everything you’re told, specially when it’s libellous. I know you don’t mean harm yourself, but — be more critical of what you hear.”</p>
<p>“I’m frightfully sorry,” said John, looking pained and abashed. “It’s so hard to know what one may say and what not.”</p>
<p>“Yes, of course,” said Magnate amiably. “Perhaps I had better explain things a bit. Any one who finds himself in a position like mine, if he’s worth his salt, has to make the best possible use of his opportunities for serving the Empire. Now he can do this partly by running his business well, partly by personal influence. And if he is to have influence he must not only be, but also appear, a man of weight. He must spend a good deal on keeping up a certain style in his way of life. The public does attend more to a man who lives a bit expensively than to a man who doesn’t. Often it would be more comfortable not to live expensively. Just as it would be more comfortable for a judge in court on a hot day to do without his robe and wig. But he mustn’t. He must sacrifice comfort to dignity. At Christmas I bought my wife a rather good diamond necklace (South African — the money stayed in the Empire). Whenever we go to an important function, say a dinner at the Town Hall, she’s got to wear it. She doesn’t always want to. Says it’s heavy or hard, or something. But I say, ‘My dear, it’s a sign that you count. It’s a badge of office. Better wear it.’ And about the knighthood. If anyone says I want to buy one, it’s just a mean lie. I give what I can to my party because I know quite well, with my experience, that it’s the party of common sense and loyalty. No other party cares seriously for British prosperity and power. No other cares about our great Empire and its mission to lead the world. Well, clearly I must support that party in any way open to me. If they saw fit to give me a knighthood, I’d be proud. I’m not one of those prigs who turn up their noses at it. I’d be glad, partly because it would mean that the people who really count were sure I was really serving the Empire, partly because the knighthood would give me more weight to go on serving the Empire with.”</p>
<p>Mr. Magnate glanced at his fellow passengers. We all nodded approval. “Thank you, sir,” said John, with solemn, respectful eyes. “And it all depends on money, doesn’t it? If I’m going to do anything big, I must get money, somehow. I have a friend who keeps saying, ‘Money’s power.’ He has a wife who’s always tired and cross, and five children, ugly dull things. He’s out of a job. Had to sell his push-bike the other day. He says it’s not fair that he should be where he is and — you where you are. But it’s all his own fault really. If he had been as wide awake as you, he’d be as rich as you. Your being rich doesn’t make anyone else poor, does it? If all the slum people were as wide awake as you, they’d all have big houses and Rollses and diamonds. They’d all be some use to the Empire, instead of being just a nuisance.”</p>
<p>The man opposite me tittered. Mr. Magnate looked at him with the sidelong glance of a shy horse, then pulled himself together and laughed.</p>
<p>“My lad,” he said, “you’re too young to understand these things. I don’t think we shall do much good by talking any more about them.”</p>
<p>“I’m sorry,” John replied, seemingly crushed. “I thought I did understand.” Then after a pause he continued: “Do you mind if we go on just a little bit longer? I want to ask you something else.”</p>
<p>“Oh, very well, what is it?”</p>
<p>“What do you think about?”</p>
<p>“What do I think about? Good heavens, boy! All sorts of things. My business, my home, my wife and children, and — about the state of the country.”</p>
<p>“The state of the country? What about it?”</p>
<p>“Well,” said Mr. Magnate, “that’s much too long a story. I think about how England is to recover her foreign trade — so that more money may come into the country, and people may live happier, fuller lives. I think about how we can strengthen the hands of the Government against the foolish people who want to stir up trouble, and those who talk wildly against the Empire. I think — —”</p>
<p>Here John interrupted. “What makes life full and happy?”</p>
<p>“You are a box of questions! I should say that for happiness people need plenty of work to keep them out of mischief, and some amusement to keep them fresh.”</p>
<p>“And, of course,” John interposed, “enough money to buy their amusements with.”</p>
<p>“Yes,” said Mr. Magnate. “But not too much. Most of them would only waste it or damage themselves with it. And if they had a lot, they wouldn’t work to get more.”</p>
<p>“But you have a lot, and you work.”</p>
<p>“Yes, but I don’t work for money exactly. I work because my business is a fascinating game, and because it is necessary to the country. I regard myself as a sort of public servant.”</p>
<p>“But,” said John, “aren’t they public servants too? Isn’t their work necessary too?”</p>
<p>“Yes, boy. But they don’t as a rule look at it that way. They won’t work unless they’re driven.”</p>
<p>“Oh, I see!” John said. “They’re a different sort from you. It must be wonderful to be you. I wonder whether I shall turn out like you or like them.”</p>
<p>“Oh, I’m not really different,” said Mr. Magnate generously. “Or if I am, it’s just circumstances that have made me so. As for you, young man, I expect you’ll go a long way.”</p>
<p>“I want to, terribly,” said John. “But I don’t know which way yet. Evidently whatever I do I must have money. But tell me, why do you bother about the country, and about other people?”</p>
<p>“I suppose,” said Mr. Magnate, laughing, “I bother about other people because when I see them unhappy I feel unhappy myself. And also,” he added more solemnly, “because the Bible tells us to love our neighbours. And I suppose I bother about the country partly because I must have something big to be interested in, something bigger than myself.”</p>
<p>“But you are big, yourself,” said John, with hero-worship in his eyes, and not a twinkle.</p>
<p>Mr. Magnate said hastily, “No, no, only a humble instrument in the service of a very big thing.”</p>
<p>“What thing do you mean?” asked John.</p>
<p>“Our great Empire, of course, boy.”</p>
<p>We were arriving at our destination. Mr. Magnate rose and took his hat from the rack. “Well, young man,” he said, “we have had an interesting talk. Come along on Saturday afternoon about 2:30, and we’ll get the chauffeur to give you a quarter of an hour’s spin in the Rolls.”</p>
<p>“Thank you, sir!” said John. “And may I see Mrs. Magnate’s necklace? I love jewels.”</p>
<p>“Certainly you shall,” Mr. Magnate answered.</p>
<p>When I had met John again outside the station, his only comment on the journey was his characteristic laugh.</p>
