<h3>六、根本价值</h3>
<p>如果我们试着回答“人是什么？”这个问题，也许就能对上述问题形成更准确的看法——而这确实与一个星际学会有关。</p>
<p>显然，不论人还是什么，他至少是——或者拥有——一具身体；那是由质子、电子等等构成的物理系统或电磁系统。J. B. S. 霍尔丹——如果我没记错——曾在某处说，人类身体包含若干百万个百万个百万个百万个氢原子，以及相应数量的其他元素。朱利安·赫胥黎——同样如果我没记错——指出，人体的尺度大致处在电子和整个宇宙之间的中点。从天文学尺度看，人类非常渺小，是漂浮在巨大真空中的一粒微小行星尘埃上，一个不可想象地微小的寄生物；而这巨大真空中只极为稀疏地点缀着庞大的太阳。大小本身并不重要；但是，如果在如此微小的人体之中，物质能够成为某种程度心灵或精神的载体，那么整个由恒星系统构成的宇宙，其心灵和精神容量又可能有多大！</p>
<p>从生物学上看，人类——正如我们已经提到的，更准确地说，是我们这个可笑地自称“智人”（Homo sapiens）的物种——是非常晚近才出现的。但它又是地球生物中发展程度最高的。也就是说，客观来看，它最复杂、整合程度最高、适应能力最广。它的专门化之处，就是“不专门化”。正因如此，人类具有非凡的适应性，并取得了对其他所有物种的支配地位。人类确实比任何其他物种都在这个星球上留下了更大的印记，而且这种印记远为复杂、有目的、有组织。从根本上说，人仍不过是一种哺乳动物，一种灵长类。人独特的禀赋，是相对高的智力；这种智力依赖手、眼和大脑皮层的独特发展。然而，我们有充分理由相信，人类仍是一种极不完善的物种。鸟类在适合自己的生活方式——在地球大气中飞翔——方面已经近乎完善；人类虽然拥有远远超出鸟类范围的能力，却还没有把这些能力完善起来。在适合自己的媒介中，也就是智能而有目的的行动、抽象思维、人格感受以及艺术创造方面，人类还不过是一只笨拙的会飞蜥蜴。</p>
<p>从心理学上说，是什么把人类同低于人类的动物、甚至同最接近我们的猿类区分开来？我们拥有同样的器官基本结构、反射、先天行为和情绪反应。人也会恐惧、攻击、自我关注、产生性冲动、结群、好奇，并且爱动手摆弄周围事物，等等。人类真正独特之处，是更强的辨别能力，也就是注意相同与不同的能力。由此产生人类天生的智力——我所谓智力，是解决新问题的能力，而这些问题无法靠本能或既成习惯解决。这种独特的辨别与智能能力，为人类打开了巨大的经验新宇宙，是地球其他生物无法进入的。所有智力都包括注意相关因素、忽略无关因素的能力。由此在人类身上产生抽象能力：我们可以把注意集中于某一特征，把它与其他具有同一特征的实例联系起来，并给这种同一性起一个名字，例如“红”“二”“愉快”。语言由此产生，抽象思维的全部世界以及具体诗歌的全部世界也由此产生。</p>
<p>人类高度集中的注意能力不仅施加于环境，也施加于自身本性。因此，人类发展出了独特的自我意识以及对其他自我的意识。事实上，人类获得了成为“人格”的能力，也获得了真正人格关系的能力，其中包括各种发展成熟的爱与恨。由此又产生一种独特的人类式、或者说人格式社会性。在低于人类的动物中，社会性不过是群居冲动——其中包括向群体自我贬抑——与自我伸张冲动的结合，后者最高表现为取得群体支配地位。当然，人类自己也经常以这种低于人的方式行动；但真正独特的人类式或人格式关系属于另一种性质，因为它包含对另一个有意识人格的尊重。要理解真正独特的人类社会，最好从真正的人类之爱出发；这种爱同样超出了低级动物意识的范围。</p>
<p>当约翰真正爱简时，他做的是任何野兽都做不到的事。他不只是对她产生一种令人愉快的温暖感受。当然，这种感受也有，但还有更多。他意识到，她是一个不同于自己的有意识存在，拥有不同的性格、需要和能力。他接受她本来的样子，而不是希望把自己的意志强加给她。他尊重她作为人格的地位，对她承担责任，珍爱她，依赖她；因为认识她和爱她，他的心灵得到丰富，同时也被她深刻塑造。两个人共同形成一个由不同存在构成的小社会，以相互之爱结合在一起；他们不仅珍爱彼此，也珍爱他们共同构成的那个“我们”。当然，他们仍然是关心自身利益的个体主义者，各自利益也常常冲突。一切爱里都掺杂着恨。但在真正的爱中，这种冲突在很大程度上被超越，因为双方都愿意相互调适、相互依赖。</p>
<p>也许我应该为离其他行星越扯越远道歉。但请相信，这段离题与我的主题极其相关。如果要讨论人类应该拿其他行星怎么办，就必须先说明自己认为人类应该拿自己怎么办。这正是我试图做的事情。我们无法避开一定程度的根本性思考；这里不是去建立什么宇宙理论，而是努力清楚地看见人类经验本身。</p>
<p>好，让我们继续这个尝试。从爱情、友谊或个人接触领域中某种真正共同体的经验出发，个体可以进一步上升为这样一种意志：在同其他人类的一切交往中，无论接触多么轻微，都追求真正的共同体关系。由此产生普遍基督教之爱或“人类兄弟情谊”的理想。几千年来，这个理想一直萦绕着人类。它以各种形式，在世界上一切伟大宗教中都发挥过重要作用。在基督教里，爱甚至被神化。我们被告知，上帝在某种隐秘意义上就是“爱”；圣灵就是爱；基督徒还相信，爱是创造整个宇宙的根本力量。相信这样的命题，在我看来已经远远超出了人类思想的正当范围；但过去许多最优秀的心灵确实相信它，这个事实也必须认真看待。</p>
<p>为什么爱、善意、伙伴关系、人格之间真正的共同体，也就是那种真正独特的人类式——或者更准确说人格式——社会关系，会受到如此高度赞扬？显然，它具有生存价值。如果你下定决心，要把人类最发达的经验与行为完全解释成原始冲动经社会条件塑造的结果，那么确实可以提出一套颇为像样的解释。但这种解释没有触及根部。认为最发达、最清醒的人类经验和行为可以完全从它们的原始来源来解释，不过是一种教条。这样做，恰恰漏掉了人类最独特的东西：人在一定程度上能够站到自身之外，对自身以外的事物因为它们本身而产生兴趣。真正恋爱过的人应该明白这一点；有过真正宗教感受的人应当更加明白。东西方伟大的圣徒们所关心的——在我看来——是人类能力所能达到最高层次的经验；不能只用原始冲动充分描述这些经验。同时我还要补充：我们也不应该不加批判地接受圣徒们自己对这些经验的解释，因为那些解释采用的是他们那个时代关于宇宙根本性质的观念。无论当时还是今天，人类语言和人类思想本身都还没有发展到能够可靠陈述那些最充分“醒来”的经验。不过我仍认为，这些经验对于任何关心怎样恰当安排人类生活的人都极其重要。有人也许会抗议：不管这些经验对圣徒多么重要，对我们都没有意义，因为我们并不分享它们。这个反对值得回答。圣徒的宗教经验无疑具有特别鲜明、特别不可抗拒的形式，大多数人无法达到；但普通男女——至少其中更敏感的人——只要肯稍微训练一下自己，把注意力集中到自己与他人、自己与宇宙之间实际感受到的关系上，也完全能够在自身经验中发现伟大神秘主义者试图描述的那种东西。</p>
<p>找不到更好的词，我将这种最有意义的经验称为“精神经验”，或“精神的经验”。不过我必须立即补充：“精神”这个词不必理解为某种不同于物质的东西或实体，而物质又被设想成另一种东西或实体。更不应该把“精神”理解为一个神圣人格或上帝。我所谓“精神”，只是某一种特定的体验和行动方式。事实上，就是基督徒有时所谓“精神的生活”或“精神之道”，也是中国圣贤简单称作“道”（Tao，the Way）的那种生活。至于精神生活究竟是什么样，我马上会讨论，并试着用现代语言、联系现代经验来描述它。</p>
<p>同时，我想提出：无论人们发明怎样的理论来证明这种生活方式为什么善、为什么正确，在他们的直接经验中，真正向他们呈现的，都只是“道”本身被看见为最高的正当。无论精神在宇宙中究竟处于什么地位，真正重要的只是精神本身。圣徒和圣贤渴望精神在自己以及他人的生活中得到充分表达。他们渴望超越——或者从——单纯自利的生活中醒来。他们寻求更广阔的视野。他们通过克己，寻求真正摆脱自我，以获得更广阔、更深、更具穿透力的视野。通过约束那个普通、自利、世俗的自我，他们试图“醒来”，采用一种假设中的普遍自我的立场。有时他们会说服自己，相信自己真的在神秘联合中被吸收进那个最高自我；即便我们对此、乃至对此普遍自我的存在都非常怀疑，仍必须承认，他们追求的是超越纯粹自我关切的局限，是通过关心其他个体乃至整个宇宙而忘却自己。事实上，他们的普通经验中闯入了一种关于“精神”的异象：精神作为一种理想生活方式，并在某种意义上就是“我们为何存在”，向他们提出了效忠要求。</p>
<p>那么，精神生活究竟是什么样的生活？如果先撇开宗教和形而上学关于宇宙及根本实在的知识性装饰，同时考虑古人没有掌握的现代知识，我认为可以用现代语言表达问题的核心。下面这个极简短的说明不会令古代导师或他们的现代追随者满意，正因为它把那些形而上学教义全部删去了；但在我看来，只要那些古代经验对普通人类具有任何意义，这个说明确实抓住了其核心。</p>
<p>我必须先说几句人格的性质，因为精神之道本质上是人格存在者的道路。某个东西意识着某个东西。“我”——无论这个“我”究竟是什么——意识到自己处在物理环境中的身体，也意识到自己的“自我”或心理人格——一个具有特定性格、需要和能力的生物——还意识到其他人格、人格组成的社会，以及物理定律和道德原则这类抽象事物；此外，我还以非常粗略的方式意识到一个自己身处其中的、完整的客观宇宙。我们对这些事物的意识当然很混乱，也充满错误，但我们都默认：确实有某种东西可以被意识，而它独立于我们的体验。换句话说，存在一个客观宇宙。</p>
<p>可以说，客观性的最内一圈，就是我的身体以及它同物理环境的关系。我把身体体验为一个持续运转的整体，一个动态存在，它趋向某些行动，并从环境中需要某些东西。我有意识地“认同”身体的需要，希望这些需要得到满足。例如，当身体需要食物时，我渴望进食；睡眠、性活动等需要也是如此。在婴儿期，被体验到的只有这一最内圈客观性。但随着儿童成长，他逐渐意识到第二圈客观性，也就是有意识的人格。他开始具有自我意识，也意识到他人。他“认同”自己心理人格的需要，也会——虽然不那么持续——认同少数其他人格的需要，例如母亲。在真正的爱中，我们会“认同”另一个人格，与他或她形成一种心理共生。重要的是：另一个人以及我自己的自我——连同其特定性格、需要和能力——对于意识它们的“我”而言，都是客观事实。当然，我对它们的认识可能在很大程度上错误，就像我会误解任何其他客观事实一样；但它们不是幻想，而就是它们本来的样子。</p>
<p>在与我有直接个人接触的人格圈之外，还可以放置另一圈客观性：一切人格社会，以及尚在形成中的整个人类社会，都出现在这一圈。再往外，是抽象观念的圈，其中既有真观念也有假观念；两者对于正在体验的“我”而言同样都是客观对象。归根到底，真观念就是被心灵把握、并从特定情境中抽象出来的客观特征。“狗”这个观念，就是所有狗共同拥有的区别性特征。当然，一切观念都部分包含错误，只是有的更多、有的更少。但即使观念中的错误成分，也扎根在客观性里。可以说，它们是把客观特征“斜眼重叠”成错误图案的结果，就像醉汉可能看见两个月亮而不是一个。他其实是把同一个月亮看了两遍，或看在两个位置上。再想想“半人马”这个概念：半马半人。世上没有这种动物；但“半人马”这个观念，是把半个人和大部分一匹马“斜眼重叠”到一起，从而形成新的抽象观念。</p>
<p>而一切抽象中最重要的，是精神，也就是理想的生活方式。它是一种理想；这种理想蕴含在我们实际体验到的客观事物性质之中，并从中涌现——就像落体的行为以及行星恒星的运动中，也能涌现出可被把握的客观规律一样。</p>
<p>同样，“善”“恶”以及一切可能类型的价值观念，都源自客观性的某一区域对有意识个体所产生的作用。纯粹抽象的主体性，不过是一种没有特征的“可以意识某物”的可能性，其中也包括意识价值。所有价值都产生于有意识地“认同”客观需要。有些是自身身体的需要，有些则是人格——包括我自己和其他自我——更微妙的需要。</p>
<p>还要注意，人格本身就包含共同体。若不存在同其他人格的真正共同体关系，也就不存在真正的人格。我所谓共同体，不是那种每个个体都力图利用别人来促进自己利益的关系，而是独特的人类社会关系：不同个体以相互尊重彼此作为人格而结合起来。精神之道，本质上就是人格在同其他人格构成的共同体中所走的道路。用基督徒的话说，它是一个“教会”应走的道路，而不是孤立个体追求个人主义救赎的道路；用共产主义者的话说，它是同志们献身于共同事业时所走的道路。</p>
<p>我认为，精神本质上就是这样一种生活方式：我们努力对客观宇宙获得充分、全面而真实的认识，并对它产生恰当感受、作出恰当而具有创造性的行动。当然，宇宙也包括作为客体的自己——一个处在其他自我之间的自我。在精神生活中，人努力以敏感而智能的方式认识宇宙中的事物——包括人格——并认识作为整体的宇宙；同时也努力对事物、对人、对整个宇宙产生恰当的感受，不偏袒作为个体的自己，不偏袒自己的家庭、民族，甚至不偏袒整个人类物种。最后，人还努力针对这一切采取恰当而创造性的行动。</p>
<p>精神作为最高善、并在某种意义上作为“我们为何存在”的经验，并不需要形而上学论证或宗教教义来支撑。对于相对清醒的心灵——更准确地说，对于没有被权力这类次要之善所迷住、也没有被唯物主义或有神论这类特定理论弄糊涂的清醒心灵——它就这样作为一个直接事实“被给予”。永远不要忘记：如果有神论是人类智力脆弱的发明，那么唯物主义也是。所有这类野心勃勃的理论，几乎肯定都是错误多于正确。任何这样的形而上学思辨，无论多么有趣、在教育上多么有价值，都极不可靠；无论它们试图支持还是否定精神经验都一样。而精神经验无需这些理论支撑，因为对清醒心灵而言，它本身就拥有压倒性的权威。我们每个人都知道“不那么醒”和“更醒”之间的差别，也知道对客观环境反应迟钝与反应充分之间的差别。我们也都比起相对昏沉的状态，更信任自己相对清醒的状态；理由非常充分：我们对客观环境认识得越全面、越准确，自己的事业越可能成功。我所谓“精神的”经验，伴随着一种无法否认的“非常清醒”之感；因为它向我们呈现、或者说揭示客观实在的某些方面，而那些方面不会在沉溺于自我的经验中显现。</p>
<p>顺便说一句，较清醒的经验在生物学和心理学意义上也发展得更高级，而且从长远看具有生存价值。但到了精神领域，生存价值本身并不是检验标准。标准只能是最清明的意识自身所作的判断；而这个判断鲜明地宣告：我们称作精神的那种生活方式，就是最高的善。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a1088"></span>VI. Fundamental Values</h3>
<p><span class="t28">P</span><span class="t31">ERHAPS</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">WE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">SHALL</span><span class="t30"> </span>be able to form more precise ideas on this subject (which really does concern an interplanetary society) if we try to answer the question, “What is man?”</p>
<p>Clearly, whatever else he is, he certainly is (or has) a body, which is a physical or electromagnetic system of protons, electrons, etc. J. B. S. Haldane (I think) remarks somewhere, that the human body contains some millions of millions of millions of millions of hydrogen atoms, and corresponding amounts of other elements. Julian Huxley (I think) has pointed out that man’s body is approximately half-way in size between an electron and the whole universe. Astronomically, man is very small, an inconceivably minute parasite on a minute planetary grain floating in an immense void that is extremely sparsely sprinkled with great suns. Size is in itself of no importance; but if in the minute human body the physical can be the vehicle of some degree of mind or spirit, how great may be the mental and spiritual capacity of the whole cosmos of star-systems!</p>
<p>Biologically, man (as we have already noted), or rather our own species of man, which we have ludicrously called <span class="t24">Homo Sapiens</span>, is of very recent growth. But he is the most developed of terrestrial organisms. That is, he is objectively the most complex, integrated, versatile. He has specialized in being unspecialized. Hence his remarkable adaptability, and his dominance over all other species. He has indeed made a bigger mark on this planet than any other species, and a much more complex and purposeful and organized mark. Basically he remains just a mammal, a primate. His unique gift is his relatively high intelligence, which has depended on the unique development of hands and eyes and cerebral cortices. There is good reason to believe, however, that man is a very imperfect species. The bird is just about perfected for its appropriate life, for flight in the terrestrial atmosphere; but man, though he has powers far beyond the range of the bird, has not perfected those powers. In his appropriate medium of intelligent purposive action, abstract thought, personal sensibility and artistic creation, he is little better than a clumsy flying lizard.</p>
<p>Psychologically, what is it that distinguishes man from the sub-human creatures, even from his nearest relatives, the apes? He has the same ground plan of organs, reflexes, innate behaviour and emotional reaction. He shows fear, aggression, self-regard, sexuality, gregariousness, curiosity, manipulative meddlesomeness, and so on. What is distinctive in him is his superior power of discrimination, of attending to likenesses and differences. Hence his native intelligence, by which I mean the power of solving <span class="t24">novel</span> problems, not to be solved by instinct or by established habit. This unique aptitude for discrimination and intelligence has opened up for him vast new universes or experience, impossible to any other terrestrial creature. All intelligence involves the power of attending to the relevant while ignoring the irrelevant. Hence, in man, comes the power of abstraction, of attending to a particular character and relating it to other instances of the same character, and giving the identity a name, such as “red,” “two,” “pleasant.” Hence language and all the worlds of abstract thought and of concrete poetry.</p>
<p>Man’s great power of concentration of attention has been applied not only to the environment but also to his own nature. Thus he has developed the possibility of a unique kind of self-awareness and awareness of other selves. In fact, he has become capable of personality, and of genuinely personal relationships, including all the forms of developed loving and hating. From this has arisen a distinctively human or personal kind of sociality. In sub-human animals, sociality is no more than a combination of the impulse of gregariousness (including self-abasement to the herd) and the impulse of self-assertion, rising to dominance over the herd. Of course, human beings themselves often behave in this sub-human way, but the distinctively human or personal way is something of a different kind, in that it involves respect for the other as a conscious person. The distinctively human society is best understood by considering the nature of genuine human love, which again is beyond the range of sub-human consciousness.</p>
<p>When John <span class="t24">really loves</span> Jane, he does what no beast can do. He does not <span class="t24">merely</span> have a pleasant glow of feeling about her. Of course he has this, but something more also. He is aware of her as a conscious being, distinct from himself, and different in character and needs and capacities. He accepts her as she is, without wishing to impose his will on her. He respects her as a person, takes responsibility for her, cherishes her, depends on her, is mentally enriched by knowing her and loving her, and is profoundly moulded by her. The two together form a little society of distinct beings united in mutual love, and cherishing not only each other but also the “we” that they together form. No doubt they also remain self-regarding individualists, and their self-interests often conflict. All love is complicated by hate. But in genuine love the conflict is largely transcended in the will for mutual adjustment and mutual dependence.</p>
<p>Perhaps I ought to apologize for wandering so far from the planets. But, believe me, this digression is very relevant to my theme. If one undertakes to discuss what man ought to do with the planets, one must first say what one thinks man ought to do with himself. And that is what I am trying to do. It cannot be done without a certain amount of fundamental thinking, not in the way of theorizing about the universe, but through trying to get a clear view of human experience itself.</p>
<p>Well, to continue this venture, from the experience of love or friendship or some form of genuine community in the field of personal contacts, the human individual may rise to the will for genuine community in all his dealings with his fellow human beings, no matter how slight his contact with them. Thus arises the ideal of universal Christian love, or the brotherhood of man. This ideal has haunted man for thousands of years. In one form or another, it has played an important part in all the great religions of the world. In Christianity, love is deified. God, we are told, actually is, in some obscure way, Love. The Holy Spirit is Love. And love is believed by Christians to be the fundamental power which created the universe. To believe such a proposition seems to me to go far beyond the legitimate scope of human thinking; but the fact that in the past many of the best minds have believed this has to be taken into account.</p>
<p>Why is it that love, kindness, fellowship, genuine community between persons, in fact, the distinctively human or rather personal kind of sociality, has been so greatly praised? Obviously it has survival value. And if you are determined to explain the most developed kinds of human experience and behaviour, <span class="t24">simply</span> in terms of the social conditioning of primitive impulses, quite a plausible explanation can be given. But this explanation does not go to the root of the matter. It is sheer dogma that the most developed and conscious human experience and behaviour can be fully explained simply in terms of its primitive sources. To do so is to leave out the most distinctive thing about man, namely his power of standing outside himself, to some extent, and being interested in things other than himself for their own sake. Anyone who has really been in love should understand this. Anyone who has had any genuine religious feelings should know it even better. The great Saints, Western and Eastern, were concerned (I should say) with experiences at the highest reach of human capacity, experiences which cannot be adequately described in terms of merely primitive impulses. I would add that neither should we accept uncritically the saints’ own interpretations of their experiences, stated in terms of their own contemporary ideas about the fundamental nature of the universe. Human language and human thought itself were not then, and are not even now, sufficiently developed to make reliable statements about those most fully “awakened” experiences. Nevertheless, the experiences themselves, I suggest, are extremely important for anyone concerned with the proper ordering of human life. Someone may protest that, however important these experiences were to the saints, they are of no importance to us, because we do not share in them. The objection is worth answering. No doubt the saints had religious experience in a peculiarly vivid and compelling form which most of us cannot attain. But even ordinary men and women, or at any rate the more sensitive of them, if they take the trouble to discipline themselves a little, and concentrate their attention on their felt relation with each other and with the universe, can quite well discover in their own experience the <span class="t24">kind</span> of thing that the great mystics were trying to describe.</p>
<p>For lack of a better term, I shall call this most significant kind of experience “spiritual experience” or “experience of spirit.” But I hasten to add that the word “spirit” need not be taken to mean some <span class="t24">thing</span> or <span class="t24">substance</span> other than matter, which is conceived as another thing or substance. Still less must spirit mean a divine person or God. I mean by “spirit” simply a particular way of experiencing and behaving. In fact, it is that kind of life which Christians sometimes call “the life of the spirit” or the “Way” of the Spirit, and Chinese sages have called simply “Tao,” the “Way.” What kind of a life the spiritual life is, I shall consider presently, trying to describe it in modern language and in relation to modern experience.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, I suggest that whatever <span class="t24">theories</span> men invent to justify the goodness or rightness of that way of life, what actually confronts them in their direct experience is simply a vision of the Way itself as supremely right. Whatever the status of spirit in the universe, what matters is simply spirit. The saints and sages yearned for the full expression of spirit in their own lives and the lives of others. They yearned to outgrow, or wake up from, the life of mere self-interest. They sought the ampler view. They sought, through self-denial, a real self-detachment for the sake of the ampler view, the deeper, more penetrating view. Through the discipline of the ordinary self-interested worldly self, they sought to “wake up,” and adopt the point of view of a supposed universal self. Sometimes they persuaded themselves that they were actually gathered up into that supreme self in mystical communion; but even if one feels very sceptical about this, and about the existence of such a universal self, one must recognize that what they strove for was a transcendence of the limitations of mere <span class="t24">self</span>-concern, a self-forgetting through concern for other individuals and for the whole universe. In fact, a vision of the spirit as an ideal way of living, and as in some sense what we are <span class="t24">for</span>, intruded into their ordinary experience and claimed their allegiance.</p>
<p>But what <span class="t24">sort</span> of a way of life is the spiritual life? Leaving out the intellectual trappings of religious and metaphysical doctrine about the universe and fundamental reality, and bearing in mind aspects of knowledge that were not available to the ancients, I think we can express the essence of the matter in modern language. The very brief account that I shall give would not satisfy the ancient teachers or their modern followers, just because it leaves out the metaphysical doctrines; yet it does seem to me to describe the essence of their experience, in so far as it can have any meaning at all for ordinary human beings.</p>
<p>I must begin with a few words about the nature of personality, for the spiritual way is essentially a way for personal beings. Something is aware of something. “I,” whatever that is, am aware of my body in its physical environment, and also of my “self” or psychological personality (as a creature with a certain character, needs and capacities), also of other persons, of societies of persons, of abstractions such as physical laws and moral principles, and (in a very sketchy way) of a whole objective universe <span class="t24">in</span> which I am. No doubt my awareness of these things is very confused and erroneous, but we all assume that there is <span class="t24">something</span> to be aware of, something which is independent of our experiencing it. In fact, there is an objective universe.</p>
<p>Now the innermost ring of objectivity, so to speak, is my own body in its relation with a physical environment. My body is experienced as a going concern, as dynamic, as tending toward certain actions and needing certain things of the environment. I consciously “espouse” my body’s needs. I desire their fulfillment. For instance, when my body needs food, I desire the activity of eating. Similarly with the needs for sleep, sexual activity, and so on. In infancy this innermost ring of objectivity is all that is experienced. But gradually the growing child becomes aware of another ring of objectivity, namely that of conscious persons. He begins to be self-conscious and other-conscious. He “espouses” the needs of his own psychological person, and (less constantly) of a few other persons, such as his mother. In genuine love we “espouse” another person, forming a psychological symbiosis with him or her. The important point is that both the other person and my own self, with its particular character and needs and powers, are facts objective to my awareness of them. Of course, my awareness of them may be largely erroneous, I may make mistakes about them, as about any other objective facts. But they are not just fancy. They are what they are.</p>
<p>Beyond the sphere of the persons with whom I have personal contact, we may place another ring of objectivity, namely that on which occur all societies of persons, and the incipient society of mankind as a whole. Beyond again, comes the ring of abstract ideas, true and false, both kinds being objective to the experiencing “I.” True ideas, in the final analysis, are simply objective characters apprehended by minds, and abstracted from their particular settings. The idea “dog” is the common character distinctive of all dogs. Of course, all ideas are partly false, some more so, some less. But even the false elements in ideas are rooted in objectivity. So to speak, they result from “squinting” objective characters together in wrong patterns, much as the drunkard may see two moons instead of one. He sees one moon twice, or in two places. Consider the idea of a “centaur,” half horse, half man. There’s no such beast; but the idea “centaur” is the product of “squinting” half a man and most of a horse together, and so forming a new abstract idea.</p>
<p>Now the overwhelmingly most important abstraction is the spirit, the ideal way of life. It is an ideal which is implied in, and emerges from, the actual experienced nature of personal beings, much as the law of gravity is implied in and emerges from the actual experienced nature of the behaviour of falling objects and the movements of planets and stars.</p>
<p>In the same way the ideas of good and evil and all possible kinds of values arise out of the impact of some region of objectivity on the conscious individual. Mere abstract subjectivity is no more than a featureless possibility of being aware of something, including values. All values arise from the conscious “espousal” of objective needs. Some are needs of one’s own body, some are the more subtle needs of persons, myself and other selves.</p>
<p>Note further that personality <span class="t24">involves</span> community. There cannot be true personality save in true community with other persons. By community I mean not simply a relationship in which each individual strives to use others for his own advancement but the distinctively human social relationship, in which individuals are united in mutual respect for each other <span class="t24">as persons</span>. The spiritual way is essentially a way for persons in community with other persons. As Christians might put it, it is the way for a “Church,” not simply for the individual, seeking individualistic salvation. As Communists might put it, it is the way for comrades working devotedly in a common cause.</p>
<p>Now I suggest that the spirit is essentially the way of life in which we strive towards full, comprehensive and true awareness of the objective universe, and toward appropriate feeling and appropriate creative action in relation to it. The universe, of course, includes oneself as an object, a self among other selves. In the spiritual life one strives for sensitive and intelligent awareness of things in the universe (including persons), and of the universe <span class="t24">as a whole</span>. One strives also for appropriate feeling about things and people and the universe as a whole, without prejudice in favour of oneself as an individual or one’s own family or nation, or even the human species. Finally one strives for appropriate and creative action in relation to all this.</p>
<p>The experience of the spirit, as the supreme good and as in some sense what we are “for,” needs no support from metaphysical argument or religious doctrine. It is simply “given,” as a datum, to the relatively awake mind, or rather to the awake mind that has not been perverted by obsession with some minor good, such as power, or bemused by some particular theory, such as materialism or theism. Let us never forget that, if theism is a frail invention of the human intellect, so is materialism. All such ambitious theories are almost certainly more false than true. All such metaphysical speculation, though interesting and educatively valuable, is quite unreliable, whether as support or denial of the experience of spirit, which needs no support other than its own overwhelming authority for the awakened mind. We all know the difference between being less awake and more awake, less responsive to the objective environment and more so. And we all trust our relatively more awake states more than our relatively somnolent states; for the very good reason that, the more comprehensively and accurately we are aware of the objective environment, the more our enterprises are likely to succeed. The kind of experience that I have called “spiritual” comes with an undeniable sense of “being very much awake,” in the sense that it presents a vision or revelation of aspects of the objective reality that are <span class="t24">not</span> revealed in the self-absorbed kind of experience.</p>
<p>Incidentally, the more awake kinds of experience are also the more developed biologically and psychologically, and have had survival value in the long run. But in the sphere of spirit, survival value is not itself the test. The criterion must be simply the verdict of the most lucid consciousness itself, which emphatically declares the way of life called spiritual to be the highest good.</p>
