<h3>第十九章　殖民地建立</h3>
<p>“滑行者号”在我预定结婚日期的前三个星期抵达英国。船上没有无线电，又因为航程很快，所以完全没有预先通知。那天我和伯莎刚买完东西，准备晚上出去之前先到我公寓放下几个包裹。我们挽着胳膊走进起居室，发现“滑行者号”的船员们已经舒舒服服安顿下来，正吃我的苹果，还有我专门留着招待伯莎的巧克力。我们一时站着没说话。我感觉伯莎挽着我的手臂收紧了。约翰坐在炉火旁的扶手椅里，悠闲地啃苹果。洛蹲在壁炉前的地毯上，翻着《新政治家》杂志。恩贡科坐在另一把扶手椅里，一边嚼糖，一边弯腰照看桑博。我想，他大概正在帮这个婴儿重新整理那些厚重而陌生的衣服；没有这些东西，桑博根本无法应付英国的气候。桑博几乎全是脑袋和肚子，四肢不过像刚冒出来的小芽，此刻正歪着一只好奇的眼睛打量我。华盛顿妮娅是我此前从未见过的；夹在这一群怪物中间，她当时反而显得令人安心地普通。</p>
<p>约翰已经站了起来，嘴里还塞着东西便说道：“嗨，老菲多！嗨，伯莎！伯莎，你肯定会恨我，不过我得借菲多几个星期，让他帮我买物资之类的东西。”</p>
<p>“可我们马上就要结婚了。”我抗议。</p>
<p>“该死！”约翰说。可令我自己都吃惊的是，我随即向他保证，我们当然可以把婚礼往后推两个月。伯莎像一下子软掉似的坐进椅子里，无声地说了句“当然”。“好极了！”约翰高兴地喊道，“这件事办完以后，我也许就再也不会来烦你了。”出乎意料的是，我心里猛地一沉。</p>
<p>接下来几个星期，我们卷进一阵纯粹实际事务的旋风。“滑行者号”需要全面整修，飞机也得修理。工具和机械、电气设备、管道材料都必须买齐，先运到瓦尔帕莱索等待转运；木材则要从南美森林运到同一港口；一般生活物资要在英国采购。在约翰指挥下，所有这些交易都由我负责交涉。约翰本人列出一张书单，我必须想尽办法把书弄到手并发运出去。其中包括几十种生物学各分支、热带农业、医学等领域的技术著作；还要有理论物理、天文学、哲学书籍，以及一组颇为诱人的纯文学作品，语言种类很多。最难找的是几十种亚洲著作，光看书名就透着神秘学气息。</p>
<p>在“滑行者号”下一次起航日期前不久，其他欧洲成员陆续抵达。约翰亲自去了匈牙利，把一个据说已经十七岁、却小得像豆丁的女孩杰莉接来。她绝称不上漂亮。她头颅的额部和枕部都异常发达，到了让人不适的程度：后脑远远向后伸出，前额也突出到超过鼻尖，而鼻子本身几乎没有发育。从侧面看，她的脑袋活像一把槌球槌。她还有兔唇，两条腿又短又弯。整体外貌几乎像个呆小症患者；然而，她不仅具有超常的智力和气质，视觉也敏锐得惊人。我们所谓“蓝色”的那一段光谱，在她眼里还能进一步分辨出两种基本色；而且她的视觉范围还深入红外区。除了这种颜色辨别力之外，她对形状的感觉也比我们“颗粒细得多”。她视网膜上的神经末梢很可能比普通人多，因为她能在二十码外读报纸，也能一眼看出一枚便士其实并不是精确的圆。她对形态的敏感达到这样的程度：如果把一幅拼图的所有碎片随手扔在她面前，她只需看一眼就能同时把握各部分之间有意义的关系，几乎不停顿便能拼出整幅图。这种惊人的知觉能力经常使她痛苦，因为任何人造物在她眼里似乎都没有真正达到制造者原先意图中的形状。至于艺术，她不仅会被执行上的不精确折磨，也会被构思本身的粗陋折磨。</p>
<p>除了杰莉，还有那个法国女孩玛丽安娜·拉丰。和杰莉相比，她完全正常，而且深眼睛、橄榄色皮肤，看起来还相当漂亮。她仿佛把整套法国文化都装进了自己脑子，任何经典作品的任何段落都能随口引用；而且凭借某种她自己的魔法，她还能把那段文字的意义扩展开来，让人仿佛直接探到作者心灵的深处。</p>
<p>另一个是瑞典女孩西格丽德。约翰叫她“执梳者”，因为“她特别会把纠缠成一团的心灵梳开，直到一个个都变得顺顺当当、恢复正常”。她过去得过肺结核，却似乎凭借某种精神上的“免疫化”作用把自己的组织治好了。即使康复以后，她仍保留着肺痨病人常见的那种愉快气质。她有一双大眼睛，身体纤弱；惊人的同情与洞察天赋，却同一种对粗暴力量近乎母性的温柔结合在一起。发现强者变得残暴时，她会责备，却仍然爱它。她能够把那种力量训得夹着尾巴哀嚎着走开，可与此同时，她自己又会“软得一塌糊涂，觉得它不过是个可爱得要命、只是有点淘气的小男孩”。</p>
<p>几个年轻的男性超常者也一个接一个出现，准备登上“滑行者号”。（那段时间，韦恩赖特家的房子简直成了骇人的贫民窟。）其中有芬兰人凯米，几乎像一个更年轻的约翰；有土耳其人沙欣，比约翰大几岁，却非常乐意做他的下属；还有来自高加索的卡吉斯。</p>
<p>在这些人里，按正常人的眼光看，最有魅力的是沙欣。他有俄罗斯舞蹈演员那样的体格，社交时又带着一种轻巧的分寸感。根据人的心情不同，这种气质既可以被看成迷人的轻浮，也可以被看成崇高的超然。</p>
<p>卡吉斯只比约翰小一点，抵达时精神状态已经接近躁狂。他搭一艘不定期货船经历了极其折磨人的旅程，而他那本来就不稳定的心灵没能承受住压力。外貌上，他同约翰属于同一种类型，只是肤色更深，体质也更弱。我始终很难对这个古怪存在形成一个连贯的印象。他不断在兴奋与迟钝、激情与超然之间摆动。有人向我保证，这种波动的原因不在他身体的生理节律，而在一些我看不见的外部事件。我问究竟是什么事件，正试图帮助我理解的洛说：“他和西格丽德一样，对人格有极强的感受力。不过他看待人的方式和她很不同。她只是爱他们，也笑他们，帮助他们，把他们治好。但对卡吉斯来说，每个人都像一件艺术作品，有自己特定的性质、风格，或者一种理想形式；这个人可能把那种形式体现得好，也可能体现得差。一旦某个人非常刺耳地背离了自己独有的风格或理想形式，卡吉斯就会痛苦得受不了。”</p>
<p>1928年8月，这十个年轻人和一个完全无助的婴儿，乘“滑行者号”启航。</p>
<p>约翰仍通过普通邮政同我们保持联系。正如我以后会解释的，“滑行者号”，有时还有那架飞机，经常需要在各岛之间或往返瓦尔帕莱索，因此约翰那些简短而谨慎的信件有机会寄出。从这些文件中，我们先后得知：出航一路平安；他们在瓦尔帕莱索停靠，装走尽可能多的物资；已经抵达小岛；“滑行者号”由恩贡科、凯米和玛丽安娜驾驶，不断往返瓦尔帕莱索，把剩余物资运来；定居点建设已经全面展开；亚洲成员也已经抵达，而且“适应得很好”；一场飓风袭击小岛，摧毁所有临时建筑，又把受损的“滑行者号”整个搁到港边一座小山丘上，还弄伤了一个西藏男孩；他们已经种下大片水果和蔬菜；造了六条捕鱼独木舟；卡吉斯患上一种严重的消化系统疾病，预计将死；后来又康复；一具加拉帕戈斯蜥蜴的残骸经过天知道多么漫长的漂流被冲上岸；西格丽德驯服了一只信天翁，而那家伙偷了早餐；殖民地经历第一次悲剧——杨忠被鲨鱼拖走，凯米为救他遭到严重撕咬，却仍没能成功；桑博把全部时间都用于阅读，可仍不会独立坐直；他们按“詹姆斯·琼斯式”做了一批管乐器，不过加装特殊附件，使普通五指手也能演奏；措莫特雷（一个西藏人）和沙欣正在创作极其美妙的音乐；杰莉得了急性阑尾炎，洛成功做了手术；洛本人因为在某些胚胎学实验上工作过度，又陷入一次可怕噩梦；后来已经醒来；玛丽安娜和沈括搬到岛的另一边居住，“因为他们想单独待一阵”；“华希”快疯了，因为她发现自己竟然开始憎恨赢得沙欣爱情的西藏女孩兰科尔；“华希”试图杀死兰科尔，也试图杀死自己；西格丽德经过漫长耐心的努力仍无法治好“华希”，自己反倒开始出现透支迹象；随后两个女孩都开始受到朗阿策从远方施加的心灵感应治疗；殖民者已经完成石砌图书馆和会议厅，天文台也开始动工；措莫特雷和兰科尔显然是最熟练的心灵感应者，如今已经能每天为殖民地提供“世界新闻公报”；队伍中更先进的成员正在朗阿策指导下接受严厉的精神训练，最终应当能把他们提升到一个全新的经验层级；最后，一场强烈地震使整座岛下沉近两英尺，因此码头不得不重新加砌好几层石块，而从此以后，“滑行者号”也必须随时保持能够紧急撤离的状态，以防整座岛有一天直接消失。</p>
<p>几个月拖成几年以后，约翰的信来得越来越少，内容也越来越短。他显然已经完全被殖民地事务吸住；而且随着这群人越来越深入地参与超常活动，他也越来越难用我们能够理解的方式描述他们的生活。</p>
<p>然而到了1932年春天，我非常意外地收到约翰一封长信。信的主要意思，是催我尽快去岛上看看。我摘录其中最关键的一段：“我要你来对我们施展你的记者本领，这话说出来你肯定会笑。其实，我到底还是想让你把从前威胁要写的那本传记写出来——不是为了我们，而是为了你们这个物种。我得解释一下。我们的开局非常好。有时确实有点惨烈，不过现在我们已经建立起一种非常令人满意的生活和社会。实际工作运行得平稳而愉快，我们终于能够共同投入一项更伟大的努力，尝试抵达更高层次的经验。和刚登岛时相比，我们的心智已经发生很大变化。我们中有些人已经把现实看得很远、很深；至少，我们如今已经清楚地看见自己承担的工作究竟是什么。但许多迹象表明，再过不了多少个月，这个殖民地就会被摧毁。如果你们这个物种发现我们，它一定会试图砸碎我们，而我们目前还没有能力击败它。朗阿策一直催促我们——而且他说得对——继续推进工作中的精神部分，争取在终结到来之前尽可能多完成一些。不过，我们也不妨留下这场小冒险的记录，给未来可能再次尝试建立新世界的超常者作参考，也给智人中较敏感的成员留一点东西。给超常者看的记录由朗阿策负责；给你们这个物种看的版本只需要正常人的能力，你就足以把它做好。”</p>
<p>这时，我已经算是个颇为成功的自由撰稿记者，面前排着满满的工作计划。我已经结婚，伯莎也正怀着孩子。可我还是毫不犹豫地接受了邀请。当天午后，我便开始打听前往瓦尔帕莱索的轮船，并给约翰回信——寄到瓦尔帕莱索留局待领——告诉他大概什么时候能等到我。</p>
<p>我带着负罪感把这消息告诉伯莎。她显然受了很大打击，却只说：“是的，如果约翰需要你，那你当然得去。”随后我去了韦恩赖特家。帕克斯让我大吃一惊，因为我刚开口说到那封信，她便打断了我。“我知道，”她说，“这几个星期，他一直断断续续让我看到岛上的一些画面，有时甚至会直接同我说话。他告诉过我，他会来找你。”</p>

<h3><span id="a349"></span>CHAPTER XIX. THE COLONY IS FOUNDED</h3>
<p><span class="t28">T</span><span class="t31">HE</span><span class="t30"> S</span><span class="t31">KID</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">REACHED</span><span class="t30"> </span>England three weeks before the date on which I was to be married. As she had no radio, and her voyage had been speedy, she arrived unannounced. Bertha and I had been shopping. We called at my flat to deposit some parcels before going out for the evening. Arm in arm, we entered my sitting-room, and found the Skid’s crew snugly installed, eating my apples and some chocolates which I kept for Bertha’s entertainment. We stood for a moment in silence. I felt Bertha’s arm tighten on mine. John was enjoying his apple in an easy chair by the fire. Lo, squatting on the hearth rug, was turning over the pages of the New Statesman. Ng-Gunko was in the other easy chair, chewing sweets and bending over Sambo. I think he was helping the infant to readjust the thick and unfamiliar clothes without which he could not have faced the English climate. Sambo, all head and stomach, with limbs that were mere buds, cocked an inquisitive eye at me. Washingtonia, whom I had not seen before, struck me at that moment as reassuringly commonplace among those freaks.</p>
<p>John had risen, and was saying with his mouth full, “Hullo, old Fido, hullo, Bertha! You’ll hate me, Bertha, but I must have Fido to help me for a few weeks, buying stores and things.” “But we’re just going to get married,” I protested. “Damn!” said John. Then to my surprise I assured John that of course we would put it off for a couple of months. Bertha wilted on to a chair with a voiceless “Of course.” “Good,” cried John cheerfully. “After this affair I may not bother you any more.” Unexpectedly my heart sank.</p>
<p>The following weeks were spent in a whirl of practical activities. The Skid had to be reconditioned, the plane repaired. Tools and machinery, electric fittings and plumbing materials must be bought and shipped to Valparaiso to await transshipment. Timber must be sent from the South American forests to the same port. General stores must be purchased in England. My task was to negotiate all these transactions, under John’s direction. John himself prepared a list of books which I must somehow procure and dispatch. There were to be scores of technical works on various biological subjects, tropical agriculture, medicine and so on. There were to be books on theoretical physics, astronomy, philosophy, and a rather intriguing selection of purely literary works in many languages. Most difficult to procure were many scores of Asiatic writings with titles suggestive of the occult.</p>
<p>Shortly before the Skid’s next sailing-date the additional European members of the party began to arrive. John himself went to Hungary to fetch Jelli, a mite said to be seventeen years old. She was no beauty. The frontal and the occipital regions of her head were repulsively overdeveloped, so that the back of her head stretched away behind her, and her brow protruded beyond her nose, which was rudimentary. In profile her head suggested a croquet mallet. She had a hare-lip and short bandy legs. Her general appearance was that of a cretin; yet she had supernormal intelligence and temperament, and also hyper-sensitive vision. Not only did she distinguish two primary colours within the spectrum-band that we call blue, but also she could see well down into the infra-red. In addition to this colour-discrimination, she had a sense of form that was, so to speak, much finer-grained than ours. Probably there were more nerve-endings in her retinae than in normal eyes, for she could read a newspaper at twenty yards’ distance, and she could see at a glance that a penny was not accurately circular. So sensitive was she to form that, if the parts of a puzzle picture were flung down before her, she took in their significant relations at a glance, and could construct the picture without a pause. This amazing percipience often caused her distress, for no man-made article appeared to her to achieve the shape that its maker intended. And in the sphere of art she was excruciated, not merely by inaccuracy of execution but also by crudity of conception.</p>
<p>Besides Jelli, there was the French girl, Marianne Laffon, quite normal in contrast with Jelli, and rather pretty with her dark eyes and olive skin. She was seemingly a repository of the whole of French culture, and could quote any passage of any classic, and, by some magic of her own, so amplify it that she seemed to plumb the author’s mind.</p>
<p>There was also a Swedish girl, Sigrid, whom John called the Comb-Wielder, “because she has such a gift for combing out tangled minds till they’re all sleek and sane.” She had been a consumptive, but had apparently cured herself by some sort of mental “immunizing” of her tissues. Even after her cure she retained the phthisic’s cheerfulness. A great-eyed, fragile thing, she combined her wonderful gift of sympathy and insight with a maternal tenderness toward brute strength. When she found it being brutal, she censured but still loved it. She was moved to send it away howling with its tail between its legs, but at the same time she “felt all sloppy about it, as though it were just a delicious little naughty boy.”</p>
<p>Several young male supernormals turned up, one by one, to join the Skid. (The Wainwrights’ house became at this time a shocking slum.) There was Kemi, the Finn, a younger John; Shahîn, the Turk, a few years older than John, but well content to be his subordinate; and Kargis from the Caucasus.</p>
<p>Of these, Shahîn was from the normal point of view the most attractive, for he had the build of a Russian dancer and in social intercourse a lightness of touch which one took according to one’s mood, either as charming frivolity or as sublime detachment.</p>
<p>Kargis, who was not much younger than John, arrived in a state bordering on mania. He had had a very trying journey in a tramp steamer, and his unstable mind had failed to stand the strain. In appearance he was of John’s type, but darker and less hardy. I found it very difficult to form a consistent idea of this strange being. He oscillated between excitement and lethargy, between passion and detachment. The cause of these fluctuations was not, I was assured, anything in his body’s physiological rhythm, but external events which were hidden from me. When I inquired what kind of events, Lo, who was trying to help me, said, “He’s like Sigrid in having a great sense of personality. But he regards persons rather differently from her way. She just loves them, and laughs at them too, and helps them, and cures them. But for him each person is like a work of art, having a particular quality or style, or ideal form which he embodies well or ill. And when a person is jarringly untrue to his peculiar style or ideal form, Kargis is excruciated.”</p>
<p>The ten young people and one helpless infant set sail in the Skid in August of 1928.</p>
<p>John kept in communication with us by the ordinary mail. As I shall explain later, the Skid, and sometimes the plane, had occasion to make frequent voyages among the Islands or to Valparaiso. Thus it was possible to post John’s brief and guarded letters. From these documents we learned first that the voyage out had been uneventful; that they had called at Valparaiso to load as much of the stores as possible; that they had reached the island; that the Skid, manned by Ng-Gunko, Kemi and Marianne, was plying to and from Valparaiso to transport the rest of the stores; that the building of the settlement was now well under way; that the Asiatic members had arrived, and were “settling in nicely,” that a hurricane had struck the island, destroying all the temporary buildings, depositing the damaged Skid on a little hill beside the harbour, and hurting one of the Tibetan boys; that they had sowed large tracts of fruit and vegetables; that they had built six canoes for fishing; that Kargis had fallen seriously ill of some digestive disease and was expected to die; that he had recovered; that the remains of a Galapagos lizard had been washed up on the shore after God knows how long a journey; that Sigrid had tamed an albatross, and that it stole the breakfast; that the Colony had suffered its first tragedy, for Yang Chung had been caught by a shark, and Kemi had been seriously mauled in the vain attempt to rescue him; that Sambo was spending all his time reading, but could not yet sit upright; that they had made for themselves pipes on the James Jones model, but with special attachments so that they could be played by normal five-fingered hands; that Tsomotre (one of the Tibetans) and Shahîn were composing wonderful music; that Jelli had developed acute appendicitis and Lo had operated successfully; that Lo herself had been working too hard on some embryological experiments and had fallen into one of her terrible nightmares; that she was awake again; that Marianne and Shên Kuo had gone to live on the far side of the Island “because they wanted to be alone for a bit”; that “Washy” was going mad, for she complained of feeling hate for Lankor (a Tibetan girl who had won the heart of Shahîn); that “Washy” had tried to kill Lankor and herself; that Sigrid, in spite of prolonged and patient efforts, had failed to cure “Washy,” and was now herself showing signs of strain, that both girls were now being cured from afar by Langatse’s telepathic influence; that the Colonists had completed their stone library and meeting-house, and the observatory was being started; that Tsomotre and Lankor, who were evidently the most expert telepathists, were now able to provide the colony with the news of the world in daily bulletins; that the more advanced members of the party, under the direction of Langatse, were undergoing severe exercises in spiritual discipline, which in time should raise them to a new plane of experience; that a severe earthquake had caused the whole island to sink nearly two feet, so that they had to lay several new courses of stone on the quays, and would henceforth need to keep the Skid in readiness for a sudden exodus, in case the island should disappear.</p>
<p>As the months protracted themselves into years, John’s letters became less frequent and more brief. He was evidently entirely absorbed in the affairs of the colony; and as the party became more and more concerned with supernormal activities, he found it increasingly difficult to give us an intelligible account of their life.</p>
<p>In the spring of 1932, however, I was greatly surprised to receive a long letter from John, the main purport of which was to urge me to visit the island as soon as possible. I quote the essential passage. “You will laugh when I tell you I want you to come and use your journalistic prowess upon us. In fact I want you to write that threatened biography after all, not for our sakes but for your own species. I must explain. We have made a wonderful start. Sometimes it was a bit grim, but now we have worked out a very satisfactory life and society. Our practical activities run smoothly, delightfully, and we are able at last to join in a great effort to reach the higher planes of experience. Already we are very different mentally from what we were when we landed. Some of us have seen far and deep into reality, and we have at least gained a clear view of the work we have undertaken. But a number of signs suggest that before very many months have passed the colony will be destroyed. If your species discovers us, it will certainly try to smash us; and we are not yet in a position to defeat it. Langatse has urged us (and he is right) to push on with the spiritual part of our work, so as to complete as much as possible of it before the end. But also we may as well leave records of our little adventure as an example for any future supernormals who may attempt to found the new world, and for the benefit of the more sensitive members of Homo sapiens. Langatse will take charge of the record for supernormals; the record for your species entails only normal powers and can be done satisfactorily by yourself.”</p>
<p>I was now a tolerably successful free-lance journalist, and I had a full programme before me. I was married, and Bertha was expecting a child. Yet I eagerly accepted the invitation. That afternoon I made inquiries about steamers for Valparaiso, and replied to John (post restante at that city), telling him when to expect me.</p>
<p>Guiltily, I broke my news to Bertha. She was hard hit by it, but she said, “Yes, if John wants you, of course you must go.” Then I went round to the Wainwrights’. Pax greatly surprised me, for no sooner had I begun to tell her about the letter than she interrupted me. “I know,” she said, “for some weeks he has been giving me little stray visions of the island, and even talking to me. He said he would be asking for you.”</p>
