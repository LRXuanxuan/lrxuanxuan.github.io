<h3>第一章</h3>
<h3>初次相见</h3>
<p>普拉克茜和我曾是恋人；不过，我们爱得并不十分安稳。她从不肯畅谈自己的过去，有时还会把自己缩进一层沉默而沮丧的阴云里。但我们也常常非常幸福，而我相信，这份幸福正在扎下越来越深的根。</p>
<p>后来，她母亲病危，普拉克茜便消失了。我偶尔收到她一两封信，信上没有地址，只说我可以把回信寄到北威尔士某个村子的邮局转交；村名时而这个，时而另一个。这些信的口气变化很大，有时只是敷衍地表示友好，有时却真切地流露出想再见到我的渴望。信中还神秘地提到一项“奇怪的职责”，据她说，这和她父亲的工作有关。我知道，那位伟大的生理学家一直在对高等哺乳动物的大脑进行极为惊人的实验。他曾培育出几只智力高得惊人的牧羊犬，而在他去世时，据说还在从事更雄心勃勃的研究。普拉克茜一封较冷淡的信里说，这项新职责给了她一种“意想不到的甜美回报”；可另一封感情强烈得多的信里，她却痛苦地抱怨“这种苛刻、迷人、把人变得不像人的生活”。有时，她仿佛正为了某件不能说明的事情陷入冲突和折磨。有一封信甚至混乱得让我担心她是否精神失常。于是我决定把即将到来的休假全用来在北威尔士徒步，碰碰运气，看能不能找到她。</p>
<p>我花了十天时间，在她信中那些地址所指的大致地区里，从一家小酒馆走到另一家，到处打听附近有没有一个姓特里隆的小姐。最后，在兰费斯蒂尼奥格，我终于听到了她的消息。据说，有一位这个姓的年轻女子住在特劳斯瓦尼兹上方某处荒原边缘的一间牧羊人小屋里。告诉我这些的当地店主神秘兮兮地说：“她确实是位很奇怪的年轻女士。她有朋友，我就是其中之一；不过，她也有敌人。”</p>
<p>我照着他的指点，沿蜿蜒的特劳斯瓦尼兹公路走了几英里，然后向左拐进一条小路。又走了大约一英里，在开阔荒原的边缘，我看见一间极小的石屋，用粗糙的页岩石板砌成，四周围着一小块园地和几株矮小的树。门关着，但烟囱里正冒着烟。我敲了门，里面没有回应。透过窗子望进去，是一间典型的乡间厨房，不过桌上却堆着一摞书。我在园里一张摇摇晃晃的凳子上坐下，看见卷心菜和豌豆一行行种得很整齐。右手远处，隔着深深的辛法尔峡谷，费斯蒂尼奥格伏在那里，像一群灰蓝色的石板大象，跟在领头的、没有尖塔的教堂后面，沿山脊向谷底缓缓走去。再往后、更高处，是莫尔温山脉。</p>
<p>我抽到第二支烟时，远处传来了普拉克茜的声音。当初最先吸引我的，正是她的声音。有一次我坐在咖啡馆里，被身后某个陌生人发出的那种敏感而富有人味的声音牢牢吸引。如今，我又一次只闻其声，不见其人。我愉快地听了一会儿她说话。正如我过去常说的，那声音像炎热夏日里，高山小湖卵石岸边细浪清凉而闪烁的絮语。</p>
<p>我站起身准备迎她，却被一种奇怪的东西钉在原地。普拉克茜的话语之间夹着的，并不是另一个人的声音，而是一种完全不同的声响——有清楚的节奏和语意，却又绝非人声。她还没从屋角转出来，我便听见她说：“可是，亲爱的，别老为自己没有手这件事耿耿于怀！你已经把这个缺陷克服得漂亮极了。”接着，她的同伴发出一串奇异而涓涓不断的声音。随后，普拉克茜和一条大狗一起穿过门，走进花园。</p>
<p>她停住了脚步，眼睛因惊讶而睁大，我希望其中也有喜悦；但很快，她的眉头便皱了起来。她把一只手放在狗头上，沉默地站了一会儿。我正好有时间看出她已经变了。她穿着有些沾泥的灯芯绒长裤和一件蓝衬衫。还是那双灰眼睛，还是那张丰润却线条果断的嘴——最近我总觉得那张嘴与她的性格并不相称——还是那蓬赤褐色、略带胡萝卜色的头发。只是从前略显苍白的脸，如今晒成了健康的红褐色，而且完全没有化妆，连口红也没有。可这副粗粝健康的外表，又被眼下的暗影和嘴角周围的紧绷奇怪地抵消了。人在恋爱时，两三秒钟里竟能注意到这么多东西，真是不可思议！</p>
<p>她的手离开狗头，伸向我表示欢迎。“好吧，”她笑着说，“既然你都循着气味把我们找出来了，那我们最好还是把你当自己人，告诉你实情。”她的语气有些尴尬，却似乎也带着一点如释重负。“是不是，天狼星？”她低头望着那条大狗补了一句。</p>
<p>直到这时，我才第一次真正打量这头非同寻常的动物。它显然绝不是一条普通的狗。大体上看，它像一条阿尔萨斯狼犬，也许还混了些大丹犬或獒犬的血，因为它实在大得惊人。整体体形近似狼，却因为腿高而比狼更修长。毛虽然短，却异常浓密柔滑，尤其是颈部，一圈紧实蓬乱的鬃毛像翻涌的浪。那种丝绸般的质感里，又掺着一丝执拗的粗硬，因此并不显得柔弱。普拉克茜有一次称它为“丝做的钢丝”。它的背部和头顶是黑色的，到了身体两侧、四肢和腹部，则淡成一种冷峻的灰黄褐色。两眼上方还有两大片黄褐色斑纹，使它的脸看起来像戴着一副怪异的面具，又像一尊希腊雕像，空洞双眼的头盔被推到了额头上。真正让天狼星与所有其他狗区别开来的，是它硕大的颅骨。事实上，这个头颅并没有达到人们对“拥有人类智力的动物”所想象的那种尺寸；因为正如我后面还要解释的，特里隆的技术不仅增加了脑的体积，也使神经纤维本身发生了精细化。尽管如此，天狼星的头顶仍比任何普通狗都高得多。高耸的额头配上丝滑的被毛，使它多少有些像著名的边境牧羊犬——牧羊犬中的卓越品种。后来我才知道，这个聪明的犬种确实参与构成了它的血统。但它的颅骨远比边境牧羊犬巨大，头顶的圆拱几乎一直高到那双硕大的阿尔萨斯狼犬式耳朵尖端。为了托住这颗沉重的头，它的颈部和肩部肌肉极为发达。我们初次相遇时，它简直带着一种狮子般的威势，因为脊背上的毛全都竖了起来。它对我的怀疑，把毛逆向刷得根根直立。那双灰色眼睛本来很像狼眼，只是瞳孔和普通狗一样是圆的，并非狼那样的狭缝。总而言之，它确实是一头令人畏惧的动物，精瘦、结实，像丛林里的野兽。</p>
<p>它的目光始终没有离开我，只张开嘴，露出一排排象牙色的锯齿，发出一个古怪的声音，结尾处音调上扬，仿佛一句问话。普拉克茜回答：“对，是罗伯特。记住，他可靠得像钢一样。”她带着歉意冲我笑笑，又补充说：“而且他也许能派上用场。”</p>
<p>天狼星很有礼貌地摆了摆那条饰毛丰厚的尾巴，但冰冷的眼睛仍紧盯着我。</p>
<p>我们之间又落下一阵尴尬的沉默，直到普拉克茜说：“我们一整天都在荒原上照看羊，午饭也没吃，我现在饿得要命。进来吧，我给大家弄点茶。”走进那间铺着石板地面的狭小厨房时，她又说：“你说的每一句话，天狼星都听得懂。刚开始你听不懂他说什么，不过我懂，我可以给你翻译。”</p>
<p>普拉克茜在小小的食品储藏室里进进出出准备吃的，我坐着和她说话。天狼星蹲在我对面，显然焦虑不安地打量我。她看到它那副样子，语气先有些尖锐，随后又柔和下来：“天狼星！我都告诉你了，他没问题。别这么疑神疑鬼！”那条狗站起来，用它那套奇怪的话说了些什么，便走进花园。“它去拿柴火了。”她说。随后又压低声音：“噢，罗伯特，见到你真好，虽然我其实并不想让你找到我。”我站起身，想把她抱进怀里，她却用力低声说：“不，不，现在不行。”天狼星叼着一根木柴回来了。它斜眼看了看我们两个，尾巴明显垂下去一些，把木柴放进炉火里，又走了出去。“为什么现在不行？”我叫道。她低声说：“因为天狼星。噢，你很快就会明白的。”停了一会儿，她又说：“罗伯特，你绝不能指望我有一天会完完全全属于你——不能指望我全心全意、毫无保留地只属于你。我已经太深地卷进……卷进我父亲的这项工作里了。”我抗议起来，一把抱住她。“可爱的、人类的罗伯特。”她叹息着，把头靠在我肩上。可她立刻又挣开，郑重地说：“不，那不是我要说的话。刚才说话的只是那个雌性的人类动物。真正的我想说的是，我没法玩你希望我玩的那种游戏，至少没法全心全意地玩。”</p>
<p>随后她朝敞开的门外喊：“天狼星，喝茶！”外面传来一声犬吠作为回答，接着它大步走进来，小心翼翼地不去看我。</p>
<p>她在地板上铺了一块小桌布，把一碗茶放在它面前，说：“它平常一天吃两顿，中午正餐，晚上再吃一顿。不过今天情况特殊。”接着她又放下一大块面包皮、一块奶酪和一只小碟子，里面搁着一点果酱。“这些够你先撑一阵吗？”她问。它哼了一声，表示满意。</p>
<p>普拉克茜和我坐在桌边，吃着面包、配给黄油和战时蛋糕。她开始给我讲天狼星的身世。有时我插一句问题，有时天狼星也会用那种呜咽和低吼混合而成的奇怪语言打断她。</p>
<p>关于这一次以及后来许多次谈论往事的内容，我会在后面的章节里一一写下。眼下我必须先说明一点：如果不是天狼星本人就坐在我面前，我绝不会相信这个故事。但它那些插话虽然仍带着犬类特征，我也一个字都听不懂，其语调变化却明白无误地表现出人的智性，并且总能引出普拉克茜清楚而合乎逻辑的回应。显然，它一直跟得上谈话，会发表评论，也在观察我的反应。因此，当我听到天狼星的来历和经历时，我虽然当然震惊，却并没有觉得不可置信。起初我听得十分焦虑，因为普拉克茜牵涉得实在太深了。我开始明白，为什么我们的爱情始终有些不安稳，也开始明白，母亲去世后，她为什么没有回到我身边。我甚至暗自盘算，要怎样把她从这种“非人的束缚”中解救出来。可随着谈话继续，我又不能不承认，少女与狗之间这种古怪的关系，在根本上有一种美，甚至有一种神圣性。（当时我心里用的就是这个词。）于是，我的问题反而变得更棘手了。</p>
<p>有一次，普拉克茜刚说到自己常常多么想再见到我，天狼星便发表了一段较长的意见。说到一半，它走到她身旁，把两只前爪搭在椅子扶手上，以极其温柔而克制的动作吻了吻她的脸颊。她安静地接受了这份亲昵，没有像人类通常面对狗的亲吻时那样躲开。可她原本健康红润的脸色更深了，眼里泛起湿意。她一边抚摸它颈下蓬松柔软的毛，一边仍望着它，对我说：“它要我告诉你，罗伯特，天狼星和普拉克茜是一起长大的，就像一只手上的拇指和食指；它以只有狗才会的方式爱我，而现在我回到它身边了，这份爱比从前更深。不过我不能因此觉得自己有义务永远留下，因为它现在已经能照顾自己。将来无论它遭遇什么，我——你刚才怎么说来着，天狼星，你这个傻亲爱的？”它飞快地补了一句，她便继续说：“哦，对了——我是它永远会追踪的那一种气味；追着这气味，它要去寻找上帝。”</p>
<p>她转过脸来冲我微笑。那笑容我永远不会忘记。那条狗方才那番认真得近乎郑重其事的告白所造成的迷乱感，我也不会忘记。后来我才明白，一到感情特别深的时候，天狼星的措辞往往会变得略显拘谨而书面化，这正是它很典型的特点。</p>
<p>接着，天狼星带着一丝狡黠的神情和尾巴的一阵轻颤，又说了一句什么。她笑着转回去看它，轻轻拍了拍它的脸。“坏东西，”她说，“这个我可不告诉罗伯特。”</p>
<p>天狼星吻她时，我猛地被一阵嫉妒攫住。（一个男人，居然吃一条狗的醋！）但普拉克茜把它那段小小的发言翻译出来后，我心里又生出了更宽厚的感情。我开始盘算，怎样由我和普拉克茜一起给天狼星一个永久的家，帮助它完成自己的命运——不管那命运究竟是什么。可是，正如我后面会讲到的，等待我们的其实是另一种结局。</p>
<p>那顿古怪的饭间，普拉克茜告诉我，正如我所猜的，天狼星是她父亲一生研究的最高成就；它从小就被当作特里隆家的一员抚养，如今正在协助经营一座牧羊场；而她本人则替它料理家务，同时也在农场干活，用自己的双手弥补它没有手的缺陷。</p>
<p>喝完茶后，我帮她洗碗。天狼星一直在旁边晃来晃去；我想，它大概是在嫉妒我拥有一双这么好用的手。收拾完后，她说他们必须去农场，把天黑前必须完成的一项工作做完。我决定徒步回费斯蒂尼奥格取行李，再坐傍晚的火车返回特劳斯瓦尼兹；当地的小酒馆里应该能找到住处。我说到这里时，注意到天狼星的尾巴垂了下来。等我宣布还打算在附近住上一个星期，希望能多见普拉克茜几次时，它的尾巴垂得更低了。她说：“我会很忙，不过晚上可以见面。”</p>
<p>临走前，她交给我一摞文件，让我带回去慢慢看。其中有她父亲的科学论文，也有记录天狼星成长和教育过程的日记。后来很久以后，她还给了我自己的日记，以及天狼星本人写下的若干简短、零碎的记录。所有这些材料，再加上我和普拉克茜的许多长谈，以及等我学会理解天狼星的语言之后和它本人的许多谈话，便构成了下面这篇叙事的主要“资料来源”。</p>
<p>对于那些资料只留下极其粗略轮廓的事件，我打算大胆使用想象，把细节补充完整。毕竟，我虽是个公务员——至少在空军把我吸收进去之前如此——同时也是一个小说家。我相信，只要有想象力，再辅以自我批评，即使掌握的资料很表面，人也常常能够深入把握事件真正的内在精神。因此，关于天狼星这个惊人的故事，我将用自己的方式来讲。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a568"></span>CHAPTER I</h3>
<p><span class="t28">P</span><span class="t30">LAXY </span><span class="t31">AND</span><span class="t30"> I </span>had been lovers; rather uneasy lovers, for she would never speak freely about her past, and sometimes she withdrew into a cloud of reserve and despond. But often we were very happy together, and I believed that our happiness was striking deeper roots.</p>
<p>Then came her mother’s last illness, and Plaxy vanished. Once or twice I received a letter from her, giving no address, but suggesting that I might reply to her “care of the Post Office” in a village in North Wales, sometimes one, sometimes another. In temper these letters ranged from a perfunctory amiability to genuine longing to have me again. They contained mysterious references to “a strange duty,” which, she said, was connected with her father’s work. The great physiologist, I knew, had been engaged on very sensational experiments on the brains of the higher mammals. He had produced some marvellously intelligent sheep-dogs, and at the time of his death it was said that he was concerned with even more ambitious research. One of the colder of Plaxy’s letters spoke of an “unexpectedly sweet reward” in connection with her new duty, but in a more passionate one she cried out against “this exacting, fascinating, dehumanizing life.” Sometimes she seemed to be in a state of conflict and torture about something which she must not explain. One of these letters was so distraught that I feared for her sanity. I determined therefore to devote my approaching leave to walking in North Wales in the hope of finding her.</p>
<p>I spent ten days wandering from pub to pub in the region indicated by the addresses, asking everywhere if a Miss Trelone was known in the neighbourhood. At last, in Llan Ffestiniog, I heard of her. There was a young lady of that name living in a shepherd’s cottage on the fringe of the moor somewhere above Trawsfynydd. The local shopkeeper who gave me this information said with an air of mystery, “She is a strange young lady, indeed. She has friends, and I am one of them; but she has enemies.”</p>
<p>Following his directions, I walked for some miles along the winding Trawsfynydd road and then turned to the left up a lane. After another mile or so, right on the edge of the open moor, I came upon a minute cottage built of rough slabs of shale, and surrounded by a little garden and stunted trees. The door was shut, but smoke rose from a chimney. I knocked. The door remained shut. Peering through a window, I saw a typical cottage kitchen, but on the table was a pile of books. I sat down on a rickety seat in the garden and noted the neat rows of cabbages and peas. Away to my right, across the deep Cynfal gorge, was Ffestiniog, a pack of slate-grey elephants following their leader, the unsteepled church, down a spur of hill towards the valley. Behind and above stood the Moelwyn range.</p>
<p>I was smoking my second cigarette when I heard Plaxy’s voice in the distance. It was her voice that had first attracted me to her. Sitting in a café I had been enthralled by that sensitive human sound coming from some unknown person behind me. And now once more I heard but did not see her. For a moment I listened with delight to her speech, which, as I had often said, was like the cool sparkling talk of small waves on the pebbly shore of a tarn on a hot day.</p>
<p>I rose to meet her, but something strange arrested me. Interspersed with Plaxy’s remarks was no other human voice but a quite different sound, articulate but inhuman. Just before she came round the corner of the house she said, “But, my dear, don’t dwell on your handlessness so! You have triumphed over it superbly.” There followed a strange trickle of speech from her companion; then through the gate into the garden came Plaxy and a large dog.</p>
<p>She halted, her eyes wide with surprise, and (I hoped) with joy; but her brows soon puckered. Laying a hand on the dog’s head, she stood silent for a moment. I had time to observe that a change had come over her. She was wearing rather muddy corduroy trousers and a blue shirt. The same grey eyes, the same ample but decisive mouth, which had recently seemed to me to belie her character, the same shock of auburn, faintly carroty hair. But instead of a rather pale face, a ruddy brown one, and a complete absence of make-up. No lip-stick, even. The appearance of rude health was oddly contradicted by a darkness under the eyes and a tautness round the mouth. Strange how much one can notice in a couple of seconds, when one is in love!</p>
<p>Her hand deserted the dog’s head, and was stretched out to me in welcome. “Oh well,” she said smiling, “since you have nosed us out, we had better take you into our confidence.” There was some embarrassment in her tone, but also perhaps a ring of relief. “Hadn’t we, Sirius,” she added, looking down at the great dog.</p>
<p>Then for the first time I took note of this remarkable creature. He was certainly no ordinary dog. In the main he was an Alsatian, perhaps with a dash of Great Dane or Mastiff, for he was a huge beast. His general build was wolf-like, but he was slimmer than a wolf, because of his height. His coat, though the hair was short, was superbly thick and silky, particularly round the neck, where it was a close turbulent ruff. Its silkiness missed effeminacy by a hint of stubborn harshness. Silk wire, Plaxy once called it. On back and crown it was black, but on flanks and legs and the under surface of his body it paled to an austere greyish fawn. There were also two large patches of fawn above the eyes, giving his face a strangely mask-like look, or the appearance of a Greek statue with blank-eyed helmet pushed back from the face. What distinguished Sirius from all other dogs was his huge cranium. It was not, as a matter of fact, quite as large as one would have expected in a creature of human intelligence, since, as I shall explain later, Trelone’s technique not only increased the brain’s bulk but also produced a refinement of the nerve-fibres themselves. Nevertheless, Sirius’s head was far loftier than any normal dog’s. His high brow combined with the silkiness of his coat to give him a look of the famous Border Collie, the outstanding type of sheep-dog. I learned later that this brilliant race had, indeed, contributed to his make-up. But his cranium was far bigger than the Border Collie’s. The dome reached almost up to the tips of his large, pointed Alsatian ears. To hold up this weight of head, the muscles of his neck and shoulders were strongly developed. At the moment of our encounter he was positively leonine, because the hair was bristling along his spine. Suspicion of me had brushed it up the wrong way. His grey eyes might have been wolf’s eyes, had not the pupils been round like any dog’s, not slits like the wolf’s. Altogether he was certainly a formidable beast, lean and sinewy as a creature of the jungle.</p>
<p>Without taking his gaze off me, he opened his mouth, displaying sierras of ivory, and made a queer noise, ending with an upward inflection like a question. Plaxy replied, “Yes, it’s Robert. He’s true as steel, remember.” She smiled at me deprecatingly, and added, “And he may be useful.”</p>
<p>Sirius politely waved his amply feathered tail, but kept his cold eyes fixed on mine.</p>
<p>Another awkward pause settled upon us, till Plaxy said, “We have been working on the sheep out on the moor all day. We missed our dinner and I’m hungry as hell. Come in and I’ll make tea for us all.” She added as we entered the little flagged kitchen, “Sirius will understand everything you say. You won’t be able to understand him at first, but I shall, and I’ll interpret.”</p>
<p>While Plaxy prepared a meal, passing in and out of the little larder, I sat talking to her. Sirius squatted opposite me, eyeing me with obvious anxiety. Seeing him, she said with a certain sharpness fading into gentleness, “Sirius! I tell you he’s all right. Don’t be so suspicious!” The dog rose, saying something in his strange lingo, and went out into the garden. “He’s gone to fetch some firewood,” she said; then in a lowered voice, “Oh, Robert, it’s good to see you, though I didn’t want you to find me.” I rose to take her in my arms, but she whispered emphatically, “No, no, not now.” Sirius returned with a log between his jaws. With a sidelong glance at the two of us, and a perceptible drooping of the tail, he put the log on the fire and went out again. “Why not now?” I cried, and she whispered, “Because of Sirius. Oh, you’ll understand soon.” After a pause she added, “Robert, you mustn’t expect me to be wholly yours ever, not fully and single-heartedly yours. I’m too much involved in — in this work of my father’s.” I expostulated, and seized her. “Nice human Robert,” she sighed, putting her head on my shoulder. But immediately she broke away, and said with emphasis, “No, I didn’t say that. It was just the female human animal that said it. What I say is, I can’t play the game you want me to play, not wholeheartedly.”</p>
<p>Then she called through the open door, “Sirius, tea!” He replied with a bark, then strode in, carefully not looking at me.</p>
<p>She put a bowl of tea for him on a little table-cloth on the floor, remarking, “He has two meals generally, dinner at noon and supper in the evening. But to-day is different.” Then she put down a large crust of bread, a hunk of cheese, and a saucer with a little lump of jam. “Will that keep you going?” she asked. A grunt signified approval.</p>
<p>Plaxy and I sat at the table to eat our bread and rationed butter and war-time cake. She set about telling me the history of Sirius. Sometimes I put in an occasional question, or Sirius interrupted with his queer speech of whimper and growl.</p>
<p>The matter of this and many other conversations about the past I shall set down in the following chapters. Meanwhile I must say this. Without the actual presence of Sirius I should not have believed the story; but his interruptions, though canine and unintelligible, expressed human intelligence by their modulation, and stimulated intelligible answers from Plaxy. Obviously he was following the conversation, commenting and watching my reaction. And so it was not with incredulity, though of course with amazement, that I learned of the origin and career of Sirius. I listened at first with grave anxiety, so deeply involved was Plaxy. I began to understand why it was that our love had always been uneasy, and why when her mother died she did not come back to me. I began to debate with myself the best way of freeing her from this “inhuman bondage”. But as the conversation proceeded I could not but recognize that this strange relationship of girl and dog was fundamentally beautiful, in a way sacred. (That was the word I used to myself.) Thus my problem became far more difficult.</p>
<p>At one point, when Plaxy had been saying that she often longed to see me again, Sirius made a more sustained little speech. And in the middle of it he went over to her, put his fore-paws oh the arm of her chair, and with great gentleness and delicacy kissed her cheek. She took the caress demurely, not shrinking away, as human beings generally do when dogs try to kiss them. But the healthy glow of her face deepened, and there was moisture in her eyes as she stroked the shaggy softness under his neck, and said to me, while still looking at him, “I am to tell you, Robert, that Sirius and Plaxy grew up together like the thumb and forefinger of a hand, that he loves me in the way that only dogs can love, and much more now that I have come to him, but that I must not feel bound to stay with him, because by now he can fend for himself. Whatever happens to him ever, I — how did you say it, Sirius, you foolish dear?” He put in a quick sentence, and she continued, “Oh, yes, I am the scent that he will follow always, hunting for God.”</p>
<p>She turned her face towards me with a smile that I shall not forget. Nor shall I forget the bewildering effect of the dog’s earnest and almost formal little declaration. Later I was to realize that a rather stilted diction was very characteristic of him, in moments of deep feeling.</p>
<p>Then Sirius made another remark with a sly look and a tremor of the tail. She turned back to him laughing, and softly smacked his face. “Beast,” she said, “I shall not tell Robert that.”</p>
<p>When Sirius kissed her I was startled into a sudden spasm of jealousy. (A man jealous of a dog!) But Plaxy’s translation of his little speech roused more generous feelings. I now began to make plans by which Plaxy and I together might give Sirius a permanent home and help him to fulfil his destiny, whatever that might be. But, as I shall tell, a different fate lay in store for us.</p>
<p>During that strange meal Plaxy told me that, as I had guessed, Sirius was her father’s crowning achievement, that he had been brought up as a member of the Trelone family, that he was now helping to run a sheep farm, that she herself was keeping house for him, and also working on the farm, compensating for his lack of hands.</p>
<p>After tea I helped her to wash up, while Sirius hovered about, jealous, I think, of my handiness. When we had finished, she said they must go over to the farm to complete a job of work before dark. I decided to walk back to Ffestiniog, collect my baggage and return by the evening train to Trawsfynedd, where I could find accommodation in the local pub. I noticed Sirius’s tail droop as I said this. It drooped still further when I announced that I proposed to spend a week in the neighbourhood in the hope of seeing more of Plaxy. She said, “I shall be busy, but there are the evenings.”</p>
<p>Before I left she handed over a collection of documents for me to take away and read at leisure. There were scientific papers by her father, including his journal of Sirius’s growth and education. These documents, together with a diary of her own and brief fragmentary records by Sirius himself, all of which I was given at a much later date, form the main “sources” of the following narrative; these, and many long talks with Plaxy, and with Sirius when I had learnt to understand his speech.</p>
<p>I propose to use my imagination freely to fill out with detail many incidents about which my sources afford only the barest outline. After all, though a civil servant (until the Air Force absorbed me) I am also a novelist; and I am convinced that with imagination and self-criticism one can often penetrate into the essential spirit of events even when the data are superficial. I shall, therefore, tell the amazing story of Sirius in my own way.</p>
