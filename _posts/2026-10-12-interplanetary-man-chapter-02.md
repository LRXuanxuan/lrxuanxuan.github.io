<h3>二、如果其他行星上已有居民</h3>
<p>在人类历史这个极其关键的时刻，人类发现自己已经站到一种新自由的门槛上：越出地球大气层，在整个太阳系中旅行和探索的自由。面对这种新力量，人类应该做什么？</p>
<p>很大程度上，这取决于人类到访的行星究竟是什么情况。必须考虑两种可能：人类要么会在太阳系其他地方发现别的智慧生命，要么不会。如果发现了，那么又有两种可能。第一种是世界之间爆发战争。面对共同危险，这种局面或许至少能暂时促成人类统一，就像俄国与西方曾因希特勒而联合起来一样。世界大战的结局，要么是人类战败毁灭，要么是人类胜利，继而为了自身进步而剥削被征服的种族。第二种可能是行星际合作。然而，考虑到我们甚至连自己的人类世界都可悲地无法统一，人类以当前状态与其他行星的异族进行合作，似乎极不可能。更可能的是，地球上彼此竞争的帝国主义和意识形态会把斗争扩展到其他世界，在那里压迫并最终毁灭当地居民。反过来，如果人类不久之后确实能够统一自己的世界社会，那么至少可以设想，与其他行星上的智慧种族建立某种互利共生关系。</p>
<p>不过总体看来，太阳系其他世界里似乎不大可能存在任何在智力上接近人类的种族。请诸位容我概括一下现有资料；如果我说错了，稍后请纠正。先从最近的邻居月球开始。据我所知，它几乎完全没有大气和水。根本没有理由认为那里现在或过去曾有智慧居民。再看行星。水星的一面过热，另一面又过冷。金星温和一些，而且大气浓厚；但显然缺少氧气，无法供我们地球上所知的生命生存，也可能缺水。火星因为引力微弱，已经失去了大部分大气和水。事实上，它的极冠最后也许会发现是二氧化碳雪，或者更糟的东西。不过，有相当证据表明火星上存在某种植被，却没有任何令人信服的证据表明那里存在智慧种族留下的人工制品。火星上的生物进化过程或许比地球缓慢，因为一般来说，地理舞台越大，产生丰富变异、供自然选择发挥作用的机会就越多。此外，火星环境迅速恶化，可能很早就截断或严重拖慢了进化过程。因此总体而言，火星生命——如果与我们所知生命相似——似乎不太可能进化到人类水平。小行星当然又太小。另一方面，木星、土星以及大概所有外行星都太大，而且它们的大气组成显然完全不适合地球型生命。</p>
<p>当然，我们不能彻底排除这样的可能：某些行星上的生命以不同的化学基础进化，对我们致命的大气，却可能适合与我们不同的生化过程。据我理解，地球生命依赖碳化合物无与伦比的多样性；虽然以碳以外某种元素为基础的生化系统并非绝无可能，但它能够形成的化合物范围会小得多，因而生物进化的空间也会受到很大限制。不过，我们仍不应该完全排除这样一种可能：木星或某个其他巨行星上生活着微小的智慧生物，其构成对我们全然陌生。证据不支持这种看法，但也没有强烈到足以彻底排除它。</p>
<p>说到这里，我忍不住要离题一下，纵容自己作一次最狂野的幻想。从根本上说，生命似乎是在利用熵增加这一普遍过程中的某种特定形式，为维持生命活动获取能量。生命从浩荡的能量洪流中截取并导引一部分，形成一条“磨坊水渠”，推动自己那只小小的“水轮”。那么，会不会存在并非以化学反应为基础，而以原子解体所释放的能量为基础的生命？会不会不是在任何行星上，而是在太阳动荡的外层，甚至太阳内部较深的区域，存在这种火焰般的有机体？其中某些物种的智力，会不会达到甚至超过人类？这当然已经公然偏离本文的主题，因为人类看来根本不可能乘着飞船侵入太阳，与这种炽热生物建立接触。</p>
<p>回到正题，太阳系中除人类以外，似乎极不可能存在其他智慧种族。火星是唯一一个让我们对此结论略有犹豫的行星，但即便在那里，出现高度发展生命的赔率也明显很低。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" lang="en" hidden>
<h3><a id="a1084"></a>II. If the Planets are Inhabited</h3>
<p><span class="t28">A</span><span class="t31">T</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">THIS</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">MOST</span><span class="t30"> </span>critical moment of human history man finds himself on the very threshold of a new freedom, the freedom to travel beyond the terrestrial atmosphere and explore the whole solar system. What should he do with this new power?</p>
<p>Much depends on the conditions of the planets that he visits. Two possibilities must be noted. Either man will find elsewhere in the solar system other intelligences, or he will not. If he does, then again there are two possibilities. One is the possibility of wars between the worlds. This situation might perhaps cause at least a temporary unification of mankind in face of the common danger, much as Russia and the West united against Hitler. The War of Worlds would be followed either by man’s defeat and ruin or by his victory, and then by exploitation of the conquered races for man’s advancement. The other possibility is interplanetary co-operation. But in view of our sad inability even to unite mankind, it seems extremely unlikely that man in his present state would succeed in co-operating with alien races on other planets. Far more likely is it that the rival imperialisms and ideologies of this planet would be extended to struggle against each other in other worlds, tyrannizing over and ultimately destroying the native peoples. If, on the other hand, man does soon succeed in unifying his world-society, then it is at least conceivable that some kind of mutually profitable symbiosis with intelligent races on other planets might be established.</p>
<p>On the whole, however, it seems unlikely that any of the other worlds within the solar system is inhabited by any race even approaching man in intelligence. Bear with me while I summarize the data, and correct me later if I am mistaken. Let us begin with our nearest neighbour, the moon. I am told that it is almost wholly without atmosphere and water. There seems to be no reason at all for supposing that it has, or ever did have, intelligent inhabitants. Of the planets themselves, Mercury is far too hot on one side and far too cold on the other. Venus is more temperate, and has a copious atmosphere; but apparently it lacks oxygen, and would not support life such as we know on our own planet. Water also may be lacking. Mars, owing to its feeble gravitation, has already lost most of its atmosphere and most of its water. Indeed, its polar caps may turn out to be composed of carbon dioxide snow, or something worse. However, there is considerable evidence that vegetation of some sort does exist on Mars; but certainly no convincing evidence of the artifacts of an intelligent race. Probably the process of biological evolution on the planet was less rapid than that of the earth, since on the whole, the larger the geographical field, the greater the chance of the occurrence of a wealth of varieties on which natural selection could work. Further, the evolutionary process was probably cut short or greatly retarded by the rapid deterioration of conditions. On the whole, then, it seems unlikely that on Mars life (such as we know) has evolved to the human level. The asteroids are of course much too small. On the other hand, Jupiter and Saturn, and probably all the outer planets, are too big, and have apparently quite the wrong kind of atmosphere for life of the terrestrial type.</p>
<p>Of course, we cannot entirely reject the possibility that on some of the planets life has evolved on a different chemical basis, and that atmospheres lethal to us may be hospitable to biochemical processes alien to ours. But I understand that terrestrial life depends on the unique diversity of the carbon compounds, and that, though a biochemical system based on some element other than carbon is not possible, it would have a far smaller range of compounds, and so the scope of its biological evolution would be very restricted. However, we should not dismiss the possibility that Jupiter or some other great planet is inhabited by minute intelligent creatures whose constitution is quite unknown to us. The evidence is opposed to this view, but not overwhelmingly.</p>
<p>At this point I cannot resist a digression, and indulgence in the wildest fantasy. Fundamentally life seems to consist of a utilizing of some particular form of the general process of the increase of entropy so as to gather power for the maintenance of vital activities. Life taps and canalizes part of the vast spate of energy, thus forming a “mill-stream” by which its own little “water-wheel” may be worked. Well, might there not be living creatures based not on chemical action but on the energy released by the disintegration of atoms? Might there not be, not on any planet but in the sun’s turbulent outer layers or in its middle depths, flame-like organisms of this type, and might not some species of them equal or excel man in intelligence? This is indeed a flagrant digression from the purpose of this paper, for it seems quite impossible that man should ever in his space-ships invade the sun and make contact with such incandescent beings.</p>
<p>To return to our subject, it seems very unlikely that there is any intelligent race anywhere in the solar system, except mankind. Mars is the only planet that should make us hesitate to accept this conclusion, but in this case too the betting is heavily against any highly developed life.</p>
</section>
