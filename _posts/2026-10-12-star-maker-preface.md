<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="zh" lang="zh-CN" aria-label="中文译文">
<p>《造星主》是我读过的最奇妙的小说。一次次重读，它的奇妙并不消退，而且越是细读，越有所获。</p>
<p>最简单地说，这本书讲的是一次追寻；但这追寻发生在宇宙尺度、超越人类的尺度上。书中的“我”让自己的精神离开地球，离开“世界的癫狂”，投向宇宙，去遭遇别的世界、别的生命、别的生命类型，最终直面宇宙本身的创造者——造星主，并获准与之交流。没有什么故事比这更简单——也没有什么企图比这更宏大。</p>
<p>在《造星主》最初的序言中，作者声称这根本不算一部小说。的确，它更像是在尝试以尽可能宏大的尺度创造一则神话——一则属于我们时代、能够同时安顿我们内心的科学家与神秘主义者的神话。也就是说，一则写给人类那颗分裂大脑两个半球的神话。正是这种尝试本身的高贵，使《造星主》区别于它表面上所近似的现代小说和科学幻想小说。它既要教诲，也要娱乐；既要想象，也要综合。</p>
<p>不难想见，能写出这样一本惊人之作的作者本人，也多少带着几分神秘。威廉·奥拉夫·斯特普尔顿（1886—1950）曾被一些批评家描绘成一个软弱无力的人。我却认为恰恰相反：他是个性格鲜明的人，年轻时在牛津还是一名运动员；而仅凭他敢于着手完成眼前这样一项浩大的事业，也足以证明他的勇气。他或许有些古怪之处，但街头随便抓来的一个普通人，怎么可能写出这样的作品！</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿是英国人，童年时光一半在英国、一半在埃及度过，他的父亲当时受雇于一家航运公司。这一点点流寓异乡的经历，或许促成了他作品中那种极为显眼的疏离感。他的第一部长篇作品（当然，“小说”一词在这里仍然未必准确）是《最后和最初的人》，1930年在伦敦出版。</p>
<p>那本书赢得了评论界热烈的赞誉，至今仍是斯特普尔顿最著名的作品。然而，就连《最后和最初的人》的接受史，也和斯特普尔顿本人的声誉一样飘忽不定——是那些世俗的幻想家、科学幻想小说读者，把它保存了下来。它一度淡出人们视野，直到1963年，正是本序作者说服企鹅图书再版此书，并为之撰写导言，它才重新受到关注。《最后和最初的人》写的是未来五十亿年的历史，堪称迄今最彻底、最令人惊异的未来史。然而七年后《造星主》问世，它无论在技艺还是规模上都更胜一筹。《造星主》的主题更宏伟，文体更圆熟，处理更微妙——更不用说，它的想象力更有压倒性的力量。</p>
<p>至于斯特普尔顿的勇气，至少有一件事实足以说明：第一次世界大战期间，他在佛兰德斯的泥泞与污秽中担任救护车司机，并因此获颁法国战争十字勋章。救护车司机对他而言倒是再合适不过：一个倾向和平主义的人，同时又觉得自己不得不参与那场可怕的斗争——正是在那场斗争中，旧世纪与新世纪被不可逆转地斩断。</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿性格中的疏离并非出于他自己的选择；无论这种特质在日常生活中给他带来多少困难，它都塑造了后来那个作家。战争会破坏文明——而它确实如此——这件事令他深感恐惧；但他也明白，战争给了他一个机会，让他能够与同伴一道工作。在那部古怪的《最后和最初的人》续作《伦敦的最后人》中，斯特普尔顿让主人公保罗说，自己参加战争，是为了“尽可能一方面接受那巨大的共同痛苦，另一方面接受那些无法与同伴分享最深激情之人的私人孤独”。《造星主》里同样挤满了巨大的共同痛苦与私人的孤独——而且那种私人孤独，往往属于整个太阳系共同体。</p>
<p>与孤独相近的疯狂，也常常萦绕在斯特普尔顿心头。一个又一个挥霍般铺展的星系，无穷尽的远景，足以威胁人的理智：</p>
<p>当我们在时间与空间中上下搜寻，发现越来越多那种名为行星的稀有尘粒时，我们看见一个又一个种族挣扎着达到某种清醒的意识程度，却又因外在事故，或更常见地，因自身天性中的某种缺陷而毁灭。于是，一种宇宙徒劳无功、毫无计划的感觉越来越沉重地压在我们心上。</p>
<p>这种阴郁情绪深受叔本华的悲观主义以及“上帝已死，或上帝只是幻觉”这一信念影响；它更像十九世纪后期那些高层次思想家的气质，而非二十世纪乐观技术迷的气质。不过，有时我们也会想起，早在极早的年代，斯特普尔顿就已经探索了后来从E. E. 史密斯等人起，雄心勃勃的科幻作家们反复使用的一项老题材：交战中的银河帝国。</p>
<p>这些帝国在彼此遥远的区域萌生，很轻易便征服了势力范围内一切尚未达到乌托邦阶段的世界。于是它们从一个行星系扩张到另一个行星系，直到终于有一个帝国与另一个帝国相遇。</p>
<p>接着爆发了我们银河系前所未有的战争。由天然世界与人工世界组成的舰队在群星之间机动，彼此斗智，并用远程的亚原子能射流相互毁灭。随着战争潮汐在空间中来回摆动，整个又整个行星系被彻底抹去。许多世界精神猝然终结。许多与战争毫无关系的低等种族，也被环绕其世界肆虐的天体战争屠戮。</p>
<p>不过，若说斯特普尔顿那势不可挡的事件洪流与传统科幻有什么相似之处，也纯属偶合。斯特普尔顿的想象力近乎无所不食。那不断追寻的精神与巨大星云的心灵交谈之后，又继续前进，终于与造星主本身建立交流。全书那被精妙维持到最后的高潮，是造物者描述自己正在试验的一连串宇宙，而我们的宇宙不过是近乎无穷系列中的一个。在整个英语散文传统中，又到哪里去寻找可与壮丽的第十五章相比之作呢？</p>
<p>一篇导言若继续替读者罗列书中种种惊奇，就未免说得太多了。不过，有一点值得指出，它解释了为何如此多读者无法坦然直面斯特普尔顿：他的挑战性太强，令人难以安适。具有科学头脑的人不信任作品中的敬畏感；宗教人士则会畏缩于这样一种造物主观念——他既不爱自己的造物，也不需要造物的爱。在我看来，正是这种毫不妥协的立场构成了此书的乐趣，也证明了斯特普尔顿的勇气。正如他笔下的造星主一样，斯特普尔顿也向读者灌输一种类似的独立。他并不请求你去爱他。他也不爱你。他的作品就在那里。你愿意怎样接受，便怎样接受。</p>
<p>正因为这种令人钦佩的帕纳索斯式超然姿态，斯特普尔顿对其他作家的直接影响反而不算多。一个作家若要显出斯特普尔顿的影响，往往得在年轻时便吸收过他，并在后来的写作中表现出这样一种感觉：宇宙过程对人类的野心漠不关心。阿瑟·C. 克拉克承认自己受过斯特普尔顿影响，我们完全可以相信。世界大战进行期间，我也很早便读到他的作品；当时，那不动声色的声音带来一种清醒本身所具有的震撼。（《银河如沙砾》便是一部仿斯特普尔顿之作。）</p>
<p>塑造斯特普尔顿独特话语方式的作家，多半应是哲学与政治写作者。当然，还有比他年长二十岁的H. G. 威尔斯。除此之外，也许还可列出但丁以及《失乐园》的作者。不过，最明显的影响似乎来自温伍德·里德——那位一度声名显赫的《人之殉难》作者。此书出版于1872年，此后八十年间不断再版。里德的作品自由穿梭于整个人类历史，同时兼具科学与政治视野。威尔斯称它是“一次极富鼓舞力的呈现：把人类历史写成一个连贯一致的过程”。正是这种把生命视作过程的观点吸引了斯特普尔顿，也吸引了威尔斯。</p>
<p>《人之殉难》在当年激起过强烈敌意，主要原因在于它对宗教的攻击。里德确实把上帝“摆回了他的位置”，如下这段文字便很能说明问题：</p>
<p>诸神在数量上的安排，完全取决于有关民族的智力水平。在所谓的“物崇拜”时期，每一条溪流、每一棵树、每一座山、每一颗星本身都是活物，或仁慈或恶毒，或沉睡或清醒。到了下一阶段，每一个物体和现象都被认为有精灵或神灵居住其中或主持其事；在有些民族中，德性与恶习甚至也被赋予人格。随着人的推理能力扩展，他们的神数量减少，却统治越来越广大的区域；直到最后，人们意识到自然中存在统一性，意识到一切存在之物都是一个和谐整体的一部分。于是，人们宣称有一个存在制造了世界，并对它实行至高统治。然而最初，这个伟大存在遥远而冷淡，是“一个坐在宇宙之外的神”；旧日诸神则成了他的总督，由他委任去治理世界。后来这些神又被降格为使者或天使，人们开始相信上帝无所不在，充满天地，恶与善都直接由他而来。不过在某些信仰体系中，人们认为他只是善的作者，而把恶的领域交给一个叛逆的天使，或另一个与他竞争的神。</p>
<p>我在这里引这段文字，不仅因为我希望找到更多人来分享我对温伍德·里德的热情，也因为你几乎可以想见奥拉夫·斯特普尔顿读到这种“给神明降级”的论述时，会如何会心发笑。</p>
<p>斯特普尔顿把这个念头推进得更远。远上好几个光年。</p>
<p>无论它究竟是什么——小说、神话、散文诗、幻景——《造星主》至今仍领先我们若干光年，仍是我们其余人尚在前往之处。</p>
<p>布赖恩·W. 奥尔迪斯</p>
</section>
<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="en" lang="en" aria-label="English original" hidden>
<p>AT A MOMENT when Europe is in danger of a catastrophe worse than that of 1914 a book like this may be condemned as a distraction from the desperately urgent defence of civilization against modern barbarism.</p>
<p>Year by year, month by month, the plight of our fragmentary and precarious civilization becomes more serious. Fascism abroad grows more bold and ruthless in its foreign ventures, more tyrannical toward its own citizens, more barbarian in its contempt for the life of the mind. Even in our own country we have reason to fear a tendency toward militarization and the curtailment of civil liberty. Moreover, while the decades pass, no resolute step is taken to alleviate the injustice of our social order. Our outworn economic system dooms millions to frustration.</p>
<p>In these conditions it is difficult for writers to pursue their calling at once with courage and with balanced judgement. Some merely shrug their shoulders and withdraw from the central struggle of our age. These, with their minds closed against the world’s most vital issues, inevitably produce works which not only have no depth of significance for their contemporaries but also are subtly insincere. For these writers must consciously or unconsciously contrive to persuade themselves either that the crisis in human affairs does not exist, or that it is less important than their own work, or that it is anyhow not their business. But the crisis does exist, is of supreme importance, and concerns us all. Can anyone who is at all intelligent and informed hold the contrary without self-deception?</p>
<p>Yet I have a lively sympathy with some of those ‘intellectuals’ who declare that they have no useful contribution to make to the struggle, and therefore had better not dabble in it. I am, in fact, one of them. In our defence I should say that, though we are inactive or ineffective as direct supporters of the cause, we do not ignore it. Indeed, it constantly, obsessively, holds our attention. But we are convinced by prolonged trial and error that the most useful service open to us is indirect. For some writers the case is different. Gallantly plunging into the struggle, they use their powers to spread urgent propaganda, or they even take up arms in the cause. If they have suitable ability, and if the particular struggle in which they serve is in fact a part of the great enterprise of defending (or creating) civilization, they may, of course, do valuable work. In addition they may gain great wealth of experience and human sympathy, thereby immensely increasing their literary power. But the very urgency of their service may tend to blind them to the importance of maintaining and extending, even in this age of crisis, what may be called metaphorically the ‘self-critical self-consciousness of the human species’, or the attempt to see man’s life as a whole in relation to the rest of things. This involves the will to regard all human affairs and ideals and theories with as little human prejudice as possible. Those who are in the thick of the struggle inevitably tend to become, though in a great and just cause, partisan. They nobly forgo something of that detachment, that power of cold assessment, which is, after all, among the most valuable human capacities. In their case this is perhaps as it should be; for a desperate struggle demands less of detachment than of devotion. But some who have the cause at heart must serve by striving to maintain, along with human loyalty, a more dispassionate spirit. And perhaps the attempt to see our turbulent world against a background of stars may, after all, increase, not lessen, the significance of the present human crisis. It may also strengthen our charity toward one another.</p>
<p>In this belief I have tried to construct an imaginative sketch of the dread but vital whole of things. I know well that it is a ludicrously inadequate and in some ways a childish sketch, even when regarded from the angle of contemporary human experience. In a calmer and a wiser age it might well seem crazy. Yet in spite of its crudity, and in spite of its remoteness, it is perhaps not wholly irrelevant.</p>
<p>At the risk of raising thunder both on the Left and on the Right, I have occasionally used certain ideas and words derived from religion, and I have tried to interpret them in relation to modern needs. The valuable, though much damaged words ‘spiritual’ and ‘worship’, which have become almost as obscene to the Left as the good old sexual words are to the Right, are here intended to suggest an experience which the Right is apt to pervert and the Left to misconceive. This experience, I should say, involves detachment from all private, all social, all racial ends; not in the sense that it leads a man to reject them, but that it makes him prize them in a new way. The ‘spiritual life’ seems to be in essence the attempt to discover and adopt the attitude which is in fact appropriate to our experience as a whole, just as admiration is felt to be in fact appropriate toward a well-grown human being. This enterprise can lead to an increased lucidity and finer temper of consciousness, and therefore can have a great and beneficial effect on behaviour. Indeed, if this supremely humanizing experience does not produce, along with a kind of piety toward fate, the resolute will to serve our waking humanity, it is a mere sham and a snare.</p>
<p>Before closing this preface I must express my gratitude to Professor L. C. Martin, Mr. L. H. Myers, and Mr. E. V. Rieu, for much helpful and sympathetic criticism, in consequence of which I re-wrote many chapters. Even now I hesitate to associate their names with such an extravagant work. Judged by the standards of the Novel, it is remarkably bad. In fact, it is no novel at all.</p>
<p>Certain ideas about artificial planets were suggested by Mr. J. D. Bernal’s fascinating little book The World, the Flesh, and the Devil. I hope he will not strongly disapprove of my treatment of them.</p>
<p>My wife I must thank both for work on the proofs and for being herself.</p>
<p>At the end of the book I have included a note on Magnitude, which may be helpful to readers unfamiliar with astronomy. The very sketchy time scales may amuse some.</p>
<p>O. S.</p>
<p>March 1937</p>
