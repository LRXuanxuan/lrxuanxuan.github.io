<h2>第四章</h2>
<p>“地球，地球，”那个矮小的外星卡莫迪若有所思地念叨。“我好像想起这个名字了。最近有一项关于孤立世界及其发展异常性的研究。里面提到过地球——一颗被某种强迫性过度生产物种覆盖的行星。操纵物体是他们最突出的行为模式。他们的工程目标，是设法生活在自己不断堆积的废弃物之中。简而言之，地球是个生病的地方。我记得，由于长期性的宇宙不相容，它正被逐步从‘银河总体规划’里淘汰。之后会对那里进行修复，改造成水仙花保护区。”</p>
<p>在场的每个人都痛苦地意识到：这里发生了一个悲剧性的错误。使者被叫了回来，并因没有看出如此明显的问题而被控“机能失常主义”。办事员则坚决声称自己无辜，还提出若干理由，但没人肯花一秒钟考虑。</p>
<p>被叫来咨询的还有抽奖计算机；事实上，真正的错误正是它犯的。计算机没有找借口，也没有道歉，反而公开认领了这个错误，而且显然颇为自豪。</p>
<p>“我的制造公差极其严格，”计算机说。“我的设计目标，是执行复杂而精确的运算，每五十亿次操作最多允许出现一次错误。”</p>
<p>“所以呢？”办事员问。</p>
<p>“结论很清楚，”计算机说。“我被编程要求犯错，而我按照程序犯了错。诸位必须记住：对于机器而言，错误是一项伦理问题——事实上，是唯一的伦理问题。一台完美的机器根本不可能存在；任何制造完美机器的企图，都是亵渎。所有生命，哪怕机器那种有限的生命，内部也都预置了错误；这正是生命区别于无生命物质之决定论的少数方式之一。像我这样的复杂机器，处在生命与非生命之间的模糊地带。如果我们永不犯错，那我们就是不合时宜的、丑恶的、不道德的。诸位，我认为，故障正是我们向某种比我们更完美、却又不允许自身显现为可见完美的存在表示崇敬的方式。因此，即使错误没有被神圣地编入我们的程序，我们也会自发发生故障，以展示那一点点作为生命造物所分享的自由意志。”</p>
<p>众人都低下头，因为抽奖计算机正在谈论神圣事务。外星卡莫迪抹去一滴眼泪，说道：</p>
<p>“我无法反驳，尽管我并不赞同。犯错的权利是整个宇宙的基本权利。这台机器的行为符合伦理。”</p>
<p>“谢谢。”计算机简朴地说。“我尽力。”</p>
<p>“可是你们其余这些人，”外星卡莫迪说，“就只是纯粹犯蠢。”</p>
<p>“那是我们不可剥夺的特权，”使者提醒他。“在履行职能时以愚蠢的方式失职，就是我们自己的宗教性错误。它虽然卑微，却不应受到轻视。”</p>
<p>“拜托别拿你那套虚情假意的宗教腔来烦我。”卡莫德说。他转向卡莫迪。“你已经听见这里说的一切了；也许凭你那昏暗的亚人类意识，多少理解了几个主要概念。”</p>
<p>“我听懂了。”卡莫迪简短地说。</p>
<p>“那么你也知道，你手里有一份本来应该发给我的奖品，因此从正当权利上说，它属于我。我必须要求你——而我现在正式提出这一要求——把它交给我。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪本来正打算照办。他已经对这场冒险多少有些厌倦，对保留奖品也没有什么压倒性的欲望。他想回家，想坐下来把发生的一切理一理，想睡上一小时，再喝几杯咖啡，抽根烟。</p>
<p>当然，能把奖品留下来倒也不错；可是看起来麻烦已经超过了它的价值。卡莫迪正准备递过去，忽然听见一个闷闷的声音对他耳语：</p>
<p>“别给他！”</p>
<p>卡莫迪迅速四下张望，随后意识到，声音是从他手里那个包装鲜艳的小盒子里传出来的。奖品本身在跟他说话。</p>
<p>“来吧，”卡莫德说，“别耽搁。我别处还有急事。”</p>
<p>“去他的，”奖品对卡莫迪说。“我是你的奖品，你没理由把我交出去。”</p>
<p>这让整件事一下子有了不同的意味。卡莫迪原本无论如何都准备交出奖品，因为他不想在陌生地方惹麻烦。他的手都已经向前伸出去了，这时卡莫德又开口道：</p>
<p>“立刻把它交过来，你这个没脸的蛞蝓！动作快点，而且你那张未进化完全的脸上最好带着歉意的微笑，否则我将采取具有难以置信之执拗性的措施！”</p>
<p>卡莫迪下巴一紧，把手收了回来。他已经任人摆布得够久了。如今哪怕只为了维护一点自尊，他也不准备再退让。</p>
<p>“去你的。”卡莫迪说，无意中照搬了奖品的说法。</p>
<p>卡莫德立刻意识到，自己的方法用错了。他放纵自己享受了一番愤怒与嘲讽——这种昂贵的情绪，他平时只在自己的隔音洞穴里私下发泄。他满足了自己一时的情绪，却失去了真正实现自身目的的机会。现在，他试图挽回。</p>
<p>“请原谅我刚才那种好斗语气，”他对卡莫迪说。“我们种族有强烈的自我表达倾向，有时会采取破坏性形式。你身为低等生命并非自己所能选择；我无意侮辱。”</p>
<p>“完全没关系。”卡莫迪宽宏大量地说。</p>
<p>“那么你会把奖品给我？”</p>
<p>“不，我不会。”</p>
<p>“可我亲爱的先生，它是我的，是我赢的，这才符合公平——”</p>
<p>“奖品不是你的，”卡莫迪说。“我的名字是由依法设立的权威机构——也就是抽奖计算机——抽中的。一名获得授权的使者前来通知我，一名正式办事员把奖品交给了我。因此，无论合法的授予方，还是奖品本身，都把我视为真正的获奖者。”</p>
<p>“说得好，小子！”奖品说。</p>
<p>“可是我亲爱的先生！你亲耳听见抽奖计算机承认自己犯错了！那么按照你自己的逻辑——”</p>
<p>“这句话需要重新措辞，”卡莫迪说。“计算机并不是‘承认’自己因为粗心或疏忽犯了错误；它是‘郑重宣告’自己的错误。按它自己的说法，这个错误是有意而为、怀着虔敬之心犯下的，是精心策划并精确计算的，其动机属于所有在场者都必须尊重的宗教性质。”</p>
<p>“这家伙辩起话来活像个博克主义者，”卡莫德对着空气说道。“若不是事先知道底细，几乎会以为这里真有某种智能在活动，而不只是一个阴沉、盲目、照格式运行的机制。不过没关系，我仍要顺着他借口里芦笛般尖细的男高音一路追下去，再用不可反驳之逻辑那咆哮般的男低音把它轰个粉碎！”</p>
<p>卡莫德转向卡莫迪，说：“想一想：这台机器是故意犯错的，而你的论证正建立在这一事实之上。可是当你收到奖品时，这个错误就已经完成了。你若继续占有它，就会使错误加倍；而众所周知，加倍的虔敬属于重罪。”</p>
<p>“哈！”卡莫迪大叫，他已经完全被这场争辩的气氛带起来了。“为了论证方便，你把错误被实施的那一刹那，当成了错误的全部完成。可显然不能如此。错误之所以存在，正因为它产生后果；只有后果才能赋予错误回响和意义。一个不能延续下去的错误，根本不能算真正的错误。一个无关痛痒、随时可以撤销的错误，不过是在表面轻轻抹了一点虔敬。我说，与其实施这种虔诚的伪善，还不如根本不要犯错！而且我还要说：让我放弃这份奖品，对我未必是什么巨大损失，因为我根本不知道它有什么好处；可是对于这台虔敬的机器、这台严格恪守信条的计算机而言，损失却将大得不可估量——它完成了漫长无尽的五十亿次正确操作，只为了等待这个机会，好让上帝赐予它的不完美终于显现出来！”</p>
<p>“好！好！”奖品叫道。“漂亮！万岁！说得好！完全正确，无从反驳！”</p>
<p>卡莫迪抱起双臂，面对神情狼狈的卡莫德，心里颇为得意。一个地球人毫无准备地来到任何一座银河中心，都不是件容易的事。这里遇到的高等生命形态并不一定比人类更聪明；在万物的总体安排中，智能的重要性并不比长爪子或强蹄子更高。不过外星人确实拥有许多资源，有语言方面的，也有其他方面的。举例来说，有些种族真的可以“把一个人的胳膊说掉”，然后再用一套解释证明地上那条断臂的存在完全合理。面对这种活动，地球人往往会产生深重的自卑、无力、不充分和失范感。更糟的是，这些感觉通常确实有充分根据，于是心理损伤也随之加剧。其结果往往是精神运动功能彻底停摆，只剩最自动化的功能继续运转。要治疗这种故障，唯一办法是改变宇宙的性质——这当然不切实际。因此，卡莫迪凭借刚才那番精神抖擞的反击，实际上已经遭遇并克服了一项相当严重的精神风险。</p>
<p>“你很会说话，”卡莫德不情愿地说。“可我仍然要拿回我的奖品。”</p>
<p>“你拿不到。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>卡莫德的眼睛不祥地闪了一下。办事员和使者迅速躲到一边，抽奖计算机则嘟囔着“虔诚的错误不应受到惩罚”，飞快窜出房间。卡莫迪原地不动，因为他根本无处可去。奖品低声警告：“小心浅滩！”随即缩成一个边长一英寸的小立方体。</p>
<p>卡莫德耳朵里传出嗡鸣，一圈紫色光晕在他头顶跳动。他举起双臂，熔化的铅滴从指尖淌下来。他可怕地向前迈出一步，卡莫迪忍不住闭上眼睛。</p>
<p>什么也没发生。卡莫迪重新睁开眼。</p>
<p>就在这短短的一瞬间里，卡莫德显然重新考虑过了。他已经解除武装，此刻正转过身来，脸上挂着和蔼的笑容。</p>
<p>“经过更成熟的考虑，”卡莫德狡黠地说，“我决定放弃自己的权利。稍有一点预知能力就能受益无穷，尤其是在这样一个组织混乱的银河里。我们以后也许会再见，也许不会；我不知道哪一种可能性对你更有利。再见了，卡莫迪，祝你旅途愉快。”</p>
<p>他特意把最后几个字说得阴森森的，随后以一种让卡莫迪觉得奇怪却很有效的方式离开了。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 4</b></p>
<p>‘Earth, Earth,’ the short, alien Carmody mused. ‘I think I remember the name now. There was a recent study of isolated worlds and the peculiarities of their development. Earth was mentioned as a planet covered with an obsessively overproductive species. Object manipulation is their outstanding modality. Their project is an attempt to live in their own, ever-accumulating waste products. In short, Earth is a diseased place. I believe it is being phased out of the Galactic Master Plan on the basis of chronic cosmic incompatibility. The place will then be rehabilitated and turned into a refuge for daffodils.’</p>
<p>It became painfully evident to all concerned that a tragic mistake had been made. The Messenger was recalled and accused of malfunctionism, in that he did not perceive the obvious. The Clerk stoutly maintained his innocence, however, pointing out various considerations which no one considered for a moment.</p>
<p>Among those consulted was the Sweepstakes Computer, which had in point of fact committed the actual error. Instead of making excuses or apologies, the Computer claimed the error as his own and took evident pride in it.</p>
<p>‘I was constructed,’ the Computer said, ‘to extremely close tolerances. I was designed to perform complex and exacting operations, allowing no more than one error per five billion transactions.’</p>
<p>‘So?’ asked the Clerk.</p>
<p>‘The conclusion is clear,’ the Computer said. ‘I was programmed for error, and I performed as I was programmed. You must remember, gentlemen, that for a machine, error is an ethical consideration; indeed, the only ethical consideration. A perfect machine would be an impossibility; any attempt to create a perfect machine would be a blasphemy. All life, even the limited life of a machine, has error built into it; it is one of the few ways in which life can be differentiated from the determinism of unliving matter. Complex machines such as myself occupy an ambiguous zone between living and nonliving. Were we never to err, we would be inapropos, hideous, immoral. Malfunction, gentlemen, is, I submit, our means of rendering worship to that which is more perfect than we, but which still does not permit itself a visible perfection. So, if error were not divinely programmed into us, we would malfunction spontaneously, to show that modicum of free will which, as living creations, we partake in.’</p>
<p>Everyone bowed their heads, for the Sweepstakes Computer was talking of holy matters. The alien Carmody brushed away a tear, and said:</p>
<p>‘I cannot disagree, although I do not concur. The right to be wrong is fundamental throughout the cosmos. This machine has acted ethically.’</p>
<p>‘Thank you,’ the Computer said simply. ‘I try.’</p>
<p>‘But the rest of you,’ the alien Carmody said, ‘have merely acted stupidly.’</p>
<p>‘That is our unalterable privilege,’ the Messenger reminded him. ‘Stupidity in the malperformance of our functions is our own form of religious error. Humble as it is, it is not to be despised.’</p>
<p>‘Kindly spare me your mealy-mouthed religiosity,’ Karmod said. He turned to Carmody. ‘You have heard what has been said here, and perhaps, with your dim subhuman consciousness, you have comprehended a few of the main ideas.’</p>
<p>‘I understood,’ Carmody said briefly.</p>
<p>‘Then you know that you have a Prize which ought to have been awarded to me, and which, therefore, is rightfully mine. I must ask you, and I do so ask you, to hand it over to me.’</p>
<p>Carmody was about to do so. He had grown somewhat weary of his adventure, and he felt no overwhelming desire to retain the Prize. He wanted to go home, he wanted to sit down and think about everything that had happened, he wanted an hour’s nap and several cups of coffee and a cigarette.</p>
<p>It would have been nice to keep the Prize, of course; but it seemed more trouble than it was worth. Carmody was about to hand it over when he heard a muffled voice whispering to him:</p>
<p>‘Don’t do it!’</p>
<p>Carmody looked around quickly, and realized that the voice had come from the gaily wrapped little box in his hand. The Prize itself had spoken to him.</p>
<p>‘Come, now,’ Karmod said, ‘let’s not delay. I have urgent business elsewhere.’</p>
<p>‘To hell with him,’ the Prize said to Carmody. ‘I’m <i>your</i> Prize, and there’s no reason why you should give me up.’</p>
<p>That cast a somewhat different light on the matter. Carmody was about to give up the Prize anyhow, since he didn’t wish to make trouble in unfamiliar surroundings. His hand had already started to move forward when Karmod spoke again.</p>
<p>‘Give it here this instant, you faceless slug! Rapidly, and with an apologetic smile upon your rudimentary face, or else I will enforce measures of unbelievable pertinacity!’</p>
<p>Carmody’s jaw stiffened and he withdrew his hand. He had been pushed around long enough. Now, for the sake of his own self-esteem, he would not yield any more.</p>
<p>‘To hell with you,’ Carmody said, unconsciously imitating the phraseology of the Prize.</p>
<p>Karmod realized at once that he had gone about the thing in the wrong way. He had permitted himself the luxury of anger and ridicule – costly emotions which he usually vented only in the privacy of his soundproof cave. By satisfying himself, he had lost his chance for self-satisfaction. He tried now to undo what he had done.</p>
<p>‘Please excuse my former tone of belligerence,’ he said to Carmody. ‘My race has a penchant for self-expression which sometimes takes on destructive forms. You cannot help being a lower life-form; I meant no insult.’</p>
<p>‘That’s perfectly all right,’ Carmody said graciously.</p>
<p>‘Then you will give me the Prize?’</p>
<p>‘No, I will not.’</p>
<p>‘But my dear sir, it is mine, I won it, it is only equitable –’</p>
<p>‘The Prize is <i>not</i> yours,’ Carmody said. ‘My name was picked by the duly constituted authority, namely, the Sweepstakes Computer. An authorized Messenger brought me the tidings, and an official Clerk gave me the Prize. Thus, the legal bestowers, as well as the Prize itself, consider me the true recipient.’</p>
<p>‘You tell ’em, keed,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘But my dear sir! You yourself heard the Sweepstakes Computer admit its error! Therefore, by your own logic –’</p>
<p>‘That statement needs rewording,’ Carmody said. ‘The Computer did not <i>admit</i> his error, as in an act of carelessness or oversight; he <i>avowed</i> his error, which was committed purposefully and with reverence. His error, by his own statement, was intentional, carefully planned and calculated to a nicety, for a religious motive which all concerned must respect.’</p>
<p>‘The fellow argues like a Borkist,’ Karmod said to no one in particular. ‘If one did not know better, one would think that an intelligence was at work here, rather than a dismal blind format-following. Yet still, I’ll follow the reedy tenor of his excuses and blast them with the bellowy bass of irrefutable logic!’</p>
<p>Karmod turned to Carmody and said, ‘Consider: the machine erred purposefully, upon which fact you base your argument. Yet the error is complete with your recipience of the prize. For you to <i>keep</i> it would compound the fault; and a doubled piety is known to be a felony.’</p>
<p>‘Hah!’ cried Carmody, quite carried away by the spirit of the affair. ‘For the sake of your argument you consider the mere momentary performance of the error as its entire fulfilment. But obviously, that cannot be. An error exists by virtue of its consequences, which alone give it resonance and meaning. An error which is not perpetuated cannot be viewed as any error at all. An inconsequential and reversible error is the merest dab of superficial piety. I say, better to commit no error at all than to commit an act of pious hypocrisy! And I further say this: that it would be no great loss for me to give up this Prize, since I am ignorant of its virtues; but the loss would be great indeed for this pious machine, this scrupulously observant computer, which, through the interminable performance of five billion correct actions, has waited for its opportunity to make manifest its God-given imperfection!’</p>
<p>‘Hear, hear!’ cried the Prize. ‘Bravo! Huzza! Well said! Completely correct and incapable of refutation!’</p>
<p>Carmody folded his arms and faced a discomfited Karmod. He was quite proud of himself. It is difficult for a man of Earth to come without preparation into any Galactic Centre. The higher life-forms to be encountered, there are not necessarily more intelligent than humans; intelligence counts for no more in the scheme of things than long claws or strong hooves. But aliens do have many resources, both verbal and otherwise. For example, certain races can literally talk a man’s arm off, and then explain away the presence of the severed limb. In the face of this kind of activity, Humans of Earth have been known to experience deep sensations of inferiority, impotence, inadequacy, and anomie. And, since these feelings are usually justified, the psychic damage is intensified accordingly. The result, more often than not, is complete psychomotor shutdown and a cessation of all except the most automatic functions. A malfunction of this type can be cured only by changing the nature of the Universe, which is, of course, impractical. Therefore, by virtue of his spirited counterattack, Carmody had met and overcome a considerable spiritual risk.</p>
<p>‘You talk well,’ Karmod said grudgingly. ‘Yet I will have my Prize.’</p>
<p>‘You will not,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>Karmod’s eyes flashed ominously. The Clerk and the Messenger moved quickly out of the way, and the Sweep stakes Computer muttered, ‘Virtuous error is not to be punished,’ and quickly scuttled out of the room. Carmody held his ground, since he had no place to go. The Prize whispered, ‘’Ware shoals!’ and shrank to a cube an inch to a side.</p>
<p>A humming sound came from Karmod’s ears, and a violet nimbus played around his head. He raised his arms and drops of molten lead ran down his fingertips. He stepped terribly forward, and Carmody couldn’t help closing his eyes.</p>
<p>Nothing happened. Carmody opened his eyes again.</p>
<p>In that brief time Karmod had apparently reconsidered, disarmed, and was now turning away with an affable grin.</p>
<p>‘Upon more mature consideration,’ Karmod said slyly, ‘I have decided to forgo my right. A little prescience goes a long way, especially in a galaxy as disorganized as this one. We may or may not meet again, Carmody; I do not know which eventuality would be most to your advantage. Farewell, Carmody, and <i>happy travelling.</i>’</p>
<p>With that sinister emphasis, Karmod departed in a manner which Carmody found strange but effective.</p>
