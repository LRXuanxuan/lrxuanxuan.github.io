<h2>第一章</h2>
<p>这一天照例过得不怎么令人满意。卡莫迪去了办公室，稍稍调戏了一下吉本小姐，恭恭敬敬地和温博克先生唱了反调，又花了十五分钟同布莱克韦尔先生讨论橄榄球巨人队的前景。临近下班时，他又和赛德利茨先生争论起来——争得慷慨激昂，而对争论内容却一无所知——话题是国家自然资源正在持续枯竭，以及联合爱迪生公司、陆军工程兵团、游客、红火蚁和纸浆造纸商等破坏力量正在无情推进。他断言，这些势力都在不同程度上糟蹋自然景观，并不断抹去硕果仅存的天然美景。</p>
<p>“好啊，汤姆，”那个尖刻而饱受胃溃疡折磨的赛德利茨说，“看来你是真认真想过这问题了，是吧？”</p>
<p>他才没有！</p>
<p>吉本小姐是个挺漂亮的年轻姑娘，就是下巴不太明显。她说：“哦，卡莫迪先生，我真觉得你不该那么说。”</p>
<p>他说了什么？为什么不该说？卡莫迪想不起来，于是既不后悔，却又隐约有点心虚。</p>
<p>他的上司温博克先生胖乎乎的，脾气温和。他说：“汤姆，你说的也许真有些道理。我会查一查。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪自己很清楚，他刚才说的话几乎没什么道理，也根本经不起细查。</p>
<p>高个子、说话刻薄的乔治·布莱克韦尔能在上嘴唇纹丝不动的情况下开口。他说：“我觉得你说得对，卡莫迪，真的。要是他们把沃斯从自由安全卫调到强侧角卫，我们肯定能看到真正的冲传压力。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪进一步想了想，决定那其实也不会有什么区别。</p>
<p>卡莫迪是个安静的人，气质大体偏忧郁，脸部轮廓也恰到好处地与他哀歌般的性情相匹配。他的身高和自我贬低程度都略高于平均水平。他姿势不好，但动机良善。他在消沉方面颇有天赋。他具有循环性情感气质——那些有点爱尔兰血统、高个子、长着猎犬般眼睛的男人通常都这样，尤其过了三十岁以后。</p>
<p>他桥牌打得不错，尽管总有低估自己手牌的倾向。名义上他是无神论者，不过主要是照惯例如此，而非出于坚定信念。他的各种潜在化身——都可以在“可能性大厅”里查阅——无一例外全是英雄。他是处女座；当土星位于太阳宫时，受土星支配。光凭这一点，他本来就足以出类拔萃。他还具有全人类共有的标志性特征：既可以预测，又深不可测——一个例行公事般的奇迹。</p>
<p>下午五点四十五分，他离开办公室，搭地铁往上城去。在车里，他被许多人推来搡去；他很愿意把这些人理解成社会上的弱势群体，却又暗自怀疑他们其实只是严重而不可救药地讨人嫌。</p>
<p>他从九十六街站出来，走过几个街区，回到西区大道上的公寓。门房愉快地向他打招呼，电梯员也友善地点点头。他打开公寓门，进去后往沙发上一躺。他妻子正在迈阿密度假；于是他可以不受惩罚地把双脚搁到旁边的大理石桌上。</p>
<p>片刻之后，客厅中央传来一声霹雳，同时闪过一道电光。卡莫迪猛地坐直，毫无缘由地一把掐住自己的喉咙。雷声隆隆持续了几秒，随后被一阵赞歌般的号角声取代。卡莫迪赶紧把脚从大理石桌上拿下来。号角停了，代之以雄壮刺耳的风笛声。又一道闪电亮起，一个男人出现在灿烂的光芒中央。</p>
<p>那人中等身材，体格结实，有一头卷曲的金发，披着金色斗篷，穿着橙色紧身裤。除了没有耳朵之外，他的五官看起来都很正常。他向前走了两步，停住，伸手探进空无一物的空气里，硬生生抽出一卷文书，抽的时候还把它撕坏了一大块。他清了清嗓子——那声音像一颗滚珠轴承在重量与摩擦力共同作用下宣告报废——然后说道：“致意！”</p>
<p>卡莫迪没有回答，因为他一时陷入了歇斯底里的失语状态。</p>
<p>“吾等前来，”陌生人说道，“乃不可言说之欲望所偶得之应答。你的！人皆如此乎？既非如此！则应否？”</p>
<p>陌生人等着答复。卡莫迪用几条只有他自己知道的证据说服自己：眼前发生在自己身上的事，确实正在发生在自己身上。于是他在现实层面作答：</p>
<p>“看在上帝的份上，这到底是怎么回事？”</p>
<p>陌生人仍然微笑着说：“此乃为你而来，卡—莫—迪！从‘现存之物’的流溢中，你赢得了一小块却意义重大的‘可能之物’。不该欢欣么？具体而言：你的名字领先其余诸名；偶然性再度得到昭雪，玫瑰肢体的‘不确定性’以沾满药渍之口欢呼，而古老的‘恒常性’则再次被锁回其‘必然洞穴’。此非一桩足以……之事乎？既如此，你为何不……？”</p>
<p>卡莫迪站起身来，觉得自己异常平静。未知之所以令人恐惧，只存在于“持续接触该现象”发生之前。（使者当然知道这一点。）</p>
<p>“你是谁？”卡莫迪厉声问。</p>
<p>陌生人思索了一下，笑容消失了。他半是自言自语地嘟囔：“这团雾脑袋居然扭起来了！他们又把我的处理搞错了！我简直羞得想把自己肢解。愿他们准确无误地遭到自己的纠缠！算了，我重新处理，我适应，我变成……”</p>
<p>陌生人把手指按进自己的脑袋，一直陷进去五厘米深。他的手指像一个人在弹一架极小的钢琴似的起伏。紧接着，他变成了一个又矮又敦实、身高普通的秃顶男子，穿着没熨过的商务西装，身上还带着一个鼓鼓囊囊的公文包、一把雨伞、一根手杖、一本杂志和一份报纸。</p>
<p>“这样正确吗？”他问。“对，我看得出来，正确。”他自己回答自己。“我真得为我们的‘相似性中心’那粗制滥造的工作道歉。就在上星期，我去西格玛四号时，被做成一只巨大的蝙蝠，嘴里叼着通知书；到了才发现我的通知对象属于睡莲科。再往前两个月——当然，我现在用的是你们当地的等价时间单位——我前往旧世界塔格玛执行任务，那群‘相似性中心’的蠢货把我处理成四个处女，可正确程序显然应该是——”</p>
<p>“你说的我一个字也听不懂，”卡莫迪说。“能不能劳驾你解释一下，这到底是怎么回事？”</p>
<p>“当然，当然，”陌生人说道。“让我先核对一下本地指涉物……”他闭上眼睛，片刻后又睁开。“奇怪，真奇怪，”他嘟囔道。“你们的语言似乎没有能装下我产品所需内容的容器；我是打比方说。不过话说回来，我又有什么资格评判呢？不精确也许在审美上别有情趣；一切终究只是品味问题。”</p>
<p>“这一切到底是什么？”卡莫迪压低嗓音，语气不祥地问。</p>
<p>“先生，这当然是‘星际抽奖’！而你当然中奖了！从我出现在这里这个事实本身，命题难道还不是内在自明的吗？”</p>
<p>“不，一点也不自明，”卡莫迪说，“而且我根本不知道你在说什么。”</p>
<p>陌生人脸上掠过一丝疑惑，随后又像被橡皮擦掉一样消失。“你不知道！可不是嘛！我想你一定早已对中奖绝望，所以把这件事从意识里搁置了，以免老惦记着。偏偏我又在你精神冬眠的时候赶来，真是不幸！不过我保证，绝无冒犯之意。你的资料档案不在手边？果然如此。我来解释吧：卡莫迪先生，你在星际抽奖中赢得了一份奖品。随机选择器在第四部、第三十二级生命形态的抽取中抽中了你的系数。你的奖品——我相信是一份非常体面的奖品——正在银河中心等你。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪发现自己开始按照如下方式同自己讲道理：</p>
<p>“我要么疯了，要么没疯。如果我疯了，我可以拒绝这些幻觉，去找精神科医生；可是这样一来，我就陷入一个荒唐局面：为了某种记忆模糊的理性，我必须否认感官告诉我的真实。这样很可能加剧我的心理冲突，让我的疯狂恶化到某种程度，以至于我那悲痛欲绝的妻子不得不把我送进精神病院。另一方面，如果我把这个疑似幻觉的东西当作现实接受，我也同样可能被关进精神病院。</p>
<p>“反过来说，如果我没有疯，那么这一切就确实正在发生。而眼下实际发生的，是一次古怪、独一无二的事件，是最高等级的冒险。显然——如果这一切确实发生——宇宙里存在智力高于人类的生命，就像我一直怀疑的那样。这些生命举办某种抽奖，从中随机抽取名字。（他们当然有权这么做；我看不出抽奖与高等智能有任何不相容之处。）最后，在这场疑似抽奖中，我的名字被抽中了。这是一件享有特权的事，很可能也是这项抽奖第一次扩展到地球。我赢得了一个奖品。这样的奖品也许会给我带来财富、声望、女人、知识，或者以上全部；无论哪一种都值得拥有。</p>
<p>“所以，综合考虑，我最好相信自己没有发疯，跟这位先生一起去领取奖品。如果我错了，我就会在精神病院醒来。到时候我可以向医生道歉，声明自己已经认清了幻觉的本质，也许还能重新获得自由。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪就是这么想的，也得出了这样的结论。这并不奇怪。极少有人——疯子除外——会宁愿接受“自己疯了”这一前提，而不接受一个令人吃惊的新假说。</p>
<p>当然，卡莫迪的推理里有一些问题；这些问题后来会纷纷站起来折磨他。不过也可以说，在当时的条件下，他居然还能推理，已经算相当不错了。</p>
<p>“我还是不太明白这一切究竟怎么回事，”卡莫迪对使者说。“我的奖品有没有附带什么条件？我是说，我是不是得做什么，或者买什么？”</p>
<p>“没有条件，”使者说。“至少没有什么值得一提的条件。奖品是免费的；如果不免费，它就不能叫奖品。你若接受，就必须随我前往银河中心——单是这趟旅行本身就值得一走。到了那里，你会拿到奖品。之后随你高兴，你可以把奖品带回这里，带回你的家。返程若需要任何帮助，我们当然会尽我们能力所及提供一切协助。事情就是这么简单。”</p>
<p>“听起来不错，”卡莫迪说道，语气和拿破仑看见内伊为滑铁卢战役作出的部署时一模一样。“我们怎么去？”</p>
<p>“这边走，”使者说。他领着卡莫迪走进门厅壁橱，又从时空连续体的一道裂缝里走了出去。</p>
<p>事情就是这么简单。在主观时间的几秒钟里，卡莫迪和使者已经跨越了相当遥远的距离，抵达银河中心。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 1</b></p>
<p>It had been a typically unsatisfactory day. Carmody had gone to the office, flirted mildly with Miss Gibbon, disagreed respectfully with Mr Wainbock, and spent fifteen minutes with Mr Blackwell, discussing the outlook for the football Giants. Towards the end of the day he had argued with Mr Seidlitz – argued vehemently and with a total lack of knowledge – about the steady depletion of the country’s natural resources, and the remorseless advance of destructive agencies such as Con Ed, the Army Engineering Corps, tourists, fire ants, and pulp-paper manufacturers. All of these, he contended, were responsible in varying degrees for the spoliation of the landscape and the steady obliteration of the remaining pockets of natural loveliness.</p>
<p>‘Well, Tom,’ sardonic, ulcerated Seidlitz had said, ‘you’ve really thought about this, haven’t you?’</p>
<p>He hadn’t!</p>
<p>Miss Gibbon, an attractive young lady without much chin, had said, ‘Oh, Mr Carmody, I really don’t think you should say that.’</p>
<p>What had he said, and why shouldn’t he say it? Carmody couldn’t remember, and thus remained unrepentant, though vaguely guilty.</p>
<p>His superior, plump, mild Mr Wainbock, had said, ‘There really may be something in what you say, Tom. I’ll look into it.’</p>
<p>Carmody was aware that there was very little in what he had said, and that it didn’t bear looking into.</p>
<p>Tall, sardonic George Blackwell, who could speak without moving his upper lip, had said, ‘I think you’re right, Carmody, I really do. If they switch Voss from free safety to strongside cornerback, we’ll <i>really</i> see a pass rush.’</p>
<p>Upon further reflection, Carmody decided that it wouldn’t make any difference.</p>
<p>Carmody was a quiet man, of a predominantly melancholic humour, with a face that neatly matched the elegiac contours of his disposition. He was somewhat above the average in height and self-deprecation. His posture was bad, but his intentions were good. He had a talent for depression. He was cyclothymic – tall, beagle-eyed men of vaguely Irish antecedents usually are, especially after the age of thirty.</p>
<p>He played a decent game of bridge, even though he tended to undervalue his hands. Nominally he was an atheist, but more by rote than conviction. His avatars, which can be viewed in the Hall of Potentialities, were uniformly heroic. He was a Virgo, dominated by Saturn when it was in the House of the Sun. This alone could have made him outstanding. He shared the common human hallmark: he was simultaneously predictable and unfathomable – a routine miracle.</p>
<p>He left the office at 5:45 and caught the subway uptown. There he was pushed and jostled by many people whom he wished to think of as underprivileged, but whom he suspected of being acutely and irrevocably undesirable.</p>
<p>He emerged at 96th Street station and walked the few blocks to his apartment on West End Avenue. The doorman greeted him cheerfully and the lift operator gave him a friendly nod. He unlocked his apartment door, went in and lay down on the couch. His wife was vacationing in Miami; therefore, with impunity he propped his feet on the nearby marble table.</p>
<p>A moment later there was a clap of thunder and a flash of lightning from the middle of the living room. Carmody sat upright and clutched at his throat for no particular reason. The thunder rumbled for several seconds, then was replaced by a paean of trumpets. Carmody hastily removed his feet from the marble table. The trumpets ceased, and were replaced by a brave skirling of bagpipes. There was another flash of lightning, and a man appeared in the middle of the brilliance.</p>
<p>The man was of medium height, stocky, had curly blond hair and wore a golden-coloured cloak and orange leggings. His features appeared normal except that he had no ears. He took two steps forward, stopped, reached into the empty air and plucked forth a scroll, tearing it badly as he did so. He cleared his throat – a sound like a ball bearing failing under a combination of weight and friction – and said, ‘Greetings!’</p>
<p>Carmody did not reply, being struck by a temporary hysterical muteness.</p>
<p>‘We are come,’ the stranger said, ‘as the fortuitous respondent of an ineffable desire. Yours! Do any men? No so, then! Shall it?’</p>
<p>The stranger waited for a reply. Carmody convinced himself by several proofs known only to himself that what was happening to him was indeed happening to him, and replied on a reality level:</p>
<p>‘What in God’s name is this all about?’</p>
<p>The stranger said, still smiling: ‘It is for you, Car-Mo-Dee! Out of the effluvium of what-is you have won a small but significant portion of what-might-be. Rejoicings, not? Specifically: your name has led the rest; the fortuitous is again vindicated, and rosy-limbed Indeterminacy rejoices with drug-stained mouth as ancient Constancy is barred again within his Cave of Inevitability. Is this not a cause for? Then why do you not?’</p>
<p>Carmody rose to his feet, feeling quite calm. The unknown is frightening only antecedent to the phenomenon of perseveration. (The Messenger knew this, of course.)</p>
<p>‘Who are you?’ Carmody demanded.</p>
<p>The stranger considered the question, and his smile faded. He muttered, half to himself, ‘The fog-minded squirms! They have processed me wrong again!’ I could mutilate myself from sheer mortification. May they haunt themselves unerringly! Never mind, I reprocess, I adapt, I become …’</p>
<p>The stranger pressed his fingers to his head, allowing them to sink in to a depth of five centimetres. His fingers rippled like those of a man playing a very small piano. Immediately he changed into a short dumpy man of average height, balding, wearing an unpressed business suit and carrying a bulging briefcase, an umbrella, a cane, a magazine, and a newspaper.</p>
<p>‘Is this correct?’ he asked. ‘Yes, I can see it is,’ he answered himself. ‘I really must apologize for the sloppy work done by our Similitude Centre. Only last week I appeared on Sigma IV as a giant bat with the Notification in my beak, only to discover that my recipient was a member of the water-lily family. And two months before (I am using local equivalent terms, of course) while on a mission to Thagma the Old World, those fools in Similitude processed me to appear as four virgins, when the correct procedure was obviously –’</p>
<p>‘I don’t understand a word you’re saying,’ Carmody said. ‘Will you kindly explain what this is all about?’</p>
<p>‘Of course, of course,’ the stranger said. ‘Let me just check the local referents …’ He closed his eyes, then opened them again. ‘Strange, very strange,’ he muttered. ‘Your language doesn’t seem to contain the containers which my product requires; metaphorically, I mean. But then, who am I to judge? Inexactitude can be aesthetically pleasing, I suppose; everything is a matter of taste.’</p>
<p>‘What is all of this?’ Carmody asked, in a low, ominous voice.</p>
<p>‘Well, sir, it’s the Intergalactic Sweepstakes, of course! And you are a winner, of course! The proposition is inherent in terms of my appearance, is it not?’</p>
<p>‘No, it is not,’ Carmody said, ‘and I don’t know what you’re talking about.’</p>
<p>An expression of doubt crossed the stranger’s face, then was erased as if by an eraser. ‘You do not know! But of course! You had, I suppose, despaired of winning, and therefore had set the knowledge aside to avoid its contemplation. How unfortunate that I have come at the time of your mental hibernation! But no insult was intended, I can assure you. Your data file is not readily available? I feared not. Then I shall explain; you, Mr Carmody, have won a Prize in the Intergalactic Sweepstakes. Your coefficients were pulled by the Random Selector for Part IV, Class 32 Lifeforms. Your Prize – a very handsome Prize, I believe – is waiting for you at Galactic Centre.’</p>
<p>Carmody found himself reasoning with himself in the following manner: ‘Either I am insane or I am not insane. If insane, I can reject my delusions and seek psychiatric aid; but this would leave me in the absurd position of trying to deny what my senses tell me is true in favour of a dimly remembered rationality. This might well compound my conflicts, thus deepening my insanity to the point where my sorrowing wife would have to put me in an institution. On the other hand, if I accept this presumed delusion as real, I might also end up being institutionalized.</p>
<p>‘If, on the other hand, I am <i>not</i> insane, then all of this is actually happening. And what <i>is</i> actually happening is a strange, unique occurrence, an adventure of the first magnitude. Evidently (if this is actually happening) there are beings in the Universe superior in intelligence to humans, just as I have always suspected. These individuals hold a sweepstakes in which names are drawn at random. (They are certainly entitled to do this; I see no manner in which a sweepstakes is inconsistent with superior intelligence.) Finally, in this presumed sweepstakes, my name has been drawn. This is a privileged occurrence, and may well be the first time that the sweepstakes has been extended to Earth. I have won a Prize in this contest. Such a Prize might bring me wealth, or prestige, or women, or knowledge, any or all of which would be worth having.</p>
<p>‘Therefore, all things considered, it will be better for me to believe that I am not insane and go with this gentleman to collect my Prize. If I am wrong, I shall wake up in an institution. There I will apologize to the doctors, state that I recognize the nature of my delusion, and perhaps win my freedom.’</p>
<p>This was what Carmody thought, and that was the conclusion he reached. It was not a surprising one. Very few humans (except the insane ones) accept the premise of insanity in favour of a startling new hypothesis.</p>
<p>There were certain things wrong with Carmody’s reasoning, of course; and these things were to rise up and plague him later on. But one might say that he did very well, under the circumstances, to reason at all.</p>
<p>‘I don’t know much about what all of this is about,’ Carmody said to the Messenger. ‘Are there any conditions attached to my Prize? I mean, am I supposed to do anything or buy anything?’</p>
<p>‘There are no conditions,’ the Messenger said. ‘Or at least, none worth speaking about. The Prize is free; if not free, it would not be a Prize. If you accept it, you must accompany me to Galactic Centre, which is in itself a trip worth the taking. There you will be given your Prize. Then, at your pleasure, you may take your Prize back to this, your home. If you require any help for the journey back, we will of course assist you to the fullest extent of our abilities. And that’s all there is to it.’</p>
<p>‘Sounds good to me,’ Carmody said, in exactly the same tones that Napoleon used when he was shown Ney’s dispositions for the Battle of Waterloo. ‘How do we get there?’</p>
<p>‘This way,’ the Messenger said. And he led Carmody into a hall closet and out through a crack in the space-time continuum.</p>
<p>It was as easy as that. Within seconds of subjective time, Carmody and the Messenger had traversed a considerable distance and arrived at Galactic Centre.</p>
