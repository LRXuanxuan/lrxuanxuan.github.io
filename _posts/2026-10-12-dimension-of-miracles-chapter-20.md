<h2>第二十章</h2>
<p>博格接受“一只会说话的哺乳动物”这个观念，花的时间比卡莫迪接受“一只会说话的爬行动物”更长。不过最后，博格毕竟还是接受了。正如奖品后来评论的那样：要让一个人相信某个事实存在，再没有什么比把那个事实本身摆在他面前更有效。</p>
<p>他们退到博格的办公室里。办公室位于一棵垂柳高大翠绿的枝叶下面。大家在那里坐好，清了清嗓子，努力想找点话说。最后，博格开口：“所以，你是一只从未来来的外星哺乳动物，嗯？”</p>
<p>“我想是吧。”卡莫迪说。“而你是一只生活在过去的本土地球爬行动物。”</p>
<p>“我从来没这么想过。”博格说。“不过，对，我想这确实是真的。你刚才说，你来自多远以后的未来？”</p>
<p>“大概一亿年以后。”</p>
<p>“哈。确实很远。对，真是相当遥远。”</p>
<p>“确实相当远。”卡莫迪表示同意。</p>
<p>博格点点头，毫无曲调地哼了几声。卡莫迪看得很清楚：他不知道下一句该说什么。博格看起来是个相当正派的家伙；好客，不过思想定型；极其顾家；完全不擅长聊天。简而言之，就是一只正派、乏味、中产阶级的霸王龙。</p>
<p>“嗯，嗯。”沉默开始变得尴尬以后，博格终于说道，“未来怎么样？”</p>
<p>“什么？”</p>
<p>“我是说，未来是个什么样的地方？”</p>
<p>“非常忙。”卡莫迪回答。“熙熙攘攘。新发明很多，也非常混乱。”</p>
<p>“嗯，嗯，嗯。”博格说。“这和我们这里一些想象力比较丰富的家伙所设想的差不多。有些人甚至预测，哺乳动物将发生进化变化，最后成为地球上的优势物种。不过我一直觉得这种想法既离谱又怪诞。”</p>
<p>“我想，在你听来肯定如此。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“那么，你们真是优势物种？”</p>
<p>“这个……算是优势物种之一。”</p>
<p>“那爬行动物呢？或者说得更具体一点，未来的霸王龙过得怎么样？”</p>
<p>卡莫迪既没有那个心，也没有那个胆量告诉他：在自己的时代，恐龙早已灭绝了大约六千万年，而爬行动物整体上也只在万物秩序中占据了一个无足轻重的位置。</p>
<p>“你们这个种族的发展，完全不比任何合理预期差。”卡莫迪回答。他觉得自己简直像皮提亚神谕一样玄妙，同时又相当鬼祟。</p>
<p>“好！我就知道会是这样！”博格说。“我们这个种族很坚韧，你知道，而且绝大多数霸王龙都有意志力和常识。人类和爬行动物共同生活，会不会经常发生麻烦？”</p>
<p>“不，不怎么麻烦。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“那我就放心了。我还担心恐龙会仗着体型巨大，变得盛气凌人。”</p>
<p>“不会，不会。”卡莫迪说。“我想，我可以代表未来的哺乳动物很有把握地说：人人都喜欢恐龙。”</p>
<p>“你这么说真厚道。”博格说。</p>
<p>卡莫迪含含糊糊地咕哝了一句。突然之间，他感到非常羞愧。</p>
<p>“未来并没有什么值得恐龙过分忧虑的事情。”博格说道，声音渐渐转成宴会后演讲者那种圆润宏亮的腔调。“不过事情并非一直如此。我们已经灭绝的祖先异特龙，似乎是种脾气暴躁、贪吃成性的蛮兽。它的祖先角鼻龙，则是一种矮小的肉食龙。从它脑壳的尺寸判断，那家伙一定蠢得不可思议。当然，还有其他一些黎明时代的肉食龙；而在它们以前，必然还存在某个缺失环节——一个极其遥远的祖先，四足恐龙和双足恐龙都从它那里分化出来。”</p>
<p>“双足恐龙当然占优势，对吧？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“当然。三角龙是一种头脑迟钝、性情凶暴的动物。我们养着几小群。它们的肉可以很好地补充一顿雷龙排。除此之外当然还有很多别的物种。你进城的时候，也许注意到一些鸭嘴龙。”</p>
<p>“是的，我看见了。”卡莫迪说。“它们在唱歌。”</p>
<p>“那些家伙一天到晚都在唱。”博格严厉地说。</p>
<p>“你们吃它们吗？”</p>
<p>“天哪，当然不！鸭嘴龙是<strong>有智慧的</strong>！除了霸王龙以外，它们是这颗行星上唯一另一种智慧物种。”</p>
<p>“你儿子说，它们是个真正的大问题。”</p>
<p>“嗯，它们确实是。”博格回答得稍微过于强硬。</p>
<p>“问题在哪里？”</p>
<p>“懒惰。而且阴沉、粗鲁。我知道自己在说什么；我雇过鸭嘴龙当仆人。它们没有野心，没有进取心，也没有坚持到底的毅力。差不多有一半时候，它们连自己是谁孵出来的都不知道，而且似乎也根本不在乎。跟你说话时，它们从来不坦坦荡荡地直视你的眼睛。”</p>
<p>“不过它们唱歌很好听。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“哦，对，唱得确实好。我们最出色的一些艺人就是鸭嘴龙。只要有人监督，它们做重型建筑工程也不错。当然，它们的长相对自己不利——那副鸭嘴样……不过这也不能怪它们。未来的鸭嘴龙问题解决了吗？”</p>
<p>“解决了。”卡莫迪说。“这个种族灭绝了。”</p>
<p>“也许这样最好。”博格说。“对，我真觉得这样最好。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪和博格又谈了好几个小时。卡莫迪了解了爬行动物城市生活面对的各种问题。随着越来越多的霸王龙和鸭嘴龙离开乡村，前往城市享受文明生活，森林城市变得越来越拥挤。过去五十年里，严重的交通问题已经出现。巨型蜥臀类喜欢高速移动，而且以自己敏捷的反射神经为傲；可当几千只巨兽同时在森林里狂奔，事故注定会发生。而事故往往十分严重：两只各重四十吨的爬行动物，以每小时三十英里的速度迎头相撞，最可能的结果就是脖子折断。</p>
<p>当然，这还不是唯一的问题。城市过度拥挤只是出生率爆炸的一个症状。世界各地的蜥臀类都生活在饥饿边缘。疾病和战争确实会削减它们的数量，却削减得还不够多。</p>
<p>“我们有这些问题，还有许许多多别的问题。”博格说。“我们的一些最杰出的头脑已经陷入绝望。不过我更乐观。我们爬行动物以前也经历过艰难时代，而且都闯过来了。我们会解决这些问题，就像解决过去所有问题一样。依我看，我们种族本身具有一种内在的高贵，一点有意识、无法扑灭的生命火花。我无法相信这火花有一天会熄灭。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪点了点头，说：“你们的人民会延续下去。”</p>
<p>他实在没有别的办法，只能像个绅士一样撒谎。</p>
<p>“我知道。”博格说。“不过得到确认总是件好事。谢谢你。那么现在，我想你大概愿意和你的朋友谈谈了。”</p>
<p>“什么朋友？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“我说的是正站在你身后的那只哺乳动物。”博格说。</p>
<p>卡莫迪猛地转过身，看见一个又矮又胖、戴着眼镜的男人，穿着深色商务西装，左臂夹着公文包和雨伞。</p>
<p>“卡莫迪先生？”那人问。</p>
<p>“是，我就是卡莫迪。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“我是国内税务局的瑟蒂斯先生。卡莫迪先生，你可真让我们追了好一阵，不过美国国税局总能逮到自己要找的人。”</p>
<p>博格说：“这和我无关。”随后转身离开。对于如此巨大的一只霸王龙来说，他离场时发出的声音小得惊人。</p>
<p>“你的朋友很不寻常。”瑟蒂斯先生望着离去的博格说。“不过这与我无关，虽然联邦调查局也许会对此有些兴趣。我来这里只有一个目的，就是处理你1965年和1966年的纳税申报。我公文包里有一份引渡令，我想你会发现手续完全合法。我的时间机器就停在这棵树外面。我建议你安静地跟我走。”</p>
<p>“不。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“请你重新考虑。”瑟蒂斯说。“针对你的案子完全可以得到令各方都满意的解决。但必须立刻解决。美国政府不喜欢被人晾着。拒绝服从最高法院命令——”</p>
<p>“我说了，不！”卡莫迪说。“你还是走吧。我知道你是谁。”</p>
<p>因为这毫无疑问就是捕食者。它模仿国内税务官员的技术差得令人难以置信。公文包和雨伞居然都直接连接在左手上；五官倒还算像，可它忘了做一只耳朵。最糟糕的是，它的膝盖朝后弯。</p>
<p>卡莫迪转身走开。捕食者站在原地，没有追上来——大概它确实无法追赶。它只发出一声饥饿与愤怒交织的吼叫，随后消失了。</p>
<p>不过卡莫迪并没有多少时间为自己的胜利洋洋得意。</p>
<p>因为片刻之后，他也消失了。</p>
</section>
<section class="stapledon-body" data-story-lang="en" lang="en" aria-label="English original" hidden>
<p><b>CHAPTER 20</b></p>
<p>It took Borg longer to accept the idea of a talking mammal than it took Carmody to accept the idea of a talking reptile. Still, Borg finally did accept it. As the Prize remarked later, there is nothing like the actual presence of a fact to make one believe in the existence of that fact.</p>
<p>They retired to Borg’s office, which was under the lofty green foliage of a weeping-willow tree. There, they sat and cleared their throats, trying to think of something to say. At last Borg said, ‘So you’re an alien mammal from the future, eh?’</p>
<p>‘I guess I am,’ Carmody said. ‘And you are an indigenous reptile from the past.’</p>
<p>‘I never thought about it that way,’ Borg said. ‘But yes, I suppose that’s true. How far ahead in the future did you say you came from?’</p>
<p>‘About a hundred million years or so.’</p>
<p>‘Hah. Quite a long time away. Yes, a long time indeed.’</p>
<p>‘It <i>is</i> quite a long time away,’ Carmody agreed.</p>
<p>Borg nodded and hummed tunelessly. It was evident to Carmody that he didn’t know what to say next. Borg seemed a very decent sort of person; hospitable, but set in his ways, very much a family man, no conversationalist, just a decent, dull, middle-class tyrannosaur.</p>
<p>‘Well, well,’ Borg said, after the silence had become uncomfortable, ‘and how <i>is</i> the future?’</p>
<p>‘Beg pardon?’</p>
<p>‘I mean, what sort of a place is the future?’</p>
<p>‘Very busy,’ Carmody replied. ‘Bustling. Many new inventions, a great deal of confusion.’</p>
<p>‘Well, well, well,’ Borg said. ‘That’s very much as some of our more imaginative chaps had pictured it. Some of them have even predicted an evolutionary change in the mammals, making them the dominant species on Earth. But I consider that farfetched and grotesque.’</p>
<p>‘I suppose it must sound that way,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Then you <i>are</i> the dominant species?’</p>
<p>‘Well … <i>one</i> of the dominant species.’</p>
<p>‘But what about the reptiles? Or more specifically, how are the tyrannosaurs doing in the future?’</p>
<p>Carmody had neither the heart nor the nerve to tell him that dinosaurs were extinct in his day, and had been extinct for sixty million years or so, and that reptiles in general had come to occupy an insignificant part in the scheme of things.</p>
<p>‘Your race is doing every bit as well as could be expected,’ Carmody replied, feeling positively Pythian and rather sneaky.</p>
<p>‘Good! I thought it would be like that!’ Borg said. ‘We’re a tough race, you know, and most of us have will power and common sense. Do men and reptiles have much trouble co-existing?’</p>
<p>‘No, not much trouble,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Glad to hear it. I was afraid the dinosaurs might have become high-handed on account of their size.’</p>
<p>‘No, no,’ Carmody said. ‘Speaking for the mammals of the future, I think I can safely say that everybody likes a dinosaur.’</p>
<p>‘It’s very decent of you to say that,’ Borg said.</p>
<p>Carmody mumbled something. He suddenly felt very ashamed of himself.</p>
<p>‘The future holds no great anxiety for a dinosaur,’ Borg said, falling into the rotund tones of an after-dinner speaker. ‘But it was not always that way. Our extinct ancestor, the allosaurus, seems to have been a bad-tempered brute and a gluttonous feeder. His ancestor, the ceratosaurus, was a dwarf carnosaur. To judge from the size of his braincase, he must have been incredibly stupid. There were other dawn-age carnosaurs, of course; and before them there must have been a missing link – a remote ancestor from which both the quadruped and the bipedal dinosaurs sprang.’</p>
<p>‘The bipedal dinosaurs are dominant, of course?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Of course. The triceratops is a dull-witted creature with a savage disposition. We keep small herds of them. Their flesh rounds out a meal of brontosaurus steak quite nicely. There are various other species, of course. You might have noticed some hadrosaurs as you came into the city.’</p>
<p>‘Yes, I did,’ Carmody said. ‘They were singing.’</p>
<p>‘Those fellows are always singing,’ Borg said sternly.</p>
<p>‘Do you eat them?’</p>
<p>‘Good heavens, no! Hadrosaurs are <i>intelligent</i>! They are the only other intelligent species on the planet, aside from tyrannosaurs.’</p>
<p>‘Your son said they were a real problem.’</p>
<p>‘Well, they are,’ Borg said, a little too defiantly.</p>
<p>‘In what way?’</p>
<p>‘They’re lazy. Also sullen and surly. I know what I’m talking about; I’ve employed hadrosaurs as servants. They have no ambition, no drive, no stick-to-it-iveness. Half the time they don’t know who hatched them, and they don’t seem to care. They don’t look you forthrightly in the eye when they speak to you.’</p>
<p>‘They sing well, though,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Oh, yes, they sing well. Some of our best entertainers are hadrosaurs. They also do well at heavy construction, if given supervision. Their appearance works against them, of course, that duckbilled look … But they can’t help that. Has the hadrosaur problem been solved in the future?’</p>
<p>‘It has,’ Carmody said. ‘The race is extinct.’</p>
<p>‘Perhaps it’s best that way,’ Borg said. ‘Yes, I really think it’s best.’</p>
<p>Carmody and Borg conversed for several hours. Carmody learned about the problems of urban reptilian life. The forest-cities were becoming increasingly crowded as more and more tyrannosaurs and hadrosaurs left the countryside for the pleasures of civilization. A traffic problem of some severity had sprung up in the last fifty years. Giant saurischians like to travel fast and are proud of their quick reflexes. But when several thousand of them are rushing through a forest at the same time, accidents are bound to happen. The accidents were often severe: when two reptiles, each weighing forty tons, meet head-on at thirty miles an hour, broken necks are the most likely result.</p>
<p>These were not the only problems, of course. The overcrowded cities were a symptom of an exploding birthrate. Saurischians in various parts of the world lived on the edge of starvation. Disease and warfare tended to thin their numbers, but not enough.</p>
<p>‘We have these and many other problems,’ Borg said. ‘Some of our finest minds have given way to despair. But I am more sanguine. We reptiles have seen difficult times before and have won through. We shall solve these problems just as we have solved all others. To my way of thinking, there is an innate nobility about our race, a spark of conscious, unquenchable life. I cannot believe that this will be extinguished.’</p>
<p>Carmody nodded and said, ‘Your people will endure.’ There really was nothing for him to do but lie like a gentleman.</p>
<p>‘I know it,’ Borg said. ‘It is always good, however, to receive confirmation. Thank you for that. And now I suppose you would like to speak with your friends.’</p>
<p>‘What friends?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘I refer to the mammal standing directly behind you,’ Borg said.</p>
<p>Carmody turned quickly and saw a short, fat, bespectacled man in a dark business suit, with a briefcase and an umbrella under his left arm. ‘Mr Carmody?’ he asked.</p>
<p>‘Yes, I’m Carmody,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I am Mr Surtees from the Bureau of Internal Revenue. You have given us quite a chase, Mr Carmody, but the IRS always gets its man.’</p>
<p>Borg said, ‘This is none of my concern.’ He exited, making very, little noise for so large a tyrannosaurus.</p>
<p>‘You have some unusual friends,’ Mr Surtees said, gazing at the departing Borg. ‘But that is no concern of mine, though it may be of some interest to the FBI. I am here solely in regard to your 1965 and 1966 tax returns. I have in my briefcase an extradition order, which I think you will find in order. My time machine is parked just outside of this tree. I suggest that you come along quietly.’</p>
<p>‘No,’ said Carmody.</p>
<p>‘I beg you to reconsider,’ said Surtees. ‘The case against you can be settled to the mutual satisfaction of all concerned. But it must be settled at once The government of the United States does not like to be kept waiting. Refusal to obey an order of the Supreme Court –’</p>
<p>‘I told you, no!’ Carmody said. ‘You might as well go away. I know who you are.’</p>
<p>For this was the predator beyond any doubt. Its mimicry of an Internal Revenue man had been unbelievably clumsy. Both the briefcase and the umbrella were jointed to the left hand. The features were fair, but an ear had been forgotten. And, worst of all, the knees were hinged backwards.</p>
<p>Carmody turned and walked away. The predator stood there, not following, presumably unable to follow. It gave a single cry of hunger and rage. Then it disappeared.</p>
<p>Carmody had little time for self-congratulation, however, for a moment later, he disappeared.</p>
