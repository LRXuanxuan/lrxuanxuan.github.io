<h2>ii. 童年的保罗</h2>
<p>我对保罗的了解，也许比对我自己的了解还深。我曾经就是保罗。我陪着保罗度过他的一生，一直看到他死亡的最后一刻。他生命中那些更重要的阶段，我反复体验过许多次。我知道他身上许多极其私密的小事，比如他口腔内壁是什么触感。我知道保罗微醉时是什么感觉，性兴奋时是什么感觉，患上一次严重感冒时又是什么感觉。甚至保罗自己都从未真正意识到，有时只是因为松饼吃得太多，他整个身体便会蒙上一层模糊而恶劣的基调，使得他仿佛看见整个世界都跟着腐坏了；这种感觉，我比他本人清楚得多。我还能把不同年龄的保罗同时保存在意识中加以比较：童年的保罗，少年的保罗，青年保罗，逐渐僵硬进入中年的保罗，以及陷入无能为力的衰老、等待死亡的保罗。</p>
<p>幸运的是，我不需要把自己所知道的关于保罗的一切细节都讲给你们听。我只需让你们看清贯穿他一生的几条主要精神主题或动机。例如，他出生时的情形我没有必要多说。那是一次普通的分娩。我曾多次重新检查在他最早那场冒险中折磨过他的模糊经验，因为我一度怀疑，也许正是在那里发生过什么，后来促成了他的特殊性。然而，无论他进入外部世界的过程，还是出生前那种真菌般朦胧、探索者极难深入的经验，我都没有发现什么真正特殊之处，只有一切同类都会经历的东西。保罗的天性比平均水平敏感，因此从子宫的安乐境地突然被抛进陌生世界，对他来说是一次严重冲击，而医生的笨拙又把这冲击稍稍加剧了。但它并没有给他的心灵造成什么非同寻常的损害，只留下了你们许多人都共有的那种渴望：向往一个更温暖、更舒适、刺激更少的世界。也正是这种渴望，使你们中的许多人容易相信过去曾有黄金时代，或者未来将有黄金天堂。</p>
<p>不过，保罗出生时确实有一个细节很有意义，也进一步证明我没有选错实验对象。他和许多婴儿一样，一开始没有呼吸，直到有人拍了他一下，才猛地吸气，随即嚎啕大哭。当然，我体验到了他对这一下惩罚又困惑又愤怒的感觉；我也体验到冰冷空气突然灌入肺部时的知觉，以及紧随其后的那一下近乎欢欣的冲击。就在那个瞬间，保罗的心灵里永久建立起了一种联系：我姑且把它称为“命运的击打”与“生命的呼吸”之间的联系。后来，保罗会清楚知道自己是个胆小的人；可即便如此，他仍会不断从命运的严酷中寻找新生命的气息。无论他处在哪个年龄，他一方面总想爬回子宫那种温暖、封闭而安静的和平中；另一方面，却又同样渴望把更广大世界的空气吸进血液。</p>
<p>我也不必详述保罗如何在摇篮、育儿室和花园中艰苦地自我教育。和所有有机体一样，他必须学习怎样应付自己的世界；和所有动物一样，他必须解决如何在一个具有空间与时间的世界中感知并行动的问题。他一点一点学会观察并回应各种对象，例如母亲那圆润、芳香、充满乳汁的乳房，以及吸引力差得多的奶瓶。他学习自己身体的地理，在日复一日、极其复杂而自己毫无察觉的探索中耐心给身体绘制地图，并逐渐发现，这些杂乱无章的发现竟可以非常漂亮地组合成一个由触觉和视觉共同构成的三维系统。他掌握了许多伟大的技艺：最初是吸吮，接着是在床上挪动，然后是摇响拨浪鼓。再后来，他学会把整个身体当作车辆，让自己能够在更广阔的范围内来去。他掌握了更危险的爬行与行走，还学会玩会弹跳的球，以及其他本领。与此同时，他开始分辨人声中安抚和恼怒的不同语调。那些时有时无的人类乳房、手臂、膝头和声音，也逐渐在他的经验里排列成相对稳定的系统；每个系统都大致可以预期会以自己独特的方式行动。最后，他终于发现，人类发出的声音是有意义的，而且他自己也可以制造这些声音，借此命令那些巨大的成年人按照他的意愿行动。</p>
<p>能够控制成年人的动作，是保罗早期最令人兴奋的胜利之一。当然，这种力量既有限，又非常不可靠。那些巨大生物常常拒绝服从，甚至反过来迫使他去做自己根本不想做的事。总体说来，成年人是保罗早年生活中一种十分令人困惑的事实：有些方面，他们如此不可或缺、如此可靠；另一些方面，却又如此前后不一，甚至具有伤害性。当然，保罗不会自觉作出这些概括，但他的行为早已包含了它们。因此，在身体照料和保护等方面，他会绝对信任成年人；但在另一些方面，他又很快学会只期待误解和嘲笑。还有些地方，他深深受制于这些强大存在所带来的威望暗示，以至于他们许多莫名其妙的赞赏与禁忌，逐渐在他自己的性情中扎根。例如，对于性，他几乎奴隶般接受了成年人的全部态度。很小的时候，他已经开始对身体上那些成年人有意无意避而不谈的部位产生兴趣，也发现触碰这些器官会带来一种模糊的愉悦。然而，还远在有人明确告诉他，注意这些地方既邪恶又危险以前，他就已经深深感觉事情确实如此；原因仅仅是，每当他提到这些器官，成年人便立刻表现得尴尬而别扭。假如保罗出生时是一只小狒狒或黑猩猩，至少可以躲过那种精神痛苦和精力浪费——后面我会讲到，他和许多同类一样，正是由于过于顺从地接受成年人的标准而付出了这些代价。不过，在性尚未变成不可抗拒的需求以前，保罗在这方面以及许多其他方面适应成年人的标准，本来就是不可避免的。成年人也是环境中的对象，必须像学会应付狗、椅子和火一样学会应付。有时会失败，于是受苦；但总体上，还是能慢慢掌握。</p>
<p>不过，我不能在保罗逐步解决这些最早期问题的过程上停留太久。在他的每一次冒险中，我这个寄生者都品尝着他的成功与失败。我承受了他每一次磕碰、擦伤和烫伤。我常常提前知道将要发生什么；我既知道有些事故几乎不可避免，又知道如果他再熟练一点，本来完全可以躲过。假如我愿意，我本可以像一个母亲那样照看他，让他避开许多灾祸；但让他自己学习更好。因此，在保罗最早的几年，我完全克制住任何干预，因为对我的实验来说，让他在某个阶段以前保持正常发育非常重要。</p>
<p>一点一点地，保罗的世界结晶成越来越广大的模式。它不再只是贫乏、模糊、像梦一般流动的形状，而变成了一个多少可靠的“常识”世界：一栋房子，一座花园，周围是由别的房子和花园、街道与田野组成的大陆。与此同时，他也逐渐能够向后回望，不再只看见一团混乱的“过去感”，而是看见由一个个白昼与夜晚组成的短暂却有秩序的纵深。</p>
<p>可是，白昼世界越是渐渐变得有秩序，保罗就越受另一个混乱世界折磨——那个每到夜晚便把他吞没的世界。灯一熄灭，他被独自留在床上，白天那个可靠的秩序便全部消失。哪怕紧闭双眼，恐怖的幽影仍会以模糊而不断变化的形状逼到面前。他看见兽头、兽肩，还有悄悄移动的大猩猩手臂。这些东西不断从一种东西变成另一种东西。它们无法理解，因此也就格外可怕。</p>
<p>更糟的时候，那团翻腾混乱的阴影会被一只单独睁大的眼睛支配。保罗逃不掉它。无论望向哪里，它都在那里盯着他。也许那是上帝的眼睛，能够看穿他，冷冷记录他所有最秘密的坏事。也许上帝因为保罗白天太顽皮，已经把白昼世界整个取消了。也许现在什么都没有，只剩下保罗、上帝和紧紧包住他的黑暗；门缝底下那条细光不过是在假装世界还存在，好维持一个表象。不知为什么，那只眼睛在保罗看来既非常聪明，又非常愚蠢：它是一个怀有报复心的聪明东西，只因为保罗不讨它喜欢，就准备单靠凝视把他杀死。有时保罗吓得尖叫起来。于是一个恼火的成年人会出现，问他到底出了什么事。他从来解释不清，因为成年人似乎天生看不见夜晚的世界，只会觉得他很傻。但对我来说，保罗的恐惧非常真实，而且完全可以理解。尽管保罗自己不知道，对他而言，夜其实就是子宫。当他很困时，它意味着休息与和平；可当他不困时，它就成了一口由黑暗、闷塞、无形和否定构成的井。它仿佛要把他从明亮、规整的子宫外世界重新吞回去——而白昼世界里有复杂的地理，有可靠的人物，还有带着来自更广大天地之模糊消息吹来的风。</p>
<p>这种白昼世界与夜晚世界的对照，贯穿了保罗一生，始终潜伏在他的思想与感情背后，对它们施加影响，也不断把它们搅乱。不过，随着年龄增长，这两个世界似乎越来越相互渗透，最后保罗终于发现，任何事物都既有一种白昼的看法，也有一种夜晚的看法。可是，究竟哪一种才是真的？也许夜晚对事物的看法不过是噩梦，是坏梦。但也许事情恰好相反。也许整个白昼宇宙，才是那个被永久囚禁在永恒子宫里的永恒胎儿做的一场愚蠢梦。当然，保罗小时候从不会用这种方式思考问题；但我能够看出，他心灵中那些模糊运动，怎样同早已遗忘的出生经验联系在一起。</p>
<p>夜晚还有一个方面，即便对童年的保罗来说，也和把他囚在床上的那种黑暗完全不同——那就是星空。他很少有机会看见它；但哪怕只是匆匆一瞥，也会在他身上留下长久影响。星空给他一种平静的振奋，使他能够抵御黑暗中的恐怖；这种情绪有时甚至会延续到第二天，安抚他的烦恼，也节制他的欢乐。这种经验后来在保罗一生中占据了重要位置，它并不完全是他自身天性的自发产物，一部分来自我在他心灵中的存在，当然也有一部分来自他天生的性情。在他一生中，每当他能够仰望夜空，这种时刻对我这个被囚禁在他身上、经常被他的经验弄得筋疲力尽的观察者，也会产生极大作用。透过他，我终于能够瞥见某种他的世界与我的世界共同拥有的东西，于是获得片刻清新。当我已经过分沉入保罗行为的琐碎细节，甚至开始觉得你们的世界比我自己的世界更真实时，哪怕只是通过你们物种那效率低下的眼睛看见群星，也足以极其鲜明地把我带回遥远未来的海王星：带回那里的平原、矮小丛林、刺破天空的高塔，以及我那些安详的同胞。这种清晰记忆会重新点燃我内心那种静止而如火焰般的热情，那正是我们这个注定灭亡却从不惊慌的共同体的精神。随后涌遍我的宁静情感也会感染保罗，给他的经验染上一种色调。虽然他从来无法真正解释这种感觉来自哪里，但随着时间推移，他开始认出，这似乎是一份来自自身尘世本性之外的礼物。我清楚记得，童年的保罗第一次看见群星时，先是一阵惊讶的沉默，随后发出咯咯的欢叫。当时他被母亲抱在怀里，伸出自己胖乎乎的双臂，缓慢左右转动脑袋，巡视整个天空。又有一次，那已经晚得多了，他从一次圣诞聚会回家，累得几乎走不动；可头顶灿烂的夜空一下把他彻底压倒，使他因为一种无法理解的喜悦而突然哭了出来。</p>
<p>随着年岁增长，保罗越来越强烈地感觉到，即便白天，星夜也仍旧存在；这种意识对他一生的影响越来越大。其中一部分是我的作为。我很快发现，只要在普通日常情境中向他投放夜空的视觉意象，就可以帮助他用某种近似天王星式的超然目光去看自己和同伴的事务。他发育早期有一个阶段，由于我的这种作用，他开始把整个宇宙想象成一块三明治：上面和下面都是夜，中间夹着白昼这块“肉”。下方，是他在闷热卧室里遭遇的那种恐怖黑暗、混乱和逼仄；上方，是群星高悬的神秘；夹在两者之间的，才是熟悉事物组成的世界。</p>
<p>这个中间世界此时已经向外铺展开来，成为一大片模糊的国度。在熟悉街道之外的某个地方，存在各式各样的奇观，但这些奇观仍然明确属于白昼世界。某个地方有印度，那里到处是老虎、大象、珠光宝气的王公和杂耍艺人。别处是冰岛，整个国家都由坚冰构成，火焰河流从水晶火山上奔流下来。然后还有法国，人们吃青蛙和蜗牛，而且礼貌得可怕；德国人专门仿制廉价的英国玩具，还彼此决斗；希腊人除了头戴插着衣刷似羽饰的头盔以外几乎不穿衣服；埃及则全是木乃伊和鳄鱼；还有仙境，那里发生魔法比在英格兰容易得多。不过，即使英格兰也不是完全没有魔法。圣诞老人当然存在，还有几十种会带来好运或厄运的东西，必须分别寻找或躲避。</p>
<p>整个由各国组成的世界上方，悬着天空，像一面蓝色或阴云覆盖的天花板。就在这里，保罗的宇宙里出现了一处矛盾。如果按照白昼世界的观点思考，那么天空和群星以外就是天堂，里面挤满穿着睡袍、长着翅膀的天使，他们的眼睛永远向上望；再往上则是上帝。保罗不知怎样把上帝想成了一个巨大成年人，身体从地平线这头摊到另一头，却偏偏低头准确地注视着保罗本人，而且连他的思想都能看见。不知为什么，那双眼睛又正是卧室下层黑暗里盯住他的“那只眼睛”。白天只要想起它，保罗便会飞快念叨：“哦，上帝，我爱你，我爱你，我爱你。”</p>
<p>然而，如果从星夜的角度来想，上帝和天使又似乎不太能塞进那个宇宙里。保罗半信半疑地觉得，他们也许只是画在白昼世界天花板上的图画。</p>
<p>当然，随着时间推移，保罗的白昼世界不再是平的，而变成了一个巨大的球。这时候，整个宇宙与其说像三明治，不如说更像一个大面团。模模糊糊地，保罗仍保留着三层世界的观念。下层黑夜现在深藏在白昼世界这个球体内部；星空则从四周包围它。除了仙境无处可寻以外，所有国家都位于这个球面上。许多国家属于大英帝国，因此在地图上涂成红色。上学以后，他学了很多英格兰和帝国的知识，也成了爱国者。英国人——怎么说呢，就是英国人。他们拥有世界上最大的帝国。只有他们比赛时讲公平，爱护动物，有能力统治黑人，能把一场注定失败的战斗坚持到最后，还统治着海洋。正因为英国人如此优秀，上帝才特意在他们国家地下埋藏了大量煤和铁，好让他们用来造船、造机器，再制造成千上万种别国人不够聪明、无法自己造出的东西。上帝甚至还用英语写了《圣经》，因为英语是最好的语言，天使当然也说英语。</p>
<p>保罗的爱国主义很大程度上是一种装出来的姿态。那时候，他非常渴望和同龄人一样，而同龄人又把自己的爱国表现得再明显不过。此外，爱国主义还给了他一个自我庆贺的来源。不知通过怎样的魔法，一旦进入爱国主义，那个像小虫一样微不足道的普通保罗，就变成了地球的主人之一。不过，还有另一个因素也在起作用。他已经开始朦胧地渴望把自己奉献给某些超越私人满足的目标。可惜，这种对崇高目的的渴望被误解得很厉害；它很大一部分表现成夸耀自己的学校、贬低其他学校。但帝国毕竟给这种冲动提供了一个更加宏伟、更有气势的对象。</p>

<h2>ii. THE CHILD PAUL</h2>
<p>IKNOW P AUL perhaps better than I know myself. I have been Paul. I have been Paul throughout his life, and watched him to the very moment of his death. The more important passages of his life I have experienced again and again. I know little intimate facts about him, like the feel of the inside of his mouth. I know the feel of Paul slightly drunk, Paul sexually excited, and Paul with one of his bad colds. I know, far better than Paul himself knew it, the obscure tone of his whole body, when through eating too many muffins, he seemed to see the world itself turn foul. I know also, and can hold together for comparison, the feel of Paul the child, Paul the boy, Paul the young man, stiffening into middle age, and Paul awaiting death in helpless senility.</p>
<p>Fortunately there is no need for me to tell you all the details that I know about Paul. I have only to make you realize the main mental themes or motifs of his life. I need not, for instance, tell you much about his birth. It was a normal birth. Several times I have examined the obscure experiences which harassed him during that earliest of his adventures. For it seemed possible that even then something might have occurred to contribute to his idiosyncrasies. But neither in his passage into the outer world nor in his fungoid pre-natal experience (so difficult for the explorer to penetrate) did I find anything significant, save what is common to all his kind. In Paul, whose nature was more sensitive than the average, the shock of exchanging the elysium of the womb for an alien world was serious, and was accentuated somewhat by the clumsiness of the physician. But it caused no unusual damage to his mind, nothing but the common yearning toward a warmer, cosier, less noxious world, which, in so many of you, favours legends of a golden age in the past, or of a golden heaven in the future.</p>
<p>One feature of Paul’s experience of birth was indeed significant, and served to confirm my choice of him as a subject of experiment. Like many others, he did not breathe till a smack made him take breath to bellow. I experienced, of course, his bewildered fury at the chastisement. I also experienced his awareness of the cold air that flooded his lungs, and the shock of glee that came with it. In that instant a connexion was registered in Paul’s mind for ever after, a connexion between what I may call fate’s smiting and the breath of life. In after years Paul was to know himself a coward, yet in spite of his cowardice, he was ever to seek in the harshness of fate for the breath of a new life. Even though, in all his ages, he yearned to creep back into the warm close peace of the womb, he craved also to absorb into his blood the atmosphere of a wider world.</p>
<p>I need not describe Paul’s arduous self-education in the cot, the nursery and the garden. Like all organisms he had to learn to cope with his world. Like all animals he had to grapple with the problems of perceiving and acting in a world of space and time. Little by little he learned to observe and to respond to all manner of objects, such as the rotund and fragrant dairy of his mother’s breast, and the less delectable bottle. He learned the geography of his own body, mapping it out patiently, unwittingly, day by day, in a surprisingly intricate system of explorations, discovering that all these complex findings fell together beautifully in a three-dimensional system of touch and sight. Many great arts he acquired, first the art of sucking, and of shifting about in bed, then the art of the rattle. Presently he learned how to use his whole body as a vehicle to transport him to and fro in a wider sphere. He mastered the more dangerous arts of crawling and walking, the art of the bouncing ball, and so on. Meanwhile he had distinguished the soothing and indignant tones of the human voice. Also, the intermittent human breasts and arms and laps and voices had fallen into order to make more or less constant systems, each of which might be expected to behave in a distinctive manner. Then at last he discovered that human voices had meanings, and that he himself could use these noises so as to make the great grown-up creatures do his bidding.</p>
<p>This power of controlling the movements of adults was one of Paul’s most exciting early triumphs. It was a limited and erratic power. All too often the great creature refused to obey, or actually turned the tables and compelled him to do what he did not want to do. Altogether these adults were a very perplexing fact in Paul’s early life. In some ways they were so necessary and reliable, in others so inconsequent and even noxious. Of course, Paul did not consciously make these generalizations, but they were implied in his behaviour. Thus in certain respects, such as physical care and protection, he trusted the adult absolutely, but in others he learned to expect nothing but misunderstanding and ridicule. In yet other respects he himself was so influenced by the prestige suggestion which these mighty beings brought to bear on him, that many of their unintelligible admirations and taboos gradually took root in his own nature. For instance, he slavishly accepted their views about sex. Very early in his childhood he had begun to take an interest in those parts of his body which adults pointedly ignored. He had discovered that a vague pleasure occurred when these organs were touched. But long before he was told that it was wicked and dangerous to take any notice of them, he already profoundly felt that it was so, merely through the awkwardness that beset adults whenever he referred to them. Had Paul been born a young baboon or chimpanzee he would at least have escaped the agony of mind and waste of energy which, as I shall tell later, he, like so many of his kind, incurred through this too ready acceptance of adult standards. Meanwhile it was inevitable that, before sex became an imperious need, Paul should adjust himself to adult standards in this as in so many other respects. Adults were objects which one had to learn to cope with, just as one had to learn to cope with dogs and chairs and fire. Sometimes one failed and suffered for it, but on the whole one succeeded.</p>
<p>I must not, however, dwell on the process by which Paul gradually mastered these earliest problems. In all his adventures, I, his parasite, tasted his success and failure. I suffered all his bumps, scratches and scaldings. I saw beforehand what was impending. I saw both that it was inevitable and that with a little more skill it might have been avoided. Had I chosen, I might have played the mother to him and saved him from many a disaster, but it was better to let him learn. During his earliest Years I refrained from any kind of influence, since it was important that he should develop normally up to a certain stage.</p>
<p>Little by little Paul’s world crystallized into extensive patterns. No longer a meagre and obscure flux of dream-like forms, it became a more or less reliable world of ‘common sense’, a house and garden, with surrounding continents made up of other houses and gardens, streets and fields. Gradually also he was able to look backwards not merely into a confused cloud of pastness but along a brief but orderly vista of nights and days.</p>
<p>But with this gradual increase of order in his daylight world, Paul became more and more distressed by that other chaotic world which swallowed him at night. When the light was put out and he was left alone in bed, the whole reliable order vanished. Even if he kept his eyes shut, terrifying shades confronted him with their vague and shifty forms. He saw animal heads and shoulders, and stealthily moving gorilla arms. They kept changing from one thing into another thing. They were unintelligible and therefore terrible.</p>
<p>Still worse, sometimes the seething confusion of shadows would be dominated by a single staring eye. Paul could not escape it. Wherever he looked, there it was, watching him. Perhaps it was God’s eye, seeing right through him, coldly noting all his most secret wickedness. Perhaps God had done away with the world of day because Paul had been so naughty in it. Perhaps there was nothing now but Paul and God and the close dark, with one streak of light pretending to be under the door, to keep up appearances. Somehow the Eye seemed to him both very wise and very stupid, a vindictive clever thing, that intended to kill him just by gazing at him, for no reason but that he did not please the Eye. Sometimes Paul was so terrified that he screamed. Then an exasperated grown-up would appear and ask what on earth was the matter. He could never tell, for grown-ups were somehow blind to the night world, and would think him silly. But to me, Paul’s trouble was very real, and also intelligible. To Paul, though he knew it not, the night was the womb. When he was very sleepy, it promised rest and peace; but when he was not sleepy, he regarded it as a well of blackness, stuffiness, formlessness, negation. It threatened to swallow him back from the bright orderly extra-uterine world of day, with its complicated geography, its reliable persons, and its winds that came with obscure messages from still wider spheres.</p>
<p>Throughout Paul’s life this contrast of the day world and the night world lay at the back of his thinking and feeling, influencing it, perplexing it. As he grew older, however, the two worlds seemed to interpenetrate more and more, until at last he found that there was a day view of everything and a night view also. But which was the true view? Perhaps the night view of things was in fact just nightmare, a bad dream. But then perhaps it was the other way round. Perhaps the whole day universe was just a foolish dream of the eternal foetus imprisoned in the eternal womb. In early days, of course, Paul never thought of the problem in this way, but I myself could see how the obscure movements of his mind were related to the forgotten experience of birth.</p>
<p>There was one aspect of the night which seemed even to the child Paul very different from the night which imprisoned him in bed, namely the starry sky. He seldom saw it, but even a brief glimpse of it would have lasting effect on him. It gave him a sort of calm elation which fortified him against the terrors of darkness, and would sometimes even last into the next day, soothing his troubles and tempering his joys. This experience, which played an important part in Paul’s life, was not simply a spontaneous outcome of his own nature. It was in part due to my presence in his mind, though in part also to his native disposition. Throughout his life, these occasions on which he was able to regard the night sky had a very considerable effect on me, the imprisoned and often fatigued observer of all his experience. They afforded me refreshing glimpses of something common to his world and my own. When I had become too deeply immersed in the minutiae of Paul’s behaviour, and was beginning to feel your world more real than mine, the sight of the stars, even through the inefficient eyes of a member of your species, would recall to me with great vividness the Neptunian plains and dwarf jungles, the sky-piercing towers and bland inhabitants of my own far-future world. This vivid recollection would rekindle in me the still, flame-like ardour which is the spirit of our doomed but unperturbed community. And the serene emotion which then surged through me would infect Paul also, lending a tone to his experience, which, though he could never properly account for it, he came in time to recognize as in some manner a gift from a source beyond his mundane nature. I well remember the hush of surprise and gurgle of delight with which the child Paul first greeted the stars. He was in his mother’s arms. He stretched out his own fat arms and turned his head slowly from side to side, surveying the heaven. On another occasion, much later, when he was being brought home from a Christmas party, so tired that he could hardly walk, a brilliant night sky overpowered him so that he burst into tears of uncomprehending joy.</p>
<p>As the years advanced, Paul’s sense that the starry night was present even by day became an increasing influence in his life. This was partly my doing; for I soon found that by giving him visual images of the night sky in the midst of mundane situations I could help him to regard the affairs of himself and his companions with Uranian detachment. There was an early stage in his development when, through this action of mine, he came to think of the universe as a sandwich made of the upper and nether night, with, between them, the meat of day. Below was the horrible darkness and confusion and closeness which he encountered in his stuffy bedroom. Above was the high mystery of the stars. Between was the world of familiar things.</p>
<p>This middle world had already spread outwards to become a vague expanse of countries, embracing somewhere beyond the familiar streets all kinds of wonders, which were nevertheless definitely day-time wonders. Somewhere there was India, full of tigers, elephants, jewelled princes, and jugglers. Elsewhere lay Iceland, all solid ice, with rivers of fire running down the crystal volcanoes. Then there was France, where they ate frogs and snails and were terribly polite; Germany, where they made cheap copies of English toys, and fought duels; Greece, where they wore no clothes except helmets with clothes-brushes on them; Egypt, all mummies and crocodiles; Fairyland, where magic happened more easily than in England. Yet even in England, magic was not unknown. There was Santa Claus, and dozens of lucky and unlucky things to be sought and avoided.</p>
<p>Over the whole world of countries hung the sky, a blue or cloudy ceiling. Here a discrepancy entered into Paul’s universe. If you thought about it from the day-time point of view, you believed that beyond the sky and the stars there was heaven, full of winged angels in nightgowns, their eyes always turned upwards. And above them again was God, whom Paul had somehow come to think of as a huge grown-up, sprawling from horizon to horizon, and looking down precisely at Paul, with eyes that saw even his thoughts. Somehow those eyes were the same as the Eye that stared at him in the nether darkness of his bedroom. When Paul remembered the Eye by day, he would repeat rapidly, ‘Oh God, I love you, I love you, I love you.’</p>
<p>But if you thought of things from the point of view of the starry night, God and the angels did not seem to fit in very well. Paul half believed that they were pictures painted on the ceiling of the day world.</p>
<p>In time, of course, Paul’s day world ceased to be flat, and became a huge ball. At this stage the universe was more like a dumpling than a sandwich. Vaguely Paul still conceived the three levels of existence. The nether night was deep down within the ball of the day world. The starry night was all around it. On the ball were all the countries except Fairyland, which was nowhere. Many of the countries were British Possessions, and red. At school he learned much about England and the Empire, and became a patriot. The English were — well, English. They had the greatest empire in the world. They were the only people who played fair in games, and were kind to animals, and could govern black people, and fight a losing battle to the end, and rule the seas. And because they were such fine people, God had hidden a lot of coal and iron under their country, so that they could use it for ships and engines and for making thousands of things that other countries were not clever enough to make for themselves. God had also written the Bible in English, because it was the best language, and the angels talked it.</p>
<p>Paul’s patriotism was largely an affectation. At this time he had a great longing to be like his fellows, and his fellows made it very obvious that they were patriotic. Also he found in patriotism a source of self-gratulation. Somehow or other, through patriotism, the worm that was the ordinary Paul became one of the lords of the earth. But there was also another factor at work. He had already begun to feel an obscure impulse to devote himself to ends beyond private gratification. Much of this sadly misconceived yearning for lofty ends was expressed in bragging about his school and vilifying other schools; but the Empire afforded it a more imposing object.</p>
