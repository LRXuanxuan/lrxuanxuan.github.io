<h2>ii. 调查</h2>
<p>保罗如今把全部闲暇都投入这场大规模探索。</p>
<p>他先从较谨慎的方式开始：大量阅读现代文学，同朋友谈政治和哲学。</p>
<p>这些事情当然都不是第一次做；但从前，他抱的是游客心态，而且还是个多少有些茫然的游客。如今，他却带着激情和一种连自己都觉得近乎奇迹的自信来做。</p>
<p>这个开端很快把他引向更系统的研究。他先仔细考察整个教师行业——从中小学一直到大学——的心态，继而又把范围扩展到几乎所有职业和心理类型，从修路工一直到内阁大臣。</p>
<p>在我持续帮助下，令他自己也惊讶的是，这个一向沉默退缩、过去总痛苦地意识到自己与同类之间隔着一道墙的年轻教师，开始主动去寻找一切可能对任务有意义的接触。</p>
<p>他惊奇地发现，自己竟然很会设计一些不冒犯人的办法，同收藏目标中各种各样的人结识。</p>
<p>事实上，他进行工作的心态非常像一个爱自己标本的收藏家：热切希望通过足够丰富的个案，发现这些“标本”存在的规律。</p>
<p>如果某一天，他忽然想到某个知名人物，或者某个不起眼的邻居，也许能给自己一些启发，那么他就会被一种猛烈、几乎具有性欲性质的渴望折磨：非见到这个被选中的人不可。</p>
<p>在达到目的以前，他会想尽一切办法。</p>
<p>在我的影响下，他还发展出一种很迷人的社交能力；不过，这种能力只在某些特殊情形下才会启动。</p>
<p>在普通而琐碎的社交场合，他通常仍然笨拙、畏缩，而且相当令人厌烦。</p>
<p>可是，一旦局面与他的任务有关，他就像突然活了过来，变成另一个人——而且他自己非常喜欢做这个人。</p>
<p>这不只是因为他发现自己有谈话天赋。</p>
<p>有时候，他几乎什么也不说。</p>
<p>但他说出来的少数几句话却会对听者产生惊人的效果：它们会把对方慢慢“引出来”，迫使这个人清楚看见自己的工作和目标，甚至逼他卸下负担，做出某种坦白；到最后，对方也许会说：</p>
<p>“对，我真正面对世界的方式大概就是这样，只是直到今天，我几乎从没这么想过。”</p>
<p>另一些时候，保罗反而会说得非常多，对对方所承担的社会功能作出评论，语气奇怪地既谦逊又笃定。</p>
<p>他还经常发现，自己会为了眼前这个具体场合临时“变成”一种合适的人格，戴上一副特别适合向眼前这个男人或女人显得聪明而有同情心的面具。</p>
<p>保罗有时会被自己这种演戏般的行为吓一跳，觉得它不够真诚；但因为效果实在惊人，他仍继续这样做。</p>
<p>而严格说来，这其实也并不虚伪，因为它只是他如今对他人心灵拥有强烈想象性洞察的自然结果。</p>
<p>有时，他会同时碰上两个差异极大、而过去分别认识的人。这时候，他就很难同时对两人戴上“正确面具”而又不激怒另一个。</p>
<p>举一个比较粗浅的例子：星期一，他赢得了一个共产党党员的信任；星期二，又获得一个“英国爱国者”的好感；星期三，他偏偏撞见两个人正在激烈争吵。</p>
<p>两人起初都把他当成盟友欢迎。</p>
<p>可等保罗终于逃掉以前，他已经先后被两个人各自痛骂了一顿。</p>
<p>保罗这项天赋还有一个奇怪限制，令他十分苦恼。</p>
<p>他非常擅长让别人更清楚地意识到自己深层的思想与欲望，却完全没有能力让别人理解他自己的思想。</p>
<p>他刚刚经历过强烈的精神体验，对当代世界的把握也日益深入，可一谈到这些事情，他就是完全表达不出来。</p>
<p>这其实并不奇怪，因为他不是天才，更不是先知；除了我需要他、并在其中激发他的那个特定职能之外，他依旧缺乏表达能力。</p>
<p>随着几个月、几年过去，这种缺陷越来越折磨他。</p>
<p>因为他越来越确信，现代世界正朝一场巨大灾难前进；除了大多数人醒来，进入一种新的心境、获得更深洞察之外，什么也救不了它。</p>
<p>在自己的冥想中，他越来越清楚地知道这种觉醒究竟应该是什么样子。</p>
<p>可是，他就是无法向别人说清楚。</p>
<p>一旦试着表达，不是结结巴巴，就是干脆说不出话。</p>
<p>问题一部分在于他本人确实无能；另一部分则在于，在我的帮助下，他同时看见了一个真理的两个侧面，而他的同伴几乎从来不能把这个真理作为一个立体整体来看。</p>
<p>所以，每一个同保罗交谈的人几乎必然对他核心信念的某一面完全失明。</p>
<p>有人无法理解他那种责任感：人类在自己这颗行星上的事业既英勇，又具有宇宙层面的紧迫性。</p>
<p>另一些人则无法理解他看似与此矛盾的另一种认识：宇宙作为整体已经完成，而且拥有一种非人的美。</p>
<p>保罗同所谓“知识阶层”相处时，这种思想瘫痪尤其痛苦。</p>
<p>他结识过许多作家和艺术家。</p>
<p>起初，他总像狗一样尊敬这些才华横溢的人，相信自己终于来到了某个已经突破旧世界、触及新真理的人面前。</p>
<p>可不知为什么，他同任何一个人的关系都无法深入太久。</p>
<p>最开始，他们会很喜欢保罗，因为他能刺激他们，以新的清晰度理解自己的“视野”。</p>
<p>可这些人的视野通常并没有多深，所以没过多久，无论他们自己还是保罗，都已经把其中值得看的东西看完了。</p>
<p>到了这一步，保罗的天赋就会毫无例外地消失。</p>
<p>从此以后，他们只觉得他乏味、资产阶级、不值得继续认识。</p>
<p>他再也抓不住他们的注意力。</p>
<p>当真正深层的话题枯竭以后，他又缺少维持社交所必需的那种源源不断的私人闲话。</p>
<p>另一方面，如果他真敢把自己的观点告诉这些人，只会把自己弄得像个傻瓜；对方不是善意嘲笑，就是根本不理。</p>
<p>因此，虽然他渐渐熟悉了伦敦知识生活的门路，却从未真正被任何圈子或学派接纳。</p>
<p>几乎每个圈子都曾在某个时候允许他待在外围，可他始终默默无闻，因为没有谁会觉得值得向别人提起他。</p>
<p>于是，他一直保持局外人身份，总能在一旁观察，而无需把信仰押给某套教条，也无需把智力押给某个理论。</p>
<p>每一种教条、每一种理论，他都暗中拿自己最近获得的启示去检验；迟早又都会拒绝。</p>
<p>正因为如此，保罗极好地服务了我的目的。</p>
<p>组织切片放在显微镜下研究时，可以先染色，使本来不可见的结构细节显现出来。</p>
<p>保罗也一样：我用一点“海王星视觉”给他染色，于是他那原始心灵的各种器官以极其精细的方式显露出来——有些结构吸收这种染色，有些则不吸收。</p>
<p>显然，要把实验的全部意义真正展示给本书读者是不可能的，因为读者自己也受到保罗那些局限的限制。</p>
<p>在这些异常拥挤而充实的年月里，保罗始终还在学校完成日常工作；不过，随着注意力越来越多被调查占据，教学不可避免地受到影响。</p>
<p>他如今把所有空闲时间、所有假期，都花在探索任务上。</p>
<p>这是一种奇怪生活：人与人的接触丰富得惊人，却又异常孤独。</p>
<p>他不敢把自己活动的真正目的、更不敢把其中海王星那一面告诉任何人，否则别人一定会把他当成疯子。</p>
<p>在朋友看来，他似乎只是被某种奇怪的“好奇虫”咬了一口；他们说，这只虫让他患上一种毫无目标的调查狂，而且正在毁掉本职工作。</p>
<p>为了推进项目，他还参加了许多活动。</p>
<p>这些事情他没有一件做得特别好，因为内心深处，他始终只把它们当成追求秘密目的的手段。</p>
<p>他替一个政党工作；给工人教育协会组织的班级讲课；参与贫困地区的社会调查。</p>
<p>他还经常出没于某些酒馆，参加宗教复兴聚会；同盗贼、骗子乃至一两个尚未被抓住的杀人犯混熟。</p>
<p>他发现，这些人非常感激有机会在他那种神秘、超然，却又具有理解力和同情心的气氛中，把压在心里的东西全说出来。</p>
<p>他接触过许多妓女和妓院经营者。</p>
<p>他带着尊重探索同性恋者的心灵，也探索其他那些欲望与周围人大不相同的人。</p>
<p>他走进许多充满痛苦的房间，守在许多临终者床边。</p>
<p>矿村欢迎过他，贫民窟住宅也欢迎过他。</p>
<p>他在工匠家里讨论革命。</p>
<p>同样，他也进入银行经理、船东和大型工业雇主的住宅。</p>
<p>他同主教交谈，也同科学界的显贵交谈；可对流浪汉也同样热切——有时两个人就坐在树篱后面，分享偷来的美食。</p>
<p>有一年夏天，他有一段时间去当码头工人；另一个夏天，又到英格兰西部做收割短工。</p>
<p>他坐最低等舱搭乘移民船，又在一艘沿波罗的海海岸航行的不定期货轮上靠工作换船票。</p>
<p>他去俄国做过一次短暂却内容极其密集的朝圣旅行，回家时已经成了一个“有所不同的共产党人”。</p>
<p>他徒步、骑自行车游历西欧；在英国，则接触许多来自东方的访客。</p>
<p>同时，他竟还挤得出时间追踪当代文学，泡在艺术家工作室里，和剑桥那些聪明的年轻哲学家讨论认识论与伦理学。</p>
<p>在所有这些工作中，他发现自己始终受到某种内在力量持续、而且有时相当严厉的引导。</p>
<p>虽然我再也没有公开同他交流，但他知道这股力量来自我。</p>
<p>因此，他获得一种几乎不会出错的选择与侦查天赋。</p>
<p>在我的帮助下，他几年之间便扫过现代生活的整个领域，却几乎从不把力量浪费在毫无结果的探索上。</p>
<p>他像鹰一样准确地扑向真正重要的个人和运动，也像鹰一样自信地忽略不相干的东西。</p>
<p>有时候，他自己的冲动会同我的引导冲突。</p>
<p>这时，他会发现自己受到一种神秘、内在、近乎催眠的冲动支配：要么放弃本来计划做的事，要么开始一次自己的天性原本丝毫不想参加的冒险。</p>
<p>一月又一月，一年又一年，保罗心中逐渐形成一幅新的、异常清楚的世界图景。</p>
<p>它既可怕又精美，既悲剧又滑稽。</p>
<p>要向别人说明保罗这幅新图景到底是什么样，并不容易，因为它的力量在很大程度上来自他近年经验那种庞大的复杂性和多样性。</p>
<p>他强烈感觉到：无数个体像虫群一样布满整颗行星；有些地方稀疏散落，有些地方则挤成巨大的人类团块；这里的人被机器驱赶，那里的人被压进土地里，只能从泥土中吸取一点勉强维生的食物。</p>
<p>他们说着成千上万种彼此听不懂的语言；生活方式在别人看来不是可憎，就是可笑；使用的概念彼此难以理解；甚至崇拜的方式也互相令人反感。</p>
<p>这种关于“人类数量和多样性”的新感觉，又被他对个体性的增强认识进一步加深。</p>
<p>无论在自己还是别人身上，他都越来越强烈地意识到个体真实存在。</p>
<p>他带着敬畏认识到：这支摧残地球的巨大蝗虫军团中，每一个单独单位都随身携带着一个被自己认识的完整宇宙；每一个都是一件响亮乐器，奏着一段强烈、又极端自我中心的心灵主题。</p>
<p>因为到这时，保罗已经非常彻底地学会：以我童年时第一次从他身上诱发出的那种穿透性洞察，去感受所有人以及整个世界的现实性。</p>
<p>另一方面，他又从未真正忘记群星。</p>
<p>所以，一边是他对人在宇宙中渺小程度的感觉，一边是他新获得的关于人类物质规模与精神强度的感觉，两者之间的撞击反而使他的惊异越来越强。</p>
<p>于是，尽管他极其清楚地感到日常事物具有不可取消的现实性，他同时又怀有一种压倒性确信：整张普通经验的织物——甚至人类、生物学、天文学共同认可的全部事实宇宙——虽然都是真实的，却遮住了某个更加庞大的现实。</p>
<p>不能忘记，在整个探索过程中，保罗始终敏锐地意识到我存在于他内部。</p>
<p>他之所以感觉还有某种更深、更隐蔽的现实，一部分原因正是他知道：在自己内部，或者说在自己之上，隐藏着一个更高层次的存在。</p>
<p>他知道我一直在他的眼睛后面，通过他的耳朵留心倾听，不断思索并筛选流过他大脑的各种活动。</p>
<p>可是，除非我用一些无法解释的渴望和抗拒去推动他，否则他完全察觉不到我的存在。</p>
<p>还应当记住：我以两种不同方式使用保罗。</p>
<p>他既是透明的观察仪器，也是你们这种心智的一份样本。</p>
<p>我感兴趣的不只是他记录下来的外部事实，也包括他对这些事实的反应，以及他自己的评价与我的评价之间有什么差异。</p>
<p>这种差异大致可以这样表达：我主要以超然态度观看地球领域，尽管这种超然内部仍包含极强烈的想象性同情；保罗则主要从人类立场看待一切，不过他能够通过持续努力，把自己也维持在那个更高平面上。</p>
<p>对我而言，最大的想象力胜利，是能够如此充分地进入你们这个遥远而奇异的世界。</p>
<p>对保罗而言，最大的胜利则是：尽管他强烈地属于人类，充满同情和愤慨，却仍能以近乎天体般的超然观看自己的世界。</p>
<p>因此，我们两个仿佛两件完全不同的乐器，以两种不同速度彼此对奏，最后共同产生一个复杂统一的节奏。</p>
<p>不过，对海王星智能而言，即使保罗最经过节制的热情，也带着一种略令人作呕的悲怆味道。</p>
<p>只有因为我在他内部施加那种隐秘而带讽刺性的影响，他才不至于把自己世界的命运看得过分沉重。</p>
<p>一颗刚刚接受了具有支配力的启示、并因此被提升到日常琐碎生活之上的心灵，在献身时很容易滑向某种夸张，甚至某种滑稽的庄严。</p>
<p>海王星人的心灵则不同。我们生来就在这种信念之中，不需要通过一句句格言式的“皈依”才得到它。</p>
<p>所以，保罗那种郑重其事有时甚至会迫使我们怀疑：就连人类最终在海王星上的那场悲剧，究竟更值得悲伤，还是更值得发笑？</p>
</section>

<h2>ii. THE RESEARCH</h2>
<p>PAUL NOW DEVOTED all his leisure to his great exploration. He began tentatively, by reading much modern literature, and talking politics and philosophy with his friends. Both these occupations were of course familiar to him; but whereas formerly they had been pursued in the spirit of the tourist, a rather bewildered tourist, now they were carried out with passion and an assurance which to Paul himself seemed miraculous. This beginning led him on to make a careful study of the mentality of the whole teaching profession, both in schools and universities; and this in turn developed into a wide and profound research into all manner of mental types and occupations from road-menders to cabinet ministers. With my constant help, and to his own surprise, the silent and retiring young schoolmaster, who had always been so painfully conscious of the barrier between himself and his kind, began actively to seek out whatever contacts promised to be significant for his task. With amazement he discovered in himself a talent for devising inoffensive methods of making acquaintance with all sorts of persons whom he needed for his collection. Indeed, he pursued his work very much in the spirit of a collector in love with his specimens, and eager to discover by wealth of instances the laws of their being. If one day he happened to conceive that such and such a well-known personage or humble neighbour could give him light, he would be tormented by a violent, almost sexual, craving to meet the chosen one, and would leave no stone unturned until he had achieved his end.</p>
<p>Under my influence he also developed a captivating power of social intercourse, which, however, would only come into action in special circumstances. In the ordinary trivial social occasions he remained as a rule clumsy, diffident and tiresome; but in any situation which was relevant to his task, he seemed to come alive, to be a different person, and one that he thoroughly delighted to be. It was not merely that he discovered a gift of conversation. Sometimes he would say almost nothing. But what he did say would have a startling effect on his hearer, drawing him out, compelling him to get a clear view of his own work and aims, forcing him to unburden himself in a confession, such that at the close of it he would perhaps declare, ‘Yes, that is really how I face the world, though till today I hardly thought of it that way.’ Sometimes, however, Paul himself would be almost voluble, commenting with a strangely modest assurance on the other’s function; Often he would find himself assuming for the occasion an appropriate personality, a mask peculiarly suited to appear intelligent and sympathetic to this particular man or woman. Paul was sometimes shocked at the insincerity of this histrionic behaviour; though he pursued it, for the sake of its extraordinary effectiveness. And indeed it was not really insincere, for it was but the natural result of his new and intense imaginative insight into other minds. Sometimes when he was in the presence of two very different individuals whom he had previously encountered apart, he would be hard put to it to assume for each the right mask without rousing the other’s indignation. Thus, to give a rather crude example of this kind of predicament, having on Monday won the confidence of a member of the Communist Party, and on Tuesday the benevolence of a British Patriot, he happened on Wednesday to come upon them both together in violent altercation. Both at first greeted him as an ally, but before he fled he had been reviled by each in turn.</p>
<p>One curious limitation of Paul’s talent he found very distressing. Brilliant as he was at evoking in others a clearer consciousness of their own deeper thoughts and desires, he was incapable of impressing them with his own ideas. Strong in his own recent spiritual experiences, and in his increasing grasp of the contemporary world, he yet failed completely to express himself on these subjects. This was but natural, for he was no genius, still less a prophet; and, save in the office in which I needed him and inspired him, he remained inarticulate. This incapacity disturbed him increasingly as the months and years passed. For increasingly he became convinced that the modern world was heading toward a huge disaster, and that nothing could save it but the awakening of the mass of men into a new mood and greater insight. More and more clearly he saw in his own meditations just what this awakening must be. Yet he could never make it clear to others, for as soon as he tried to express himself, he fell stammering or mute. The trouble lay partly in his incompetence, but partly also in the fact that through my help he had seen both sides of a truth which to his fellows was almost never revealed thus in the solid. Paul’s interlocutor, therefore, was sure to be blind either to one or to the other aspect of Paul’s central conviction; either to his sense of obligation in the heroic and cosmically urgent enterprise of man upon his planet, or to his seemingly inconsistent perception of the finished, the inhuman, beauty of the cosmos.</p>
<p>Most painfully when he was with members of the ‘intelligentsia’ did his mental paralysis seize him. He made contact with many writers and artists. Each of these brilliant beings he treated at first with doglike respect, believing that at last he had come into the presence of one who had broken into new truth. But somehow he never went very far with any of them. For a while he delighted them, because he stimulated them to apprehend their own vision with a new clarity. But since their visions were not as a rule very profound, they and he soon saw all there was to see in them. At this point Paul’s talent invariably disappeared, and henceforth they thought him dull, bourgeois, not worth knowing. He could no longer hold their attention. He had not the necessary flow of personal tittle-tattle to carry him through when deeper interests flagged. On the other hand, if ever he dared to tell them his own views, he merely made a fool of himself, and was treated either with kindly ridicule or with inattention. Thus, though he came to know his way about the intellectual life of London, he was never taken into any circle or school. Most circles had at one time or other tolerated him on their fringes; yet he remained unknown, for no one ever bothered to mention him to anyone else. Thus it happened that he was always apart, always able to look on without pledging his faith to any creed, his intellect to any theory. Every creed, every theory, he secretly tested and sooner or later rejected in the light of his own recent illumination. In this manner Paul served my purpose admirably. As a tissue-section, to be studied under the microscope, may be stained to bring out details of structure that would otherwise have remained invisible, so Paul, whom I had treated with a tincture of Neptunian vision, revealed in exquisite detail the primitive organs of his mind, some of which took the stain and others not. Obviously it is impossible to reveal the whole issue of the experiment to readers of this book, since they themselves suffer from Paul’s limitations.</p>
<p>Throughout these crowded years Paul still carried on his daily work at the school, though inevitably as his attention became more and more occupied, his teaching suffered. All his free time, all his holidays, he now spent on his task of exploration. It was a strange life, so rich in human intercourse, and yet so lonely; for he dared not tell anyone of the real purpose of his activities, of their Neptunian aspect, lest he should bethought insane. To his friends he appeared to have been bitten by some queer bug of curiosity, which, they said, had filled him with a quite aimless mania for inquiry, and was ruining his work.</p>
<p>To aid his project he undertook a number of enterprises, none of which he did well, since at heart he regarded them only as means of the pursuit of his secret purpose. He worked for a political party, lectured to classes organized by the Workers’ Educational Association, collaborated in a social survey of a poor district. Also he haunted certain public-houses, attended revivalist meetings, became intimate with burglars, swindlers, and one or two uncaught murderers, who, he found, were extremely thankful for the opportunity of unburdening themselves under the spell of his mysteriously aloof sympathy and understanding. He made contact with many prostitutes and keepers of brothels. He respectfully explored the minds of homosexuals, and others whose hungers did not conform to the lusts of their fellows. He found his way into many chambers of suffering, and was present at many death-beds. He was welcomed in mining villages, and in slum tenements. He discussed revolution in the homes of artisans. Equally he was received in the houses of bank managers, of ship-owners, of great industrial employers. He conferred with bishops, and also with the dignitaries of science, but no less eagerly with vagabonds; over stolen delicacies, seated behind hedges. Part of one summer he spent as a dock labourer, and part of another as a harvest hand in the West of England. He travelled steerage on an emigrant ship, and worked his passage on a tramp steamer along the Baltic coasts. He made a brief but crowded pilgrimage to Russia, and came home a Communist, with a difference. He tramped and bicycled in Western Europe; and in England he made contact with many visitors from the East. Yet also he found time to keep abreast of contemporary literature, to haunt studios, to discuss epistemology and ethics with bright young Cambridge philosophers.</p>
<p>In all this work he found that he was constantly and sometimes sternly guided by an inner power, which, though I never again openly communicated with him, he knew to spring from me. Thus he was endowed with an infallible gift of selection and of detective inquiry. Through my help he covered in a few years the whole field of modern life, yet he never wasted his strength on vain explorations. With the precision of a hawk he descended upon the significant individual, the significant movement; and with a hawk’s assurance he neglected the irrelevant. Sometimes his own impulses would run counter to my guidance. Then he would find himself directed by a mysterious, an inner and hypnotic, impulsion, either to give up what he had planned to do, or to embark on some adventure for which his own nature had no inclination.</p>
<p>Month by month, year by year, there took shape in Paul’s mind a new and lucid image of his world, an image at once terrible and exquisite, tragic and farcical. It is difficult to give an idea of this new vision of Paul’s, for its power depended largely on the immense intricacy and diversity of his recent experience; on his sense of the hosts of individuals swarming upon the planet, here sparsely scattered, there congested into great clusters and lumps of humanity, here machine-ridden, there ground into the earth from which they sucked their scanty livelihood. Speaking in ten thousand mutually incomprehensible dialects, living in manners reprehensible or ludicrous to one another, thinking by concepts unintelligible to one another, they worshipped in modes repugnant to one another. This new sense of the mere bulk and variety of men was deepened in Paul’s mind by his enhanced apprehension of individuality in himself and others, his awed realization that each single unit in all these earth-devastating locust armies carried about with it a whole cognized universe, was the plangent instrument of an intense and self-important theme of mind. For Paul had by now learned very thoroughly to perceive the reality of all human beings and of the world with that penetrating insight which I had first elicited from him in childhood. On the other hand, since he was never wholly forgetful of the stars, the shock between his sense of human littleness in the cosmos and his new sense of man’s physical bulk and spiritual intensity increased his wonder. Thus in spite of his perception of the indefeasible reality of everyday things, he had also an overwhelming conviction that the whole fabric of common experience, nay the whole agreed universe of human and biological and astronomical fact, though real, concealed some vaster reality.</p>
<p>It must not be forgotten that throughout his exploration Paul was jealously mindful of my presence within him, and that his sense of an ulterior, a concealed, reality, was derived partly from the knowledge that within himself, or above himself, there was concealed a more exalted being. He knew that I was ever behind his eyes, ever attentive in his ears, ever pondering and sifting the currents of his brain. Yet, save when I swayed him with unaccountable cravings and reluctances, my presence was wholly unperceived.</p>
<p>It must be remembered that I used Paul in two manners, both as a transparent instrument of observation, and as a sample of your mentality. I was interested not only in the external facts which he recorded, but in his reaction to them, in the contrast between his appraisement of them and my own. That contrast I may express by saying that whereas I myself regarded the Terrestrial sphere primarily with detachment, though with detachment which held within itself a passion of imaginative sympathy, Paul regarded all things primarily from the human point of view, though he was able also by a continuous effort to maintain himself upon the loftier plane. For me it was a triumph of imagination that I could enter so fully into your remote and fantastic world. For Paul it was a triumph that in spite of his fervent humanity, his compassion and indignation, he could now regard his world with celestial aloofness. Thus the two of us together were like two different musical instruments playing against one another in two different tempos to produce a single intricacy of rhythm.</p>
<p>To the Neptunian intelligence, however, there was a slightly nauseating pathos even in Paul’s most chastened enthusiasm. Only through the secret and ironical influence of my presence within him could he avoid taking the fate of his world too much to heart. The mind that has but lately received a commanding revelation which exalts it above the pettiness of daily life is very prone to a certain extravagance, a certain farcical pomp, in its devotion. The Neptunian mind, on the other hand, born into the faith and needing no sententious conversion, was sometimes impelled by Paul’s solemnity to wonder whether even the final tragedy of man on Neptune called most for grief or for laughter.</p>
