<h3>第十四章　工程问题</h3>
<p>约翰告诉我他为接触其他“超常者”所做的努力后没多久，又把自己对未来的计划告诉了我。我们当时在地下工作间里。他正全神贯注于一项新发明，说是一种发电—蓄能装置。工作台上堆满了试管、玻璃罐、金属碎片、瓶子、绝缘导线、电压表和石块。他投入得如此彻底，我忍不住说：“我看你是在退回童年。这一类东西又把你迷住了，连——苏格兰那一切都忘了。”</p>
<p>“不，你错了，”他说，“这个小玩意是我计划中很重要的一环。等我做完这次试验就告诉你。”他默不作声地继续实验。过了一会儿，他忽然低低欢呼一声：“这次成了！”</p>
<p>我们一边喝咖啡，一边讨论他的计划。他已经下定决心，要搜索整个世界，希望能找到少数几个自己的同类，而且年龄合适，能够同他一起在地球某个偏僻角落建立一个小小的超常者殖民地。为了不浪费时间，他说，自己必须拥有一艘能远洋航行的游艇，还要有一架小型飞机，或者某种飞行器，可以收纳在游艇上。我抗议说，他对飞行根本一无所知，更不用说设计飞机了。他回答：“噢，不，我懂。我昨天刚学会飞。”原来，他设法说服某位才华横溢的年轻飞行员，不仅带他兜了一圈，还让他长时间亲自操纵飞机。“一旦找到感觉，”他说，“也没多难。我降落了两次，起飞了两次，还做了几个特技。当然，要学的东西还多得很。至于设计，我已经在干了，游艇也一样。不过很多东西都取决于这个新装置。我不太好解释。至少，我能解释个大概，可你就是不会相信。最近我一直在研究核化学，而结合我在苏格兰的那些经历，我忽然想到一个办法。大概连你也知道——虽说你在与科学保持脱节这方面简直天赋异禀——每个原子核里都锁着多得要命的能量。之所以没法把它释放出来，是因为开锁需要强得荒唐的电流，好克服把电子、质子之类东西束缚在一起的那些力。嗯，我找到了一把方便得多的钥匙。不过它根本不是物理钥匙，而是一把心理钥匙。没必要试图硬压过那些可怕的相互锁定力。你只要暂时把它们取消掉；可以说，让它们睡着。那些彼此锁定的力，还有造成崩裂的力，说到底不过是基本物理单位的自发冲动——你愿意的话，就把它们叫电子和质子。于是，我做的事情就是把这些小混蛋催眠，让它们一瞬间软下来，松开彼此。等它们醒来，就会兴高采烈地自由乱撞；你要做的，只是确保它们乱撞时能推动你的机器。”</p>
<p>我笑了，说这个寓言挺不错。“去他的寓言，”他说，“它之所以算寓言，只不过因为‘质子’和‘电子’本身就是寓言里虚构出来的人物。它们根本不是真正独立存在的实体，而是一个系统——宇宙——内部的规定性。并且它们也不真正只是物理的，而是一个心理—物理系统中的规定性。当然，如果你把‘智人版’物理学当作上帝的终极真理，而不是更深层真理的一种抽象，那这个想法听起来当然疯透了。可我觉得值得研究，而结果证明它能工作。当然，困难还是有。最大的困难是心理上的。‘智人’心灵永远做不到这一招；它还没有醒到那个程度。可超常者拥有必要的影响力，而且练习之后，这件事就会变得相当安全、相当容易。至于物理上的困难，”他说着瞥了一眼自己的装置，“主要都跟两件事有关：选择最合适的原子作为对象，以及在能量洪流开始释放时把它引出来。我现在就在解决这些问题。河口里普通的泥就很适合干这个。里面含有极微量的一种非常方便的元素。”</p>
<p>他用一把镊子从试管里夹出一小撮泥，放进一个铂金小碗。他打开工作间的活板门，把小碗放到外面，然后回来，把门几乎完全合上。我们俩从留下的缝隙望着那只小碗。他笑着说：“现在，所有小电子、小质子都乖乖睡觉，等妈妈叫你们再醒。”随后转向我补充：“顺便说一句，这些台词是讲给观众听的，不是讲给魔术师帽子里的兔子听的。”</p>
<p>他的脸上浮现出极其严肃的专注神情，呼吸也加快了。“现在！”他说。外面猛地爆出一道骇人的闪光，紧接着是一声枪响似的爆炸。</p>
<p>约翰拿一块脏兮兮的手帕擦了擦额头，评论道：“我一个人做到了！”然后我们回去继续喝咖啡，也继续谈他的计划。</p>
<p>“我还得找到一种真正好用的办法，把能量装瓶保存到需要的时候。你不可能一边催眠电子，一边给船导航。我也许只能把这股能量拿去驱动发电机，再给蓄电池充电。不过还有一种更有意思的可能。等我把那些小混蛋催眠以后，也许能给它们一种类似‘催眠后暗示’的东西，让它们只有受到某个特定刺激时，才会重新醒来、重新乱撞。明白吗？”</p>
<p>我笑了。我们俩都抿了口咖啡。这里不妨直接说明，后来这种“催眠后”方案最终证明可行，并且真的被采用了。</p>
<p>“嗯，你看得出来，”他说，“我这套新把戏的可能性非常大。现在，在游艇和飞机建造期间，你得陪我去趟欧洲大陆。（我相信伯莎一定会很高兴放个假，暂时不用伺候你。）我想做些调查。巴黎显然有一个超常心灵，埃及也有一个，也许附近还有别的。等游艇和飞机准备好以后，我就环游世界，寻找其余的人。如果能找到几个合适的年轻家伙，我就去太平洋航行，找一座适合建立殖民地的岛。”</p>
<p>接下来的两个月里，约翰完全投入到实际工作中：设计游艇和飞机，完善新的动力技术，并提高自己的飞行水平。</p>
<p>这一时期，人们经常能看见他像普通男孩一样，在公园湖或者风浪更大的河口“玩船”。他已经十八岁多，可外表看起来还不到十五，因此这种行为完全不显得异常。他做了大量模型，装上电动机或者蒸汽机，无论天气怎样，都把它们送到湖面上航行，再极其仔细地观察表现。设计很大程度上受两项要求支配：飞机收起机翼后必须能装进船里；整艘船还必须具有极强的适航性。约翰最终选中的船型非常古怪，当地玩帆船的人只把它看成对“船”这种东西的滑稽模仿。他按最终设计专门做了一艘三英尺长的模型，而且细节装备得极为完整。大体形状上，它的船宽宽得荒唐，吃水却很浅，实际上是被夸张的快艇船体：像快艇和救生艇杂交，又仿佛祖先里还混进了一只碟子，也许还有一块适合打水漂的扁石头。毫无疑问，它是一件非常可爱的玩具；而我敢肯定，约翰也确实单纯把它当玩具享受过，而且花在上面的功夫远远超过实验本身的需要。这艘模型代表的是一条小型拖船大小的实际船只。装备细节一个不缺。有九个人的固定铺位，必要时二十个人也能挤着睡下；同时，一个人就能轻松操纵整条船。里面有做得像模像样的餐厅，有桌椅、橱柜；有厕所；有玻璃舷窗；还有极小的导航控制装置。这些控制甚至可以通过岸上的某种无线电设备远程操作。发动机则是约翰计划安装在真正船只上的亚原子发动机的相当精细的缩小复制品。</p>
<p>约翰让这艘模型做出的种种恶作剧，给我们带来了不少乐趣。在公园湖，他会让它慢悠悠追逐被吓坏的鸭子。在河口涨潮时，他又会让船停在离岸很远的海面，然后说服帆船俱乐部某位好心成员划小艇去“打捞”。等那个划得满头大汗的人终于靠近这艘小小弃船，伸手要抓时，站在岸上、离着半英里远的约翰就会让模型忽然往前跑一两码，看着那个人一次次重新努力靠近。最后，他会把速度一下开到最大，让模型直冲岸边；小船则像一条训练有素的狗，最终自动回到主人手里。</p>
<p>约翰同时也在制作好几架模型飞机。他常花很多时间放飞它们；不过这件事秘密进行，因为他担心如果别人看见这些模型做出那些惊人动作，一定会引来太多注意。因此，他通常骑摩托车，或者坐我的汽车，带着模型退到北威尔士的荒野里。在那里，他会利用多变的山风测试这些飞机；亚原子动力使它们能完成任何橡皮筋驱动模型都绝不可能做到的动作。</p>
<p>他最终选中的飞机同样是一件令人惊讶的机械，比例同那艘模型游艇一致，而且能够拆开，收纳在船上。约翰能拿这架短翼小机器折腾自己、也折腾我好几个小时：让它从一个“llyn”——当地语所谓的山湖——水面起飞（它同时装有轮子和浮筒），再一路爬升到天空高处，直到我们必须用望远镜才能追踪。它能够自动保持平衡，但航向由地面无线电操纵。等约翰熟练掌握这只机械鸟以后，他有时会拿它玩一种现代版鹰猎：让那只麻雀大小的小东西追逐杓鹬、鵟和乌鸦。这种游戏不仅需要非常精细的控制，也需要极敏锐的判断。通常，猎物一旦意识到自己被追，就会立刻加速逃走；飞机则一路驱赶，甚至俯冲到鸟背上。不过有一只老乌鸦转身迎战。约翰还没来得及利用玩具飞机更高的速度逃开，那只乌鸦坚硬的喙已经划破一侧丝制机翼，飞机翻滚着摔进石南丛中。</p>
<p>约翰还没满十九岁，游艇和飞机的设计图就全部完成了。我没必要细说自己怎样同造船厂和飞机制造商谈判，又怎样最终下单制造真正的成品。反正我很快得了个“疯掉的百万富翁”的名声：因为那些设计看起来根本不可能工作，而别人提出的任何反对意见，我一概不考虑。最主要的问题是，无论飞机还是游艇，设计中留给动力发生装置的空间，按照一切常规标准都小得完全不够。至于发电装置和其他机械的合同，则被分散给好几家工程公司，以尽可能减少外界好奇。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a344"></span>CHAPTER XIV. ENGINEERING PROBLEMS</h3>
<p><span class="t28">N</span><span class="t31">OT</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">LONG</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">AFTER</span><span class="t30"> </span>John told me of his efforts to make contact with other supernormals he took me into his confidence about his plans for the future. We were in the subterranean workshop. He was absorbed in a new invention, a sort of generator-accumulator, he said. His bench was covered with test-tubes, jars, bits of metal, bottles, insulated wires, voltmeters, lumps of stone. He was so intent on his work that I said, “I believe you’re regressing to childhood. This sort of thing has got hold of you again and made you forget all about — Scotland.”</p>
<p>“No, you’re wrong,” he said. “This gadget is an important part of my plan. When I have finished this test I’ll tell you.” Silently he proceeded with the experiment. Presently, with a little shout of triumph, he said, “Got it, this time!”</p>
<p>Over a cup of coffee we discussed his plans. He was determined to search the whole world in the hope of discovering a few others of his kind, and of suitable age for joining with him in the founding of a little colony of supernormals in some remote part of the earth. In order to do this without loss of time, he said, he must have an ocean-going yacht and a small aeroplane, or flying machine of some kind, which could be stowed on the yacht. When I protested that he knew nothing about flying and less about designing planes, he replied. “Oh yes, I do. I learned to fly yesterday.” It seems he had managed to persuade a certain brilliant young airman to give him not only a joy-ride but a long spell in control of the machine. “Once you get the feel of it,” he said, “it’s easy enough. I landed twice, and took off twice, and did a few stunts. But of course there’s a good deal more to learn. As for designing, I’m on the job already, and on the yacht design too. But a lot depends on this new gadget. I can’t explain it very well. At least, I can explain, in a way, but you just won’t believe it. I’ve been looking into nuclear chemistry lately, and in the light of my Scotch experiences an idea struck me. Probably even you know (though you have a genius for keeping out of touch with science) that there’s the hell of a lot of energy locked up in every atomic nucleus, and that the reason why you can’t release it is that the unlocking would take a fantastically powerful electric current, to overcome the forces that hold the electrons and protons, and so on, together. Well, I’ve found a much handier key. But it’s not a physical key at all but a psychical one. It’s no use trying to overcome those terrific interlocking forces. You must just abolish them for the time being; send them to sleep, so to speak. The interlocking forces, and the disruptive forces too, are just the spontaneous urges of the basic physical units, call them electrons and protons, if you like. What I do, then, is to hypnotize the little devils so that they go limp for a moment and loosen their grip on one another. Then when they wake up they barge about in hilarious freedom, and all you have to do is to see that their barging drives your machinery.”</p>
<p>I laughed, and said I like his parable. “Parable be damned,” he said. “It’s only a parable in the sense that the protons and electrons themselves are merely fictitious characters in a parable. They’re not really independent entities at all, but determinations within a system — the cosmos. And they’re not really just physical, but determinations within a psycho-physical system. Of course if you take ‘sapient’ physics as God’s truth, and not as an abstraction from a more profound truth, the whole idea seems crazy. But I thought it worth looking into, and I find it works. Of course there are difficulties. The main one is the psychological one. The ‘sapient’ mind could never do the trick; it’s not awake enough. But the supernormal has the necessary influence, and practice makes the job reasonably safe and easy. The physical difficulties,” he said, glancing at his apparatus, “are all connected with selecting the most favourable atoms to work on, and with tapping the flood of energy as it comes into action. I’m working on those problems now. Ordinary mud from the estuary is pretty good for the job. There’s a minute percentage of a very convenient element in it.”</p>
<p>With a pair of tweezers he took a pinch of mud from a test-tube and put it in a platinum bowl. He opened the trap-door of the workshop and placed the bowl outside, then returned, almost closing the trap-door. We both looked through the opening at the little bowl. Smiling, he said, “Now all you little electrons and protons go to sleep, and don’t wake up till Mummy tells you.” Turning to me, he added, “The patter, I may say, is for the audience, not for the rabbits in the conjurer’s hat.”</p>
<p>An expression of grave concentration came over his face. His breathing quickened. “Now!” he said. There was a terrific flash, and a report like a gun.</p>
<p>John wiped his forehead with a grubby pocket handkerchief, and remarked, “Alone I did it!” We returned to our coffee, and his plans.</p>
<p>“I’ve still got to find some really good way of bottling the energy till it’s wanted. You can’t be at one and the same time hypnotizing electrons and navigating a ship. I may simply have to use the energy to drive a dynamo and charge an accumulator. But there’s a more interesting possibility. I may be able, when I have hypnotized the little beggars, to give them a sort of ‘post-hypnotic suggestion,’ so that they can only wake up and barge about again in response to some particular stimulus. See?”</p>
<p>I laughed. We both sipped our coffee. I may as well say at once that the “post-hypnotic” system turned out ultimately to be feasible, and was adopted.</p>
<p>“Well, you can see,” he said, “there are great possibilities in this new dodge of mine. Now, while the yacht and plane are building, you are to come on the Continent with me. (I’m sure Bertha will be glad to have a holiday from you.) I want to do a bit of research. There’s an obviously supernormal mind in Paris, and one in Egypt, and perhaps others, not too far away. When I have the yacht and plane I’ll do the world tour in search of the rest. If I find a few suitable young things, I’ll voyage in the Pacific to find a satisfactory island for the Colony.”</p>
<p>During the next two months John was absorbed in the practical work of designing the yacht and the plane, perfecting the new power technique, and improving his flying.</p>
<p>At this time he was often to be seen “playing at boats” on the Park Lake or the more boisterous Estuary, like any ordinary boy. He was now over eighteen, but in appearance under fifteen. Thus his behaviour seemed quite normal. He produced a large number of models and fitted them out with electric motors or steam engines. These he dispatched across the lake in all weathers, observing their performance with great care. The design was largely determined by the necessity of stowing the plane on board, with wings folded, and by the need for extreme seaworthiness. John’s final choice was an extraordinary craft which local yachtsmen regarded as a mere caricature of a ship. John made a special three-foot model to this design, and fitted her out in great detail. In general shape she was ludicrously broad in the beam, and of shallow draft, in fact an exaggeration of the speed-boat hull; a sort of cross between a speed-boat and a life-boat, with a saucer somewhere in her ancestry, and perhaps a flat pebble of the “ducks and drakes” type. She was certainly a delightful toy; and I feel sure that John thoroughly enjoyed her simply as a toy, and had put much more work into her than was needed for mere experimentation. She represented a vessel the size of a small tug. No detail was omitted from her equipment. There were bunks for nine persons, but twenty could sleep on board at a pinch, and she could be handled comfortably by a single navigator. There was a realistic dining-saloon, with tables, chairs, cupboards. There was a latrine, glass portholes, minute navigation controls. These controls could be operated by some sort of radio device on shore. The engine was a fairly detailed replica of the sub-atomic engine that John intended for the actual ship.</p>
<p>Much entertainment was afforded by the antics which John made his model perform. On the Park Lake he would send her in leisurely pursuit of the terrified ducks. On the estuary, when the tide was in, he would stop her far out at sea, and persuade some kindly member of the sailing club to salvage her in dinghy. When the sweating oarsman had reached the little derelict, and was putting out his hand to seize her, John (on shore, and half a mile away) would set her going for a yard or two and watch the man’s repeated efforts to recover her. Finally he would let her out at full speed for the shore, and she would return to her master’s hand like a well-trained dog.</p>
<p>John had also been at work on several model planes. He used to spend much time flying them; but in secret, for he feared that, if they were seen performing their surprising antics, they would attract too much attention. He therefore used to retire with them into the wilds of North Wales, by means of his motor-bike or my car. There he would try out his models in the fickle mountain winds, their sub-atomic power enabling them to perform feats which no elastic-driven model could possibly achieve.</p>
<p>His final choice was a surprising mechanism, made on the same scale as the model yacht, and capable of being dismantled and stowed on board. With this stub-winged instrument he would amuse himself and me by the hour, making it rise from the surface of a “llyn” (it had both wheels and floats), and climb heavenwards, till we had to use a field-glass to follow it. It maintained its equilibrium automatically, but was steered by radio from the ground. When he had become adept in the management of this mechanical bird, he sometimes used it for a modern sort of hawking, sending the sparrow-like little object in chase of curlews, buzzards and ravens. This sport needed very delicate perception as well as control. As a rule the quarry would hurry away as soon as it realized it was being chased. The plane would then chevy it, or even swoop upon its back. But one old raven turned to fight, and before John could bring his toy’s superior speed into operation for escape, the raven’s horny neb had slashed one of the silken wings, and the plane came tumbling to the heather.</p>
<p>The plans for the yacht and the plane were finished before John reached the age of nineteen. I need not describe how I negotiated with shipbuilders and aeroplane manufacturers, and finally placed orders for the actual construction. I gained the reputation of being a mad millionaire; for the designs appeared to be quite unworkable, and I would not consider any of the objections raised against them. The main trouble was that in both plane and yacht the space allotted to the generation of power was by all ordinary standards quite insufficient. Contracts for the generators and machinery were distributed among several engineering firms in such a manner as to arouse as little curiosity as possible.</p>
