<h2>第三章</h2>
<p>“喂！”卡莫迪叫道。</p>
<p>“于是，又一次，”那个肤色黝黑的人说，“罪犯逃进了自己的末日。看着我，卡莫迪！我就是你的行刑官！你现在要为自己反人类的罪行，以及对你本人犯下的罪孽付出代价。不过我得补充一句：此次处决属于暂定性质，并不包含任何价值判断。”</p>
<p>行刑官从袖子里滑出一把刀。卡莫迪咽了口唾沫，总算找回声音。</p>
<p>“等等！”他喊道。“我不是来受刑的！”</p>
<p>“我知道，我知道。”行刑官安抚似的说道，一边沿着刀锋瞄准卡莫迪的颈静脉。“你还能说什么别的呢？”</p>
<p>“可这是真的！”卡莫迪尖叫道。“我是来领奖的！”</p>
<p>“领什么？”行刑官问。</p>
<p>“奖品，该死的，奖品！有人告诉我中奖了！去问使者，是他把我带来领奖的！”</p>
<p>行刑官打量了他一会儿，然后有些不好意思地把目光移开。他按下旁边控制板上的一个开关。箍住卡莫迪的钢带变成了纸彩带；行刑官的黑衣变成了白衣；刀子变成了一支自来水笔；脸颊上的刀疤则换成了一个肉瘤。</p>
<p>“好吧，”他说，毫无悔意。“我早就警告过他们，别把‘轻微犯罪处’跟‘抽奖办公室’合并，可他们偏不听。我要是真把你杀了，也算他们活该。那可就热闹了，对吧？”</p>
<p>“对我来说会挺惨的。”卡莫迪声音发抖地说。</p>
<p>“得了，没流出来的血就别哭了，”奖品办事员说。“如果我们把所有可能发生的情况都充分考虑进去，很快就会没有可能发生的情况可供我们充分考虑……我刚才说了什么？算了，词不对不要紧，句法结构是对的。你的奖品肯定就在这儿某个地方。”</p>
<p>他按下控制板上的一个按钮。立刻，一张硕大而凌乱的办公桌在离地两英尺处凭空出现，悬了一瞬，然后轰隆一声砸到地板上。办事员拉开抽屉，开始往外扔纸张、三明治、复写带、索引卡和铅笔头。</p>
<p>“嗯，肯定就在这里某处，”他说，语气里透出一丝绝望。他又按了控制板上的一个按钮。办公桌和控制板一起消失了。</p>
<p>“该死，我现在神经全乱了。”办事员说。他把手伸进空气里，摸到某样东西，用力一捏。显然那是个错误的按钮，因为伴随着一声惨叫，办事员本人也消失了。房间里只剩卡莫迪一个人。</p>
<p>他站在那里，嘴里没调子地哼着什么。过了一会儿，办事员重新出现，看起来并没有遭受什么严重损伤，除了额头上多了块淤青，脸上写满难堪。他胳膊底下夹着一个包装鲜艳的小盒子。</p>
<p>“请原谅刚才的中断，”他说。“眼下似乎什么事都不顺。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪试着开了个蹩脚的玩笑。“你们就是这么管理银河系的？”他问。</p>
<p>“那你以为我们该怎么管理？我们也不过是有意识的生物而已。”</p>
<p>“我知道，”卡莫迪说。“可我原以为这里是银河中心——”</p>
<p>“你们这些乡下人都一个样，”办事员疲惫地说道。“脑子里塞满了关于秩序与完美的不可能梦想，其实那不过是你们自身不完整性的理想化投射。你早该知道，生命本来就是件马马虎虎的事；力量更倾向于把东西拆散，而不是拼到一起；智能越高，能够察觉到的复杂性就越高。你也许听说过霍尔吉定理：所谓秩序，只不过是在宇宙混沌之中，对对象作出的一种原始而任意的关系分组；而当一个存在的智能与力量趋近最大值时，它的控制系数——定义为智能与力量的乘积，用符号 ing 表示——反而将趋近最小值。原因在于，需要理解和控制的对象数量以灾难性的几何级数增长，而‘掌控力’只以算术级数增长，前者最终必然远远超过后者。”</p>
<p>“我倒从来没这么想过。”卡莫迪还算客气地说。但他已经开始厌烦银河中心这些满嘴漂亮话的公务员了。他们凡事都有解释；可事实不过是，他们的工作干得并不好，却把自己的失误都归咎于宇宙条件。</p>
<p>“嗯，对，这也是真的，”办事员说。“你的观点——我擅自读了你的心——很有道理。和其他一切生物一样，我们也利用智能来替各种不一致找借口。不过事实是，事情永远刚刚好超出我们的掌握。还有一点也是真的：我们并没有把自己的掌握能力发挥到极限；有时我们机械地、粗心地、甚至错误地干活。重要的数据表会被放错地方，机器会失灵，整套行星系统也会被忘掉。但这只说明我们和所有具有某种程度自我决定能力的生物一样，会受到情绪支配。你还能要求什么？总得有人控制银河系，不然一切都会散架。银河是其居民的映照；在每个人、每样东西都能统治自己以前，某种外部控制仍然必不可少。要不是我们，谁来干这活？”</p>
<p>“不能造机器来做吗？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“机器！”办事员轻蔑地说。“我们有的是机器，有些复杂得精妙绝伦。可就算其中最好的，也不过像些白痴天才。造恒星、毁行星这种又乏味又直截了当的工作，它们干得还行；可你给它们一点真正困难的活，比如安慰一个寡妇，它们立刻就全垮掉。你能相信吗，我们部门最大的计算机能给整颗行星做景观设计，却不会煎鸡蛋，不会唱准一个调，在伦理学方面懂的还不如一只刚出生的小狼崽。你愿意让那样的东西管理你的人生吗？”</p>
<p>“当然不愿意，”卡莫迪说。“可是不能有人造出一台具有创造力和判断力的机器吗？”</p>
<p>“已经有人造出来了，”办事员说。“这种机器被设计成从经验中学习，这就意味着，它必须先犯错误，才能抵达真理。它有许多形状和尺寸，而且大多相当便携。它的缺陷显而易见，却似乎是抵消其优点所必需的配重。许多人试过，可至今没有谁能改进这种基本设计。这种巧妙装置叫作‘智能生命’。”</p>
<p>办事员露出了格言制造者那种自鸣得意的微笑。卡莫迪很想一拳砸在他那张得意洋洋的扁鼻子上。但他忍住了。</p>
<p>“如果你的训话已经结束，”卡莫迪说，“我想拿我的奖品。”</p>
<p>“悉听尊便，”办事员说。“只要你真的确定自己想要它。”</p>
<p>“我有什么理由不想要？”</p>
<p>“没有什么特别的理由，”办事员说。“只有一个普遍性的理由：任何新奇事物进入一个人的生活模式，都很容易造成扰动。”</p>
<p>“这个风险我自己承担，”卡莫迪说。“把奖品给我吧。”</p>
<p>“很好。”办事员说。他从后面一个很小的口袋里掏出一块很大的写字夹板，又摸出一支铅笔。“我们得先把这个填完。你的名字是卡—莫—迪，来自73C号行星，BB454C252系统，左象限，本地银河系统指涉 LK by CD；你是从大约二十亿名参赛者中随机抽中的。对吗？”</p>
<p>“你说是就是吧。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“让我看看，”办事员飞快扫过表格，“关于你自行承担风险、自负责任接受奖品的那部分，我可以跳过，对吧？”</p>
<p>“行，跳过。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“然后还有‘可食用等级’一栏，你与银河中心抽奖办公室之间的‘相互可谬性谅解条款’，关于‘不负责任伦理’的部分，当然还有‘终止决定因子余项’。不过这些全是标准条款，我想你都同意吧。”</p>
<p>“当然，为什么不同意？”卡莫迪说，觉得脑袋有点发飘。他非常想看看银河中心发的奖品到底是什么样，只盼办事员别再抠字眼。</p>
<p>“很好，”办事员说。“现在你只需在页面底部这个思维感应区域表示你接受这些条款，就完成了。”</p>
<p>卡莫迪不太清楚具体该怎么做，于是在心里想：是的，我接受奖品以及附带条件。页面底部随即变成了粉红色。</p>
<p>“谢谢，”办事员说。“合同本身就是协议的见证。恭喜你，卡莫迪，这是你的奖品。”</p>
<p>他把那个包装花哨的盒子递给卡莫迪。卡莫迪咕哝着道谢，兴冲冲开始拆包装。然而他还没拆多少，突然就发生了一场猛烈的打断：一个矮小、无毛、身穿闪亮衣服的男人冲进房间。</p>
<p>“哈！”他大叫。“克鲁滕斯在上，我抓你们现行了！你们真以为这事能蒙混过去？”</p>
<p>那人冲到卡莫迪面前，一把去抢奖品。卡莫迪伸直手臂，把盒子举到他够不着的地方。</p>
<p>“你想干什么？”他问。</p>
<p>“干什么？当然是来领取本该属于我的奖品！我就是卡莫迪！”</p>
<p>“不，你不是，”卡莫迪说。“我才是卡莫迪。”</p>
<p>小个子停下来，好奇地打量他。“你声称自己是卡莫迪？”</p>
<p>“我不是声称，我就是卡莫迪。”</p>
<p>“73C号行星的卡莫迪？”</p>
<p>“我不知道那是什么意思，”卡莫迪说。“我们把那个地方叫地球。”</p>
<p>矮个卡莫迪盯着他，愤怒的表情渐渐转为难以置信。</p>
<p>“地球？”他问。“我好像从没听说过。它是克尔泽里安联盟的成员吗？”</p>
<p>“据我所知不是。”</p>
<p>“那独立行星经营者协会呢？斯卡戈廷恒星合作社呢？银河行星居民联合会呢？都不是？你们行星参加过任何恒星系外组织吗？”</p>
<p>“我想没有。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“我就知道。”矮个卡莫迪说。他转向办事员。“看看他，你这个白痴！看看你把我的奖品发给了什么东西！瞧那双呆滞的猪眼，那副野蛮的下巴，还有那些角质化的指甲！”</p>
<p>“等等，”卡莫迪说。“没必要出言侮辱人。”</p>
<p>“我明白了，我明白了，”办事员说。“我先前根本没仔细看。我是说，谁会想到——”</p>
<p>“该死，”外星卡莫迪说道，“谁一眼都能看出，这东西根本不是第三十二级生命形态。事实上，他离第三十二级还差得远，甚至连银河级资格都没有！你这个彻头彻尾的蠢货，居然把我的奖品发给了一个无名之辈，一个文明圈外的东西！”</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 3</b></p>
<p>‘Hey!’ Carmody cried.</p>
<p>‘And so, once again,’ the dark individual said, ‘the criminal has escaped into his doom. Behold me, Carmody! I am your executioner! You pay now for your crimes against humanity as well as for your sins against yourself. But let me add that this execution is provisional, and implies no value judgement.’</p>
<p>The executioner slipped a knife from his sleeve. Carmody gulped and found his voice.</p>
<p>‘Hold it!’ he cried. ‘I’m not here to be executed!’</p>
<p>‘I know, I know,’ the executioner said soothingly, sighting along his knife blade on Carmody’s jugular vein. ‘What else could you say?’</p>
<p>‘But it’s true!’ Carmody shrieked. ‘I’m supposed to collect a Prize!’</p>
<p>‘A what?’ the executioner asked.</p>
<p>‘A Prize, damn you, a Prize! I was told I’d won a Prize! Ask the Messenger, he brought me here to collect a Prize!’</p>
<p>The executioner studied him, then looked away sheepishly. He pushed a switch on a nearby switchboard. The steel bands around Carmody turned into paper streamers. The executioner’s black garments changed to white. His knife turned into a fountain pen. The scar on his cheek was replaced by a wen.</p>
<p>‘All right,’ he said, with no hint of repentance. ‘I warned them not to combine the Department of Petty Crime with the Office of the Sweepstakes, but no, they wouldn’t listen to me. It would serve them right if I had killed you. Wouldn’t <i>that</i> have been a pretty mess, eh?’</p>
<p>‘It would have been messy for me,’ Carmody said shakily.</p>
<p>‘Well, no sense crying over unspilt blood,’ the Prize Clerk said. ‘If we took full account of our eventualities, we’d soon run out of eventualities to take full account of … What did I say? Never mind, the construction is right even if the words are wrong. I’ve got your prize here somewhere.’</p>
<p>He pressed a button on his switchboard. Immediately a large, messy desk materialized in the room two feet above the floor, hung for a moment, then dropped with a resounding thud. The Clerk pulled open the drawers and began to throw out papers, sandwiches, carbon ribbons, file cards and pencil stubs.</p>
<p>‘Well, it has to be here somewhere,’ he said, with a tone of faint desperation. He pushed another button on the switchboard. The desk and the switchboard vanished.</p>
<p>‘Damn it, I’m all nerves,’ the Clerk said. He reached into the air, found something and squeezed it. Apparently, it was the wrong button, for, with an agonizing scream, the Clerk himself vanished. Carmody was left alone in the room.</p>
<p>He stood, humming tunelessly under his breath. Then the Clerk reappeared, looking none the worse for his experience except for a bruise on his forehead and an expression of mortification on his face. He carried a small, brightly wrapped parcel under his arm.</p>
<p>‘Please excuse the interruption,’ he said. ‘Nothing seems to be going right just at present.’</p>
<p>Carmody essayed a feeble joke. ‘Is this any way to run a galaxy?’ he asked.</p>
<p>‘Well, how did you, expect us to run it? We’re only sentient, you know.’</p>
<p>‘I know,’ Carmody said. ‘But I had expected that here, at Galactic Centre –’</p>
<p>‘You provincials are all alike,’ the Clerk said wearily. ‘Filled with impossible dreams of order and perfection, which are mere idealized projections of your own incompletion. You should know by now that life is a sloppy affair, that power tends to break things up rather than put things together, and that the greater the intelligence, the higher the degree of complication which it detects. You may have heard Holgee’s Theorem; that Order is merely a primitive and arbitrary relational grouping of objects in the chaos of the Universe, and that, if a being’s intelligence and power approached maximum, his coefficient of control (considered as the product of intelligence and power, and expressed by the symbol <i>ing</i>) would approach minimum – due to the disastrous geometric progression of objects to be comprehended and controlled outstripping the arithmetic progression of Grasp.’</p>
<p>‘I never thought of it that way,’ Carmody said politely enough. But he was beginning to grow annoyed at the glib civil servants of Galactic Centre. They had an answer for everything; but the fact was, they simply didn’t do their jobs very well, and they blamed their failures on cosmic conditions.</p>
<p>‘Well, yes, that’s also true,’ the Clerk said. ‘Your point (I took the liberty of reading your mind) is well made. Like all other organisms, we use intelligence to explain away disparity. But the fact is, things are forever just a little beyond our grasp. It is also true that we do not extend our grasp to the utmost; sometimes we do our work mechanically, carelessly, even erroneously. Important data sheets are misplaced, machines malfunction, whole planetary systems are forgotten. But this merely points out that we are subject to emotionality, like all other creatures with any measure of self-determination. What would you have? <i>Somebody</i> has to control the galaxy; otherwise everything would fly apart. Galaxies are reflections of their inhabitants; until everyone and everything can rule himself and itself, some outer control is necessary. Who would do the job if we didn’t?’</p>
<p>‘Couldn’t we build machines to do the work?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘Machines!’ the Clerk said scornfully. ‘We have many of them, some exquisitely complex. But even the best of them are much like idiot savants. They do adequately on tedious straightforward tasks like building stars or destroying planets. But give them something tough, like solacing a widow, and they simply go to pieces. Would you believe it, the largest computer in our section can landscape an entire planet; but it cannot fry an egg or carry a tune, and it knows less about ethics than a newborn wolf cub. Would you want something like <i>that</i> to run your life?’</p>
<p>‘Of course not,’ Carmody said. ‘But couldn’t someone build a machine with creativity and judgement?’</p>
<p>‘Someone has,’ the Clerk said. ‘It has been designed to learn from experience, which means that it must make errors in order to arrive at truths. It comes in many shapes and sizes, most of them quite portable. Its flaws are readily apparent, but seem to exist as necessary counterweights to its virtues. No one has yet improved on the basic design, though many have tried. This ingenious device is called “intelligent life.”’</p>
<p>The Clerk smiled the self-satisfied smile of the aphorism-maker. Carmody felt like hitting him square on his smug pug nose. But he restrained himself.</p>
<p>‘If you are quite through lecturing,’ Carmody said, ‘I would like my Prize.’</p>
<p>‘Just as you wish,’ the Clerk said. ‘If you are quite sure that you want it.’</p>
<p>‘Is there any reason why I shouldn’t want it?’</p>
<p>‘No particular reason,’ the Clerk said. ‘Just a general one; the introduction of any novel object into one’s life pattern is apt to be disrupting.’</p>
<p>‘I’ll take my chances on that,’ Carmody said. ‘Let’s have the Prize.’</p>
<p>‘Very well,’ the Clerk said. He took a large clipboard out of a small rear pocket and produced a pencil. ‘We must fill this in first. Your name is Car-Mo-Dee, you’re of Planet 73C, System BB454C252, Left Quadrant, Local Galactic System referent LK by CD, and you were picked at random from approximately two billion contestants. Correct?’</p>
<p>‘If you say so,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Let me see now,’ the Clerk said, scanning the page rapidly, ‘I can skip the stuff about you accepting the Prize on your own risk and recognizance, can’t I?’</p>
<p>‘Sure, skip it,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘And then there’s the section on Edibility Rating, and the part on Reciprocal Fallibility Understandings between you and the Sweepstakes Office of the Galactic Centre, and the part about Irresponsible Ethics, and, of course, the Termination Determinant Residue. But all of that is quite standard, and I suppose you adhere to it.’</p>
<p>‘Sure, why not?’ Carmody said, feeling lightheaded. He was very eager to see what a Prize from Galactic Centre would look like, and he wished that the Clerk would stop quibbling.</p>
<p>‘Very well,’ the Clerk said. ‘Now simply signify your acceptance of the terms to this mind-sensitive area at the bottom of the page, and that’ll be it.’</p>
<p>Not quite knowing what to do, Carmody thought, <i>Yes, I accept the Prize and the conditions attached to it.</i> The bottom of the page grew pink.</p>
<p>‘Thank you,’ the Clerk said. ‘The contract itself is witness to the agreement. Congratulations, Carmody, and here is your Prize.’</p>
<p>He handed the gaily wrapped box to Carmody, who muttered his thanks and began eagerly to unwrap it. He didn’t get far, though; there was a sudden, violent interruption. A short, hairless man in glittering clothes burst into the room.</p>
<p>‘Hah!’ he cried. ‘I’ve caught you in the act, by Klootens! Did you really think you could get away with it?’</p>
<p>The man rushed up to him and grabbed at the Prize. Carmody held it out of arm’s reach.</p>
<p>‘What do you think you’re doing?’ he asked.</p>
<p>‘Doing? I’m here to claim my rightful Prize, what else? I am Carmody!’</p>
<p>‘No, you aren’t,’ Carmody said. ‘I am Carmody.’</p>
<p>The little man paused and looked at him with curiosity. ‘You claim to be Carmody?’</p>
<p>‘I don’t claim, I <i>am</i> Carmody.’</p>
<p>‘Carmody of Planet 73C?’</p>
<p>‘I don’t know what that means,’ Carmody said. ‘We call the place Earth.’</p>
<p>The shorter Carmody stared at him, his expression of rage changing to one of disbelief.</p>
<p>‘Earth?’ he asked. ‘I don’t believe I’ve heard of it. Is it a member of the Chlzerian League?’</p>
<p>‘Not to the best of my knowledge.’</p>
<p>‘What about the Independent Planetary Operators Association? Or the Scagotine Stellar Co-operative? Or the Amalgamated Planet-Dwellers of the Galaxy? No? Is your planet a member of <i>any</i> extrastellar organization?’</p>
<p>‘I guess it isn’t,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I suspected as much,’ the short Carmody said. He turned to the Clerk. ‘Look at him, you idiot! Look at the creature to whom you have awarded my Prize! Observe the dull piggish eyes, the brutish jaw, the horny fingernails!’</p>
<p>‘Now just a minute,’ Carmody said. ‘There’s no reason to be insulting.’</p>
<p>‘I see, I see,’ the Clerk replied. ‘I never really looked before. I mean, one hardly expects –’</p>
<p>‘Why, damn it,’ the alien Carmody said, ‘<i>anyone</i> could tell at once that this creature is not a Class 32 Life-Form. As a matter of fact, he’s not even <i>close</i> to Class 32, he hasn’t even attained Galactic status! You utter imbecile, you have awarded my Prize to a nonentity, a creature from beyond the pale!’</p>
