<h2>15</h2>
<h2><em>A Portrait is Coloured</em></h2>
<p>Baley took it between the eyes and tried not to show it.</p>
<p>Presumably, living as they did, Solarians considered one another’s private lives to be sacrosanct. Questions concerning marriage and children were in bad taste. He supposed then that chronic quarrelling could exist between husband and wife and be a matter into which curiosity was equally forbidden.</p>
<p>But even when murder had been committed? Would no one commit the social crime of asking the suspect if she quarrelled with her husband? Or of mentioning the matter if they happened to know of it?</p>
<p>Well, Leebig had.</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘What did the quarrels concern?’</p>
<p>‘You had better ask her, I think.’</p>
<p>He better had, thought Baley. He rose stiffly. ‘Thank you, Dr Leebig, for your co-operation. I may need your help again later. I hope you will keep yourself available.’</p>
<p>‘Done viewing,’ said Leebig, and he and the segment of his room vanished abruptly.</p>
<p>For the first time Baley found himself not minding a plane flight through open space. Not minding it at all. It was almost as though he were in his own element.</p>
<p>He wasn’t even thinking of Earth or of Jessie. He had been away from Earth only a matter of weeks, yet it might as well have been years. He had been on Solaria only the better part of three days and yet it seemed for ever.</p>
<p>How fast could a man adapt to nightmare?</p>
<p>Or was it Gladia? He would be seeing her soon, not viewing her. Was that what gave him confidence and this odd feeling of mixed apprehension and anticipation?</p>
<p>Would she endure it? he wondered. Or would she slip away after a few moments of seeing, begging off as Quemot had done?</p>
<p>She stood at the other end of a long room when he entered. She might almost have been an impressionistic representation of herself, she was reduced so to essentials.</p>
<p>Her lips were faintly red, her eyebrows lightly pencilled, her ear-lobes faintly blue, and, except for that, her face was untouched. She looked pale, a little frightened, and very young.</p>
<p>Her brown-blonde hair was drawn back, and her grey-blue eyes were somehow shy. Her dress was a blue so dark as to be almost black, with a thin white edging curling down each side. She wore long sleeves, white gloves, and flat-heeled shoes. Not an inch of skin showed anywhere but in her face. Even her neck was covered by a kind of unobtrusive ruching.</p>
<p>Baley stopped where he was. ‘Is this close enough, Gladia?’</p>
<p>She was breathing with shallow quickness. She said, ‘I had forgotten what to expect really. It’s just like viewing, isn’t it? I mean, if you don’t think of it as seeing.’</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘It’s all quite normal to me.’</p>
<p>‘Yes, on Earth.’ She closed her eyes. ‘Sometimes I try to imagine it. Just crowds of people everywhere. You walk down a road, and there are others waiting with you and still others walking in the other direction. Dozens—’</p>
<p>‘Hundreds,’ said Baley. ‘Did you ever view scenes on Earth in a book-film? Or view a novel with an Earth setting?’</p>
<p>‘We don’t have many of those, but I’ve viewed novels set on the other Outer Worlds where seeing goes on all the time. It’s different in a novel. It just seems like a multiview.’</p>
<p>‘Do people ever kiss in novels?’</p>
<p>She flushed painfully. ‘I don’t read that kind.’</p>
<p>‘Never?’</p>
<p>‘Well – there are always a few dirty films around, you know, and sometimes, just out of curiosity – It’s sickening, really.’</p>
<p>‘Is it?’</p>
<p>She said with sudden animation, ‘But Earth is so different. So many people. When you walk, Elijah, I suppose you even t-touch people. I mean, by accident.’</p>
<p>Baley half smiled. ‘You even knock them down by accident.’ He thought of the crowds on the Expressways, tugging and shoving, bounding up and down the strips, and for a moment, inevitably, he felt a pang of homesickness.</p>
<p>Gladia said, ‘You don’t have to stay way out there.’</p>
<p>‘Would it be all right if I came closer?’</p>
<p>‘I think so. I’ll tell you when I’d rather you wouldn’t any more.’</p>
<p>Stepwise Baley drew closer, while Gladia watched him, wide-eyed.</p>
<p>She said suddenly, ‘Would you like to see some of my field colourings?’</p>
<p>Baley was six feet away. He stopped and looked at her. She seemed small and fragile. He tried to visualise her, something in her hand (what?), swing furiously at the skull of her husband. He tried to picture her, mad with rage, homicidal with hate and anger.</p>
<p>He had to admit it could be done. Even a hundred and five pounds of woman could crush a skull if she had the proper weapon and were wild enough. And Baley had known murderesses on Earth, who, in repose, were bunny rabbits.</p>
<p>He said, ‘What are field colourings, Gladia?’</p>
<p>‘An art form,’ she said.</p>
<p>Baley remembered Leebig’s reference to Gladia’s art. He nodded. ‘I’d like to see some.’</p>
<p>‘Follow me, then.’</p>
<p>Baley maintained a careful six-foot distance between them. At that, it was less than a third the distance Klorissa had demanded.</p>
<p>They entered a room that burst with light. It glowed in every corner and every colour.</p>
<p>Gladia looked pleased, proprietary. She looked up at Baley, eyes anticipating.</p>
<p>Baley’s response must have been what she expected, though he said nothing. He turned slowly, trying to make out what he saw, for it was light only, no material object at all.</p>
<p>The gobbets of light sat on embracing pedestals. They were living geometry, lines and curves of colour, entwined into a coalescing whole yet maintaining distinct identities. No two specimens were even remotely alike.</p>
<p>Baley groped for appropriate words and said, ‘Is it supposed to mean anything?’</p>
<p>Gladia laughed in her pleasant contralto. ‘It means whatever you like it to mean. They’re just light-forms that might make you feel angry or happy or curious or whatever <em>I</em> felt when I constructed one. I could make one for you, a kind of portrait. It might not be very good, though, because I would just be improvising quickly.’</p>
<p>‘Would you? I would be very interested.’</p>
<p>‘All right,’ she said, and half-ran to a light-figure in one corner, passing within inches of him as she did so. She did not seem to notice.</p>
<p>She touched something on the pedestal of the light-figure and the glory above died without a flicker.</p>
<p>Baley gasped and said, ‘Don’t do that.’</p>
<p>‘It’s all right. I was tired of it, anyway. I’ll just fade the others temporarily so they don’t distract me.’ She opened a panel along one featureless wall and moved a rheostat. The colours faded to something scarcely visible.</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘Don’t you have a robot to do this! Closing contacts?’</p>
<p>‘Shush, now,’ she said impatiently. ‘I don’t keep robots in here. This is <em>me</em>.’ She looked at him, frowning. ‘I don’t know you well enough. That’s the trouble.’</p>
<p>She wasn’t looking at the pedestal, but her fingers rested lightly on its smooth upper surface. All ten fingers were curved, tensed, waiting.</p>
<p>One finger moved, describing a half curve over smoothness. A bar of deep yellow light grew and slanted obliquely across the air above. The finger inched backwards a fraction and the light grew slightly less deep in shade.</p>
<p>She looked at it momentarily. ‘I suppose that’s it. A kind of strength without weight.’</p>
<p>‘Jehoshaphat!’ said Baley.</p>
<p>‘Are you offended?’ Her fingers lifted and the yellow slant of light remained solitary and stationary.</p>
<p>‘No, not at all. But what is it? How do you do it?’</p>
<p>‘That’s hard to explain,’ said Gladia, looking at the pedestal thoughtfully, ‘considering I don’t really understand it myself. It’s a kind of optical illusion, I’ve been told. We set up force-fields at different energy levels. They’re extrusions of hyper-space, really, and don’t have the properties of ordinary space at all. Depending on the energy level, the human eye sees light of different shades. The shapes and colours are controlled by the warmth of my fingers against appropriate spots on the pedestal. There are all sorts of controls inside each pedestal.’</p>
<p>‘You mean if I were to put my finger there—’ Baley advanced and Gladia made way for him. He put a hesitant forefinger down upon the pedestal and felt a soft throbbing.</p>
<p>‘Go ahead. Move your finger, Elijah,’ said Gladia.</p>
<p>Baley did so and a dirty-grey jag of light lifted upwards skewing the yellow light. Baley withdrew his finger sharply and Gladia laughed and then was instantly contrite.</p>
<p>‘I shouldn’t laugh,’ she said. ‘It’s really very hard to do, even for people who’ve tried a long time.’ Her own hand moved lightly and too quickly for Baley to follow and the monstrosity he had set up disappeared, leaving the yellow light in isolation again.</p>
<p>‘How did you learn to do this?’ asked Baley.</p>
<p>‘I just kept on trying. It’s a new art form, you know, and only one or two really know how—’</p>
<p>‘And you’re the best,’ said Baley sombrely. ‘On Solaria everyone is either the only or the best or both.’</p>
<p>‘You needn’t laugh. I’ve had some of my pedestals on display. I’ve given shows.’ Her chin lifted. There was no mistaking her pride.</p>
<p>She continued, ‘Let me go on with your portrait.’ Her fingers moved again.</p>
<p>There were few curves in the light-form that grew under her ministrations. It was all sharp angles. And the dominant colour was blue.</p>
<p>‘That’s Earth, somehow,’ said Gladia, biting her lower lip. ‘I always think of Earth as blue. All those people and seeing, seeing, seeing. Viewing is more rose. How does it seem to you?’</p>
<p>‘Jehoshaphat, I can’t picture things as colours!’</p>
<p>‘Can’t you?’ she asked abstractedly. ‘Now you say “Jehoshaphat” sometimes and that’s just a little blob of violet. A little sharp blob because it usually comes out ping, like that.’ And the little blob was there, glowing just off centre.</p>
<p>‘And then,’ she said, ‘I can finish it like this.’ And a flat, lustreless hollow cube of slate grey sprang up to enclose everything. The light within shone through it, but dimmer; imprisoned, somehow.</p>
<p>Baley felt a sadness at it, as though it were something enclosing him, keeping him from something he wanted. He said, ‘What’s that last?’</p>
<p>Gladia said, ‘Why, the walls about you. That’s what’s most in you, the way you can’t go outside, the way you have to be inside. You <em>are</em> inside there. Don’t you see?’</p>
<p>Baley saw and somehow he disapproved. He said. ‘Those walls aren’t permanent. I’ve been out today.’</p>
<p>‘You have? Did you mind?’</p>
<p>He could not resist a counterdig. ‘The way you mind seeing me. You don’t like it but you can stand it.’</p>
<p>She looked at him thoughtfully. ‘Do you want to come out now? With me? For a walk?’</p>
<p>It was Baley’s impulse to say: Jehoshaphat, no!</p>
<p>She said, ‘I’ve never walked with anyone, seeing. It’s still day-time, and it’s pleasant weather.’</p>
<p>Baley looked at his abstractionist portrait and said, ‘If I go, will you take away the grey?’</p>
<p>She smiled and said, ‘I’ll see how you behave.’</p>
<p>The structure of light remained as they left the room. It stayed behind, holding Baley’s imprisoned soul fast in the grey of the Cities.</p>
<p>Baley shivered slightly. Air moved against him and there was a chill to it.</p>
<p>Gladia said, ‘Are you cold?’</p>
<p>‘It wasn’t like this before,’ muttered Baley.</p>
<p>‘It’s late in the day now, but it isn’t really cold. Would you like a coat? One of the robots could bring one in a minute.’</p>
<p>‘No. It’s all right.’ They stepped forward along a narrow paved path. He said, ‘Is this where you used to walk with Dr Leebig?’</p>
<p>‘Oh, no. We walked way out among the fields, where you only see an occasional robot working and you can hear the animal sounds. You and I will stay near the house though, just in case.’</p>
<p>‘In case what?’</p>
<p>‘Well, in case you want to go in.’</p>
<p>‘Or in case you get weary of seeing?’</p>
<p>‘It doesn’t bother me,’ she said recklessly.</p>
<p>There was a vague rustle of leaves above and an all-pervading yellowness and greenness. There were sharp, thin cries in the air about, plus a strident humming, and shadows, too.</p>
<p>He was especially aware of the shadows. One of them stuck out before him, in shape like a man, that moved as he did in horrible mimicry. Baley had heard of shadows, of course, and he knew what they were, but in the pervasive indirect lighting of the Cities he had never been specifically aware of one.</p>
<p>Behind him, he knew, was the Solarian sun. He took care not to look at it, but he knew it was there.</p>
<p>Space was large, space was lonely, yet he found it drawing him. His mind pictured himself striding the surface of a world with thousands of miles and light-years of room all about him.</p>
<p>Why should he find attraction in this thought of loneliness? He didn’t want loneliness. He wanted Earth and the warmth and companionship of the man-crammed Cities.</p>
<p>The picture failed him. He tried to conjure up New York in his mind, all the noise and fullness of it, and found he could remain conscious only of the quiet, air-moving chill of the surface of Solaria.</p>
<p>Without quite willing it Baley moved closer to Gladia until he was two feet away, then grew aware of her startled face.</p>
<p>‘I beg your pardon,’ he said at once, and drew off.</p>
<p>She gasped, ‘It’s all right. Won’t you walk this way? We have some flower-beds you might like.’</p>
<p>The direction she indicated lay away from the sun. Baley followed silently.</p>
<p>Gladia said, ‘Later in the year, it will be wonderful. In the warm weather I can run down to the lake and swim, or just run across the fields, run as fast as I can until I’m just glad to fall down and lie still.’</p>
<p>She looked down at herself. ‘But this is no costume for it. With all this on, I’ve <em>got</em> to walk. Sedately, you know.’</p>
<p>‘How would you prefer to dress?’ asked Baley.</p>
<p>‘Halter and shorts at the <em>most</em>,’ she cried, lifting her arms as though feeling the freedom of that in her imagination. ‘Sometimes less. Sometimes just sandals so you can feel the air with every inch – Oh, I’m sorry. I’ve offended you.’</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘No. It’s all right. Was that your costume when you went walking with Dr Leebig?’</p>
<p>‘It varied. It depended on the weather. Sometimes I wore very little, but it was viewing, you know. You <em>do</em> understand, I hope.’</p>
<p>‘I understand. What about Dr Leebig, though? Did he dress lightly too?’</p>
<p>‘Jothan dress lightly?’ Gladia smiled flashingly. ‘Oh, no. He’s very solemn, always.’ She twisted her face into a thin look of gravity and half winked, catching the very essence of Leebig and forcing a short grunt of appreciation out of Baley.</p>
<p>‘This is the way he talks,’ she said. ‘“My dear Gladia, in considering the effect of a first-order potential on positron flow—”’</p>
<p>‘Is that what he talked to you about? Robotics?’</p>
<p>‘Mostly. Oh, he takes it so seriously, you know. He was always trying to teach me about it. He never gave up.’</p>
<p>‘Did you learn anything?’</p>
<p>‘Not one thing. Nothing. It’s just all a complete mix-up to me. He’d get angry with me sometimes, but when he’d scold, I’d dive into the water, if we were anywhere near the lake, and splash him.’</p>
<p>‘<em>Splash</em> him? I thought you were viewing.’</p>
<p>She laughed. ‘You’re <em>such</em> an Earthman. I’d splash where he was standing in his own room or on his own estate. The water couldn’t touch him, but he would duck just the same. Look at that.’</p>
<p>Baley looked. They had circled a wooded patch and now came upon a clearing, centred about an ornamental pond. Small bricked walks penetrated the clearing and broke it up. Flowers grew in profusion and order. Baley knew them for flowers from book-films he had viewed.</p>
<p>In a way the flowers were like the light-patterns that Gladia constructed and Baley imagined that she constructed them in the spirit of flowers. He touched one cautiously, then looked about. Reds and yellow predominated.</p>
<p>In turning to look about Baley caught a glimpse of the sun.</p>
<p>He said uneasily, ‘The sun is low in the sky.’</p>
<p>‘It’s late afternoon,’ called Gladia back at him. She had run towards the pond and was sitting on a stone bench at its edge. ‘Come here!’ she called, waving. ‘You can stand if you don’t like to sit on stone.’</p>
<p>Baley advanced slowly. ‘Does it get this low every day?’ and at once he was sorry he asked. If the planet rotated, the sun must be low in the sky both mornings and afternoons. Only at midday could it be high.</p>
<p>Telling himself this couldn’t change a lifetime of pictured thought. He knew there was such a thing as night and had even experienced it, with a planet’s whole thickness interposing safely between a man and the sun. He knew there were clouds and a protective greyness hiding the worst of outdoors. And still, when he thought of planetary surfaces, it was always a picture of a blaze of light with a sun high in the sky.</p>
<p>He looked over his shoulder, just quickly enough to get a flash of sun, and wondered how far the house was if he should decide to return.</p>
<p>Gladia was pointing to the other end of the stone bench.</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘That’s pretty close to you, isn’t it?’</p>
<p>She spread out her little hands, palms up. ‘I’m getting used to it. Really.’</p>
<p>He sat down, facing towards her to avoid the sun.</p>
<p>She leaned over backwards towards the water and picked a small cup-shaped flower, yellow without and white-streaked within, not at all flamboyant. She said, ‘This is a native plant. Most of the flowers here are from Earth originally.’</p>
<p>Water dripped from its severed stem as she extended it gingerly towards Baley.</p>
<p>Baley reached for it as gingerly. ‘You killed it,’ he said.</p>
<p>‘It’s only a flower. There are thousands more.’ Suddenly, before his fingers more than touched the yellow cup, she snatched it away, her eyes kindling. ‘Or are you trying to imply I could kill a human being because I picked a flower?’</p>
<p>Baley said in soft conciliation. ‘I wasn’t implying anything. May I see it?’</p>
<p>Baley didn’t really want to touch it. It had grown in wet soil and there was still the effluvium of mud about it. How could these people, who were so careful in contact with Earthmen and even with one another, be so careless in their contact with ordinary dirt?</p>
<p>But he held the stalk between thumb and forefinger and looked at it. The cup was formed of several thin pieces of papery tissue, curving up from a common centre. Within it was a white convex swelling, damp with liquid and fringed with dark hairs that trembled lightly in the wind.</p>
<p>She said, ‘Can you smell it?’</p>
<p>At once Baley was aware of the odour that emanated from it. He leaned towards it and said, ‘It smells like a woman’s perfume.’</p>
<p>Gladia clapped her hands in delight. ‘How like an Earthman. What you really mean is that a woman’s perfume smells like <em>that.</em>’</p>
<p>Baley nodded ruefully. He was growing weary of the outdoors. The shadows were growing longer and the land was becoming sombre. Yet he was determined not to give in. He wanted those grey walls of light that dimmed his portrait removed. It was quixotic, but there it was.</p>
<p>Gladia took the flower from Baley, who let it go without reluctance. Slowly she pulled its petals apart. She said, ‘I suppose every woman smells different.’</p>
<p>‘It depends on the perfume,’ said Baley indifferently.</p>
<p>‘Imagine being close enough to tell. I don’t wear perfume because no one is close enough. Except now. But I suppose you smell perfume often, all the time. On Earth, your wife is always with you, isn’t she?’ She was concentrating very hard on the flower, frowning as she plucked it carefully to pieces.</p>
<p>‘She’s not always with me,’ said Baley. ‘Not every minute.’</p>
<p>‘But most of the time. And whenever you want to—’</p>
<p>Baley said suddenly, ‘Why did Dr Leebig try so hard to teach you robotics, do you suppose?’</p>
<p>The dismembered flower consisted now of a stalk and the inner swelling. Gladia twirled it between her fingers, then tossed it away, so that it floated for a moment on the surface of the pond. ‘I think he wanted me to be his assistant,’ she said.</p>
<p>‘Did he tell you so, Gladia?’</p>
<p>‘Towards the end, Elijah. I think he grew impatient. Anyway, he asked me if I didn’t think it would be exciting to work in robotics. Naturally, I told him I could think of nothing duller. He was quite angry.’</p>
<p>‘And he never walked with you again after that.’</p>
<p>She said, ‘You know, I think that may have been it. I suppose his feelings were hurt. Really, though, what could I do?’</p>
<p>‘It was before that, though, that you told him about your quarrels with Dr Delmarre.’</p>
<p>Her hands became fists and held so in a tight spasm. Her body held stiffly to its position, head bent and a little to one side. Her voice was unnaturally high. ‘What quarrels?’</p>
<p>‘Your quarrels with your husband. I understand you hated him.’</p>
<p>Her face was distorted and blotched as she glared at him. ‘Who told you that? Jothan?’</p>
<p>‘Dr Leebig mentioned it. I think it’s true.’</p>
<p>She was shaken. ‘You’re still trying to prove I killed him. I keep thinking you’re my friend and you’re only – only a detective.’ She raised her fists and Baley waited.</p>
<p>He said, ‘You know you can’t touch me.’</p>
<p>Her hands dropped and she began crying without a sound. She turned her head away.</p>
<p>Baley bent his own head and closed his eyes, shutting out the disturbing long shadows. He said, ‘Dr Delmarre was not a very affectionate man, was he?’</p>
<p>She said in a strangled way, ‘He was a very busy man.’</p>
<p>Baley said, ‘You <em>are</em> affectionate, on the other hand. You find a man interesting. Do you understand?’</p>
<p>‘I c-can’t help it. I know it’s disgusting, but I can’t help it. It’s even disgusting t-to talk about it.’</p>
<p>‘You did talk about it to Dr Leebig, though?’</p>
<p>‘I <em>had</em> to do something and Jothan was handy and he didn’t seem to mind and it made me feel better.’</p>
<p>‘Was this the reason you quarrelled with your husband? Was it that he was cold and unaffectionate and you resented it?’</p>
<p>‘Sometimes I hated him.’ She shrugged her shoulders helplessly. ‘He was just a good Solarian and we weren’t scheduled for ch – for ch—’ She broke down.</p>
<p>Baley waited. His own stomach was cold and open air pressed down heavily upon him. When Gladia’s sobs grew quieter, he asked, as gently as he could, ‘Did you kill him, Gladia?’</p>
<p>‘N-no.’ Then, suddenly, as though all resistance had corroded within her: ‘I haven’t told you everything.’</p>
<p>‘Well, then, please do so now.’</p>
<p>‘We were quarrelling that time, the time he died. The old quarrel. I screamed at him but he never shouted back. He hardly ever even said anything and that just made it worse. I was so angry, so angry. I don’t remember after that.’</p>
<p>‘Jehoshaphat!’ Baley swayed slightly and his eyes sought the neutral stone of the bench. ‘What do you mean you don’t remember?’</p>
<p>‘I mean he was dead and I was screaming and the robots came—’</p>
<p>‘Did you kill him?’</p>
<p>‘I don’t remember it, Elijah, and I would remember it if I had, wouldn’t I? Only I don’t remember anything else, either, and I’ve been so frightened, so frightened. Help me, please, Elijah.’</p>
<p>‘Don’t worry, Gladia. I’ll help you.’ Baley’s reeling mind fastened on the murder weapon. What happened to it? It must have been removed. If so, only the murderer could have done it. Since Gladia was found immediately after the murder on the scene, she could not have done it. The murderer would have to be someone else. No matter how it looked to everyone on Solaria, it had to be someone else.</p>
<p>Baley thought sickly: I’ve got to get back to the house.</p>
<p>He said, ‘Gladia—’</p>
<p>Somehow he was staring at the sun. It was nearly at the horizon. He had to turn his head to look at it and his eyes locked with a morbid fascination. He had never seen it so. Fat, red, and dim somehow, so that one could look at it without blinding, and see the bleeding clouds above it in thin lines, with one crossing it in a bar of black.</p>
<p>Baley mumbled, ‘The sun is so red.’</p>
<p>He heard Gladia’s choked voice say drearily, ‘It’s always red at sunset, red and dying.’</p>
<p>Baley had a vision. The sun was moving down to the horizon because the planet’s surface was moving away from it, a thousand miles an hour, spinning under that naked sun, spinning with nothing to guard the microbes called men that scurried over its spinning surface, spinning madly for ever, spinning – spinning …</p>
<p>It was his head that was spinning and the stone bench that was slanting beneath him and the sky heaving, blue, dark blue, and the sun was gone, with the tops of trees and the ground rushing up and Gladia screaming thinly and another sound …</p>
