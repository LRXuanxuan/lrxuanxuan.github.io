<h3>第十一章　奇异的遭遇</h3>
<p>关于智人困境的那个严峻判断，似乎恰好出现在约翰自身的发展已经成熟到足以引发一场深刻精神危机的时候。就在我刚才叙述的那件事过去几周之后，他比以往任何时候都更深地退回自己内心，甚至躲避那些一向把自己视作他朋友的人。他过去对周围这些奇怪生物的活泼兴趣，仿佛突然蒸发了。他的谈话变得敷衍，只有极少数时候会突然爆发出带敌意的傲慢。有时他似乎非常渴望亲密接触，却又完全无法真正抵达它。他会劝我同他去山里待一天，或晚上一起去剧院；可在勇敢地试图恢复我们从前的相处方式之后，他很快又会痛苦地沉默下来，对我努力维持的谈话几乎充耳不闻。或者，他会在母亲身后一声不响地跟上一阵，却找不到一句能对她说的话。帕克斯被他的状态吓坏了，甚至担心“他的脑子是不是要坏掉了”，因为他有时会陷入一种空洞、悲惨而无言的状态。她后来告诉我，有天夜里，她听见约翰房间里传来声音，便悄悄进去看看出了什么事。他“哭得像个怎么也无法从噩梦里醒来的孩子”。她抚摸他的头，求他把事情告诉她。约翰仍旧抽泣着，只说：“噢，帕克斯，我太孤独了。”</p>
<p>这种痛苦的状态持续了好几个星期以后，约翰从家里消失了。他父母早已习惯他偶尔离开几天，可这一次他们收到一张盖着苏格兰邮戳的明信片，上面说他要去山里度假，“很久很久以后”才会回来。</p>
<p>一个月后，我们开始真正担心起他来。我有个熟人叫泰德·布林斯顿，知道约翰失踪的事。他告诉我，他有个朋友麦克惠斯特，是个攀岩者，最近在苏格兰北部的山区碰见过“一个像野孩子似的家伙”。他说可以介绍我去见麦克惠斯特。</p>
<p>拖了一阵之后，布林斯顿请我吃饭，让我同麦克惠斯特以及他的攀岩伙伴诺顿见面。真正到了那天，我却意外而不安地发现，这两个人似乎都不愿坦率谈论那件把我们四个人聚到一起的事。不过最后，也许是酒精起了作用，也许是我的焦虑逼得太紧，他们终于开了口。他们那段时间一直在罗斯和克罗马蒂一带探索一些少有人去的峭壁，每次就在方便取水的小溪或湖边扎帐篷住几天。一个炎热的日子里，他们正沿着一座山的草坡脊线往上爬——那座山的名字他们拒绝透露——忽然听见一种奇怪的声音，似乎来自右侧峡谷的尽头。那声音半像动物，半像人，怪得让他们非去看看不可。不久，他们下到溪边，看见一个一丝不挂的男孩坐在一处小瀑布旁边，正在吟唱，或者说嚎叫。“那声音让我后背发凉。”麦克惠斯特说。男孩看见他们，立刻逃走，消失在白桦林里。他们找了一阵，却没能再找到他。</p>
<p>几天以后，两人在一家小酒馆里讲起这件事。一个红胡子的当地人——喝得绝不算少——立刻倒出一大堆关于这种男孩的故事；当然，前提是那真是个男孩，而不是什么水妖。此人的嫂子的外甥声称，自己曾经真的追过那东西，还亲眼看见他化成一缕雾气。另一个人说，自己绕过一块岩石时同他迎面撞上，那怪物的眼睛足有炮弹那么大，黑得跟地狱一样。</p>
<p>那周晚些时候，两位攀岩者又遇见了这个野孩子。他们正在爬一道相当困难的岩沟，已经到了一个看起来无法继续直接上攀的位置。领攀的麦克惠斯特刚把第二个人带上来，正准备横移绕过一处暴露得厉害的岩壁凸角，去寻找可能通过的路线。突然，一只小手从凸角另一边伸出来，正在摸索抓点。紧接着，一只瘦削的棕色肩膀慢慢挪进视野，随后出现了麦克惠斯特一生见过的最古怪的一张脸。根据他的描述，我几乎可以肯定那就是约翰。不过，他一再强调那张脸瘦得骇人，这让我很不安。两颊仿佛已经缩水成两片皮革，双眼却亮得惊人。约翰刚一出现，脸上立刻浮现出一种几乎近于恐惧的厌恶表情，随即又退回凸角后面。麦克惠斯特横移出去，想再看他一眼。约翰已经下到一面光滑岩壁的半腰，而这面岩壁正是他们上来时试过、后来嫌太难而放弃，改爬那道岩沟的地方。讲到这里，麦克惠斯特叫道：“老天！那小子真会爬！他简直像液体一样从一个支点流到另一个支点。”约翰下完那段险坡后，立刻斜向左边离开，很快消失不见。</p>
<p>他们最后一次遇见约翰，时间要长得多。那天傍晚很晚，他们正在暴风雪中摸索下山。两个人都已经浑身湿透。风猛烈得几乎让他们无法逆风前进。不久，他们意识到自己在云雾里走错了路，误上了山的另一条支脊。最后，他们发现自己四面都被悬崖堵住，只好结绳，沿着一条塞满坠石的沟槽或宽岩缝往下爬。爬到一半时，他们忽然闻到烟味，看见烟从路线旁边一块巨石板后方冒出来。那块石板卡在岩石夹角中。麦克惠斯特费了很大力气，依靠稀少而不可靠的支点横移过去，抵达冒烟石板附近的一小块平台；诺顿随后跟上。光线正从石板下面和后面透出来。再攀几步，他们便来到石板一端与崖壁之间那个被照亮的缝隙。石板两侧和另一端都死死卡在许多较小的石块中，又被岩沟两侧共同夹住，因此稳稳停在原地。他们弯下腰，从那明亮的小洞往里看，里面是个不规则的小洞穴，由一堆泥炭和石南点起的火照亮。约翰正躺在一张干草和干石南铺成的床上。他盯着火焰，满脸都是泪。他全身赤裸，不过身边堆着一团鹿皮。火旁的一块扁石上，放着一只烤熟的鸟，还剩下一部分。</p>
<p>两名攀岩者看见这个古怪男孩在流泪，不知为什么都感到有些窘迫，便悄悄退了出去。不过他们低声商量了一阵，认为无论如何都不能就这么不管他。于是，他们故意用靴子在岩石上弄出声响，装作刚刚走到洞口；自己仍藏在看不见的位置，由麦克惠斯特喊道：“里面有人吗？”没有回答。他们又从小小的入口往里窥看。约翰仍按原来的姿势躺着，毫无反应。那只鸟旁边放着一把结实的骨刀或短匕首，显然是“自己做的”，但磨得相当锋利，刀尖也修整得很好。周围还散着别的骨制或鹿角工具，其中一些刻有装饰花纹。此外，还有一支类似排箫的芦管乐器和一双兽皮凉鞋或软鞋。最让两人吃惊的是，这里完全没有文明留下的痕迹——比如说，什么金属制品都没有。</p>
<p>他们小心地又说了几句话，约翰仍没有理会。麦克惠斯特故意弄出声响，从洞口钻了进去，轻轻握住男孩赤裸的脚，摇了摇。约翰慢慢转过头，用迷惑的目光盯着闯入者；可紧接着，他整个人突然像被敌意的智慧点燃。他猛地蜷身蹲起，手里已经攥住一柄细长尖刀，那东西是用鹿角上最大的一根角叉磨成的。麦克惠斯特被他那双巨大而怒视的眼睛、以及不像人的咆哮吓了一跳，连忙从逼仄的洞口退了出去。</p>
<p>“接着，”麦克惠斯特说，“发生了一件怪事。那孩子的怒气仿佛一下子消失了。他死死盯着我，好像我是一种他生平从没见过的怪兽。突然之间，他似乎又想起了别的东西。他把武器扔下，重新盯着火，脸上又是那种彻底绝望的神情。眼泪再次涌上来。他的嘴扭成一种拼命想笑的样子。”</p>
<p>麦克惠斯特讲到这里停了下来，看上去既苦恼又尴尬。他狠狠吸了几口烟斗，终于继续往下说。</p>
<p>“显然，我们不能把孩子就这么扔在那儿，所以我很小心地问，有没有什么能替他做的。他没回答。我又爬进去，蹲在他旁边等着。我尽量轻地把手放到他膝盖上。他猛地一惊，浑身打了个寒战，然后皱着眉看我，仿佛正努力在脑子里把事情理清。他迅速伸手去拿那把尖刀，又硬生生停住；最后突然露出一种歪歪的、很孩子气的笑，说：‘噢，请进吧。别敲门，这是家商店。’随后又补一句：‘你们这些混蛋就不能让一个人安静待会儿吗？’我说，我们完全是偶然碰见他的，可当然难免觉得奇怪。我还说，前几天看见他的攀爬本事，我们都佩服得不得了；可惜他一个人困在这里。要不要跟我们一起走？他笑着摇头，说自己待在这里好得很，只是在过一个稍微粗野点的假期，顺便想些事情。刚开始时自己弄吃的有点困难，现在已经掌握诀窍了，而且留下大把时间可以思考。说完，他忽然笑了一声。那笑声又短又尖，像一下爆裂的脆响，听得我头皮发麻。”</p>
<p>诺顿这时插话说：“那会儿我也已经爬进洞里了。我一下子就被他那副瘦得可怕的样子震住了。全身上下找不到半点脂肪，肌肉像一绺绺绳子贴在皮肤底下。他浑身都是伤疤和淤青。可最叫人不安的还是他的脸——那种神情，我只在一个做完大手术、刚从麻醉里醒来的人脸上见过。怎么说呢，像是被净化过一样。可怜的小鬼，显然真是熬过了什么东西，可究竟熬过了什么？”</p>
<p>“起先，”麦克惠斯特说，“我们都以为他疯了。但现在我敢发誓，他没有疯。他像是被什么东西攫住了。某种我们根本不了解的东西占据了他；是好是坏，我说不清。整件事都让我毛骨悚然：外面暴风雪在吼，洞里只有昏暗火光，烟还不断从他自己弄出的那种烟道里倒灌回来。我们当时也饿得有点发昏。顺便说，他还把剩下的鸟肉和一些越橘给我们吃，不过我们当然不愿意把他的口粮吃掉。我们问，有没有什么是我们能帮他的。他说有：请我们务必不要把他的事告诉任何人。我问，要不要给他家里人带个消息。他立刻变得非常严肃，非常坚决，说：‘不。谁也别告诉，一个人都别告诉。忘掉这回事。要是报纸顺着我的踪迹找上来，’他说得很慢，声音冷冰冰的，‘我就只好自杀。’这下我们被难住了。我们觉得自己明明应该做点什么，可不知为什么，又觉得必须答应他。”</p>
<p>麦克惠斯特停了一下，随后有些恼火地说：“于是我们真答应了。然后就离开那里，在黑暗里跌跌撞撞摸回帐篷。下那段岩壁时，我们结了绳；那孩子没结绳，走在最前面给我们带路。”他又停了停，接着说：“前几天，我碰巧听布林斯顿提起你们那个失踪的孩子，就违背了承诺。现在我心里糟透了。”</p>
<p>我笑了，说：“没关系，没造成什么坏处。我不会去告诉报社。”</p>
<p>诺顿又开口：“事情没这么简单。麦克惠斯特还有一件事没告诉你。说吧，麦克。”</p>
<p>“你自己说。”麦克惠斯特答道，“我宁可不说。”</p>
<p>沉默了一阵。随后诺顿尴尬地笑了，说：“好吧，这种事坐在这里喝着咖啡，清醒地讲出来，听上去简直疯透了。可该死，如果那件事根本没发生，那我们两个身上也一定发生过某种极其古怪的事，因为我们俩都看见了——看得就像你现在看见我们一样清楚。”</p>
<p>他停了下来。麦克惠斯特从扶手椅上站起身，开始端详我们身后书架上一排排的书。</p>
<p>诺顿继续说：“那孩子说了些话，大意是要让我们明白，我们碰上了某种巨大、而我们无法理解的东西；还说他要给我们一点值得记住的东西，好帮助我们保守秘密。他的声音变得很怪，非常低，非常安静，镇定得异常。他把那条瘦骨嶙峋的胳膊伸向洞顶，说：‘这块石板至少有五十吨重。它上面就是暴风雪。你们看，门口还能看见雨点。’他指着洞口。‘那又怎样？’他用一种冷而骄傲的声音说，‘让我们看看星星。’接着——老天，你当然不会相信——那孩子用十根手指尖托着那块该死的巨石，像掀活板门一样把它举了起来。一股猛烈的风和冰雨瞬间灌进洞里，可立刻又消失了。随着石板升高，他也站起身来。头顶竟是一片无风、清澈、布满群星的天空。火堆的烟柱摇晃着直线上升，底部被火光照亮，越往上越暗，最后在很高的地方散开，像一道黑色尾迹遮住几颗星。他把石板一直推到竖直，然后只用一条手臂轻轻倚着它，另一只手叉在腰间。‘看。’他说。借着星光和火光，我看见他仰头望着天空的脸。要我说，那张脸像被彻底改变了——明亮、锐利、安详。”</p>
<p>“他一动不动地站了大概半分钟，也一句话没说。然后低头看我们，笑了笑，说：‘别忘了。我们曾一起看过星星。’接着，他轻轻把巨石放回原位，说：‘我想你们现在最好走了。我带你们下第一段。晚上那里很难走。’我们俩当时差不多已经被困惑震得麻木了，一时谁也没有要离开的意思。他轻轻笑了，像是在安慰我们，然后说了一句话，从那以后一直缠着我——麦克惠斯特是不是也这样，我不知道。他说：‘这只是一个孩子气的奇迹。可我仍然是个孩子。当精神正在痛苦地摆脱自身的孩子气时，它偶尔也可以回到自己的玩具旁边，借此得到一点安慰——只要它清楚知道，那些东西本来微不足道。’这时我们已经开始从洞里往外爬，重新钻进暴风雪。”</p>
<p>屋里沉默下来。过了一会儿，麦克惠斯特重新转过身，神色有些狂乱地瞪着诺顿。“我们得到过一个伟大的征兆，”他说，“可我们背叛了它。”</p>
<p>我试图安慰他说：“也许在字面上背叛了，但在精神上没有。我很确定，约翰不会介意我知道。至于那个奇迹，我看不必为它烦恼。”这话说得比我实际拥有的信心更足，“他大概只是用某种古怪方法把你们俩都催眠了。他本来就是个怪孩子。”</p>
<p>夏末的时候，帕克斯收到一张明信片，上面写着：“明晚很晚回家。请准备热水澡。约翰。”</p>
<p>一有机会，我便同约翰长谈了他的这次假期。让我惊讶的是，他完全愿意坦率地说，而且此前让我们如此担心、促使他逃离家的那段封闭而痛苦的时期，似乎已经彻底过去。我怀疑自己是否真正听懂了他告诉我的内容；而且我确信，他还有很多东西没有说，因为他知道我理解不了。我始终有一种感觉：他一直在努力把自己真正的思想翻译成我能听懂的语言，而这种翻译在他看来粗陋得近乎可笑。下面，我只能复述其中我所理解的那一部分。</p>
</section>

<h3><span id="a341"></span>CHAPTER XI. STRANGE ENCOUNTERS</h3>
<p><span class="t28">T</span><span class="t31">HE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">GRAVE</span><span class="t30"> </span><span class="t31">DECISION</span><span class="t30"> </span>about the plight of Homo sapiens seems to have occurred at a time when John’s own development had ripened him for a far-reaching spiritual crisis. Some weeks after the incident which I have just described he seemed to retire within himself more than ever, and to shun companionship even with those who had counted themselves among his friends. His former lively interest in the strange creatures among whom he lived apparently evaporated. His conversation became perfunctory; save on rare occasions when he flared up into hostile arrogance. Sometimes he seemed to long for intimacy and yet be quite unable to attain it. He would persuade me to go off with him for a day on the hills or for an evening at the theatre, and after a brave effort to restore our accustomed relationship, he would fall miserably silent, scarcely listening to my attempts at conversation. Or he would dog his mother’s footsteps for a while, and yet find nothing to say to her. She was thoroughly frightened about his state, and indeed feared that “his brain was giving out,” so blankly miserable and speechless could he be. One night, so she told me, she heard sounds in his room and crept in to see what was the matter. He was “sobbing like a child that can’t wake from a bad dream.” She stroked his head and begged him to tell her all about it. Still sobbing, he said, “Oh, Pax, I’m so lonely.”</p>
<p>When this distressful state of affairs had lasted many weeks, John disappeared from home. His parents were well used to absences of a few days, but this time they received a post card, bearing a Scotch postmark, saying that he was going to have a holiday in the mountains, and would not be back “for quite a long time.”</p>
<p>A month later, when we were beginning to feel anxious about him, an acquaintance of mine, Ted Brinston, who knew that John had disappeared, told me that a friend of his, McWhist, who was a rock-climber, had encountered “a sort of wild boy In the mountains of northern Scotland.” He offered to put me in touch with McWhist.</p>
<p>After some delay Brinston asked me to dine with him to meet McWhist and his climbing companion, Norton. When the occasion arrived I was surprised and disconcerted to find that both men seemed reluctant to speak frankly about the incident that had brought us four together. Alcohol, however, or my anxiety about John, finally broke down their reluctance. They had been exploring the little-known crags of Ross and Cromarty, pitching their tent for a few days at a time beside a handy burn or loch. One hot day, as they were climbing a grassy spur of a mountain (which they refused to name) they heard a strange noise, apparently coming from the head of the glen to the right of them. They were so intrigued by its half-animal, half-human character that they went in search of its cause. Presently they came down to the stream and encountered a naked boy sitting beside a little waterfall and chanting or howling “in a way that gave me the creeps,” said McWhist. The lad saw them and fled, disappearing among the birch-trees. They searched, but could not find him.</p>
<p>A few days later they told this story in a little public-house. A red-bearded native, who had not drunk too little, immediately retailed a number of yarns about encounters with such a lad — if it was a lad, and not some sort of kelpie. The good man’s own sister-in-law’s nephew said he had actually chased him and seen him turn into a wisp of mist. Another had come face to face with him round a rock, and the creature’s eyes were as large as cannon balls, and black as hell.</p>
<p>Later in the week the climbers came upon the wild boy again. They had been climbing a rather difficult chimney, and had reached a point where further direct ascent seemed impossible. McWhist, who was leading, had just brought his second man up, and was preparing to traverse round a very exposed buttress in search of a feasible route. Suddenly a small hand appeared round the far side of the buttress, feeling for a hold. A moment later a lean brown shoulder edged its way round into view, followed by the strangest face that McWhist had ever seen. From his description I judged confidently that it was John. I was disturbed by the stress that McWhist laid on the leanness of the face. The cheeks seemed to have shrunk to pieces of leather, and there was a startling brightness about the eyes. No sooner had John appeared than his face took on an expression of disgust almost amounting to horror, and he vanished back round the buttress. McWhist traversed out to catch a view of him again. John was already half-way down a smooth face of rock which the climbers had attempted on the way up and then rejected in favour of the chimney. Recounting the incident, McWhist ejaculated, “God! The lad could climb! He oozed from hold to hold.” When John reached the bottom of the bad pitch, he cut away to the left and disappeared.</p>
<p>Their final encounter with John was more prolonged. They were groping their way down the mountain late one evening in a blizzard. They were both wet through. The wind was so violent that they could hardly make headway against it. Presently they realized that they had missed their way in the cloud, and were on the wrong spur of the mountain. Finally they found themselves hemmed in by precipices, but they roped themselves and managed to climb down a gully or wide chimney, choked with fallen rocks. Half-way down, they were surprised to smell smoke, and saw it issuing from behind a great slab jammed in an angle of rocks beside their route. With considerable difficulty McWhist worked his way by rare and precarious holds over to a little platform near the smoking slab, and Norton followed. Light came from under it and behind it. A step or two of scrambling brought them to the illuminated space between one end of the slab and the cliff. The sides and opposite end of the slab were jammed in a mass of lesser rocks, and held in position by the two sides of the chimney. Stooping, they peered through the bright hole into a little irregular cave, which was lit by a fire of peat and heather. Stretched on a bed of dried grass and heather lay John. He was gazing into the fire, and his face was streaming with tears. He was naked, but there was a jumble of deer-skin beside him. By the fire was part of a cooked bird on a flat stone.</p>
<p>Feeling unaccountably abashed by the tears of this strange lad, the climbers quietly withdrew. Whispering together, however, they decided that they really must do something about him. Therefore, making a noise on the rocks with their boots as though in the act of reaching the cave, they remained out of sight while McWhist demanded, “Is anyone there?” No answer was given. Once more they peered through the tiny entrance. John lay as before, and took no notice. Near the bird lay a stout bone knife or dirk, obviously “home-made,” but carefully pointed and edged. Other implements of bone or antler were scattered about. Some of them were decorated with engraved patterns. There was also a sort of pan-pipe of reeds and a pair of hide sandals or moccasins. The climbers were struck by the fact that there were no traces whatever of civilization, nothing, for instance, that was made of metal.</p>
<p>Cautiously they spoke again, but still John took no notice. McWhist crept through the entrance, noisily, and laid a hand on the boy’s bare foot, gently shaking it. John slowly looked round and stared in a puzzled way at the intruder; then suddenly his whole form came alive with hostile intelligence. He sprang into a crouching posture, clutching a sort of stiletto made of the largest tine of an antler. McWhist was so startled by the huge glaring eyes and the inhuman snarl that he backed out of the cramping entrance of the cave.</p>
<p>“Then,” said McWhist, “an odd thing happened. The boy’s anger seemed to vanish, and he stared intently at me as though I were a strange beast that he had never seen before. Suddenly he seemed to think of something else. He dropped his weapon, and began gazing into the fire again with that look of utter misery. Tears welled in his eyes again. His mouth twisted itself in a kind of desperate smile.”</p>
<p>Here McWhist paused in his narrative, looking both distressed and awkward. He sucked violently at his pipe. At last he proceeded.</p>
<p>“Obviously we couldn’t leave the kid like that, so I asked cautiously if we could do anything for him. He did not answer. I crept in again, and squatted beside him, waiting. As gently as I could I put a hand on his knee. He gave a start and a shudder, looked at me with a frown, as if trying to get things straight in his mind, made a quick movement for the stiletto, checked himself, and finally broke into a wry boyish grin, remarking, ‘Oh, come in, please. Don’t knock, it’s a shop.’ He added, ‘Can’t you blighters leave a fellow alone?’ I said we had come upon him quite by accident, but of course we couldn’t help being puzzled about him. I said we’d been very struck with his climbing, the other day. I said it seemed a pity for him to be stuck up here alone. Wouldn’t he come along with us? He shook his head, smiling, and said he was quite all right there. He was just having a bit of a rough holiday, and thinking about things. At first it had been difficult feeding himself, but now he’d got the technique, and there was plenty of time for the thinking. Then he laughed. A sudden sharp crackle it was that made my scalp tingle.”</p>
<p>Here Norton broke in and said, “I had crawled into the cave by then, and I was terribly struck by the gaunt condition he was in. There wasn’t a bit of fat on him anywhere. His muscles looked like skeins of cord under his skin. He was covered with scars and bruises. But the most disturbing thing was the look on his face, a look that I have only seen on someone that had just come out of the anaesthetic after a bad op. — sort of purified. Poor kid, he’d evidently been through it all right, but through what?”</p>
<p>“At first,” said McWhist, “we thought he was mad. But now I’m ready to swear he wasn’t. He was possessed. Something that we don’t know anything about had got him, something good or bad, I don’t know which. The whole business gave me the creeps, what with the noise of the storm outside, and the dim firelight, and the smoke that kept blowing back on us down the sort of chimney he’d made for it. We were a bit light-headed with lack of food, too. He offered us the rest of his bird, by the way, and some bilberries, but of course we didn’t want to run him short. We asked if there wasn’t anything we could do for him, and he answered, yes, there was, we could make a special point of not telling anyone about him. I said, couldn’t we take a message to his people. He grew very serious and emphatic, and said, ‘No, don’t tell a soul, not a soul. Forget. If the papers get on to my tracks,’ he said very slowly and coldly, ‘I shall just have to kill myself.’ This put us in a hole. We felt we really ought to do something and yet somehow we felt we must promise.”</p>
<p>McWhist paused, then said crossly, “And we did promise. And then we cleared out, and floundered about in the dark till we reached our tent. We roped to get down the rock, and the lad went in front, unroped, to show us the way.” He paused again, then added, “The other day when I happened to hear from Brinston about your lost lad, I broke my promise. And now I’m feeling damned bad about it.”</p>
<p>I laughed, and said, “Well, no harm’s done. I shan’t tell the Press.”</p>
<p>Norton spoke again. “It’s not as simple as that. There’s something McWhist hasn’t told you yet. Go on, Mac.”</p>
<p>“Tell him yourself,” said McWhist, “I’d rather not.”</p>
<p>There was a pause, then Norton laughed awkwardly, and said, “Well, when one tries to describe it in cold blood over a cup of coffee, it just sounds crazy. But damn it, if the thing didn’t happen, something mighty queer must have happened to us, for we both saw it, as clearly as you see us now.”</p>
<p>He paused. McWhist rose from his arm-chair and began examining the rows of books on shelves behind us.</p>
<p>Norton proceeded: “The lad said something about making us realize we’d come up against something big that we couldn’t understand, said he’d give us something to remember, and help us to keep the secret. His voice had changed oddly. It was very low and quiet and composed. He stretched his skinny arm up to the roof, saying, ‘This slab must weigh fifty tons. Above it there’s just blizzard. You can see the raindrops in the doorway there.’ He pointed to the cave entrance. ‘What of it?’ he said in a cold proud voice. ‘Let us see the stars.’ Then, my God, you won’t believe it, of course, but the boy lifted that blasted rock upon his finger-tips like a trap door. A terrific gust of wind and sleet entered, but immediately died away. As he lifted he rose to his feet. Overhead was a windless, clear, starry sky. The smoke of the fire rose as a wavering column, illuminated at the base, and spreading dimly far above us, where it blotted out a few stars with a trail of darkness. He pushed the rock back till it was upright, then leaned lightly against it with one arm, crooking the other on his hip. ‘There!’ he said. In the starlight and firelight I could see his face as he gazed upwards. Transfigured, I should call it, bright, keen, peaceful.</p>
<p>“He stayed still for perhaps half a minute, and silent; then, looking down at us, he smiled, and said, ‘Don’t forget. We have looked at the stars together.’ Then he gently lowered the rock into position again, and said, ‘I think you had better go now. I’ll take you down the first pitch. It’s difficult by night.’ As we were both pretty well paralysed with bewilderment we made no immediate sign of quitting. He laughed, gently, reassuringly, and said something that has haunted me ever since. (I don’t know about McWhist.) He said, ‘It was a childish miracle. But I am still a child. While the spirit is in the agony of outgrowing its childishness, it may solace itself now and then by returning to its playthings, knowing well that they are trivial.’ By now we were creeping out of the cave, and into the blizzard.”</p>
<p>There was a silence. Presently McWhist faced us again, and glared rather wildly at Norton. “We were given a great sign,” he said, “and we have been unfaithful.”</p>
<p>I tried to calm him by saying, “Unfaithful in the letter, perhaps, but not in the spirit. I’m pretty sure John wouldn’t mind my knowing. And as to the miracle, I wouldn’t worry about that,” I said, with more confidence than I felt. “He probably hypnotized you both in some odd sort of way. He’s a weird kid.”</p>
<p>Toward the end of the summer Pax received a post card, saying “Home late to-morrow. Hot bath, please. John.”</p>
<p>On the first opportunity I had a long talk with John about his holiday. It was a surprise to me to find that he was ready to talk with perfect frankness, and that he had apparently quite got over the phase of uncommunicative misery which had caused us so much anxiety before his flight from home. I doubt if I really understood what he told me, and I am sure there was a good deal that he didn’t tell me because he knew I wouldn’t understand. I had a sense that he was all the while trying to translate his actual thoughts into language intelligible to me, and that the translation seemed to him very crude. I can give only so much of his statement as I understood.</p>
