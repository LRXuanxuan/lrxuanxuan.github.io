<h2>第二十六章</h2>
<p>西斯赖特一如既往地准时，把卡莫迪送进了另一个地球概率世界。</p>
<p>这次转换比“瞬间”还稍微快了一点。事实上，它快到让时间出现了极轻微的倒流，于是卡莫迪获得了一种相当诡异的体验：在接收到必要刺激之前，就先作出了反应。</p>
<p>这当然构成了矛盾。矛盾很小，可毕竟违法。西斯赖特用标准消除程序把它处理掉了，也没人费心向有关主管机关举报。除了让时空连续体多受了一点磨损之外，它没有造成任何后果，而卡莫迪甚至根本没有察觉。</p>
<p>卡莫迪发现自己站在一座小镇里。</p>
<p>单从表面上看，辨认这里似乎毫无困难：这座小镇就是——或者自称是——新泽西州的梅普尔伍德。卡莫迪从三岁到十八岁一直住在这里。假如他在宇宙中还算有一个“家”，那这里就是他的家。</p>
<p>或者更准确地说：如果这里真和看起来一样，那它就是他的家。</p>
<p>可这一点仍有待证明。</p>
<p>他站在杜兰路和梅普尔伍德大道交口，位于小镇上端。正前方是商业区。身后是一条条郊区街道，枫树、橡树、栗树、榆树、山茱萸以及其他树木枝繁叶茂。右边是基督科学会阅览室。左边是火车站。</p>
<p>“旅人，如今感觉如何？”一个声音在他右大腿旁边说道。</p>
<p>卡莫迪低头一看，发现自己手里提着一台相当大的晶体管收音机。他立刻明白，这就是奖品。</p>
<p>“所以你又回来了。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“回来？我从来没离开。”</p>
<p>“上一个概率世界里我根本没看见你。”</p>
<p>“那是因为你没认真找。”奖品说。“当时我就在你口袋里，变成了一枚伪造得很蹩脚的第纳尔银币。”</p>
<p>“那我怎么可能知道？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“你只要问就行。”奖品说。“我的本性就是不断变形，而且连我自己都预测不了下一刻会变成什么。这个你早就知道。难道每到一个地方，我都必须主动宣布一次自己在场吗？”</p>
<p>“那样会方便很多。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“我的自尊不允许我做这种充满焦虑感的行为。”奖品坚定地说。“有人叫我，我就回答；没人叫我，我就不擅自假定别人需要我。上一个概率世界里，你显然根本不需要我。所以我趁机去了斯洛克洛尔餐厅，好好吃了一顿；接着去哈加尼希特养护所，把外皮送去干洗；然后又去了瓦里内尔太阳信标酒吧，喝了几杯，和一个恰好在附近的朋友聊聊天；然后又去——”</p>
<p>“你怎么可能做完这么多事？”卡莫迪问。“我在那个世界待了还不到半小时。”</p>
<p>“我早就告诉过你，我们各自的持续时间流非常不一样。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“对，你确实说过……不过那些地方到底在哪儿？”</p>
<p>“要解释清楚会花上相当长时间。”奖品说。“事实上，直接去一趟都比解释怎么去容易。总之，那些地方也不适合你。”</p>
<p>“为什么？”</p>
<p>“嗯……理由很多。只举一个例子吧：太阳信标酒吧提供的食物，你肯定接受不了。”</p>
<p>“我已经见过你吃 orithi 了。”卡莫迪提醒奖品。</p>
<p>“对，当然。不过 orithi 只是偶尔品尝的珍馐，一辈子吃上一两次的小点心。可在太阳信标酒吧，我们奖品以及一些近缘物种吃的，是日常维生的主食。”</p>
<p>“那主食是什么？”</p>
<p>“你不会想知道的。”奖品警告他。</p>
<p>“我就是想知道。”</p>
<p>“我知道你现在想知道；可你听完以后，就会希望自己从来没知道过。”</p>
<p>“说吧。”卡莫迪说。“你的主食到底是什么？”</p>
<p>“好吧，爱打听的先生。”奖品说。“不过记住，是你坚持要问的。我的主食就是——我自己。”</p>
<p>“是什么？”</p>
<p>“我自己。我早说过你不会喜欢。”</p>
<p>“你的食物是你自己？你的意思是，你吃自己身体上的肉？”</p>
<p>“完全正确。”</p>
<p>“见鬼，”卡莫迪说，“先不说这有多恶心，根本就不可能。你不可能靠吃自己活下去！”</p>
<p>“我可以，而且我就是这么活的。”奖品说。“我还相当引以为荣。从道德角度看，这是个人自由最杰出的范例之一。”</p>
<p>“可它就是不可能。”卡莫迪说。“这违反能量守恒，或者质量守恒，或者类似的什么定律。反正他妈的一定违反了某条自然规律。”</p>
<p>“只在一种专门化的意义上如此。”奖品说。“如果更仔细地研究一下这个问题，我想你会发现，这种不可能性更多只是表面现象，而不是真正的不可能。”</p>
<p>“这他妈又是什么意思？”</p>
<p>“我不知道。”奖品坦白。“我们所有教科书上都这么回答。以前从来没人质疑过。”</p>
<p>“我要把这事弄明白。”卡莫迪说。“你是说，你会真真正正、字面意义上地吃掉自己身体的一部分？”</p>
<p>“对。”奖品说。“就是这个意思。不过你也不该把范围只限定在肌肉。我的肝脏其实相当美味，尤其切碎以后拌上煮熟的鸡蛋，再加一点鸡油。我的小排骨也很适合随便做顿快餐；至于后腿肉，最好先轻度腌制几个星期，然后——”</p>
<p>“够了。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“抱歉。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“可你只回答我一件事：你这一辈子，身体怎么可能持续给身体提供足够食物？——这句话说出来都荒唐。”</p>
<p>“嗯，”奖品若有所思地说，“首先，我饭量并不特别大。”</p>
<p>“也许我还没说清楚。”卡莫迪说。“我的意思是：如果你一边用自己的身体组织喂养身体，一边又要保持身体本身，那你怎么补足被吃掉的那些体积？”</p>
<p>“恐怕我没太听懂。”奖品说。</p>
<p>“我再试一次。假设你吃掉自己的肉——”</p>
<p>“事实上我确实这么做。”奖品插嘴。</p>
<p>“假设你吃掉自己的肉，又把消化所得用来滋养同一批肉……等一下。假设你体重五十磅——”</p>
<p>“事实上，在我的母星，我恰好重五十磅。”</p>
<p>“太好了！那么，你重五十磅，又假设在一年里吃掉自己四十磅，以维持自己的生命，那么最后还剩多少？”</p>
<p>“十磅？”奖品问。</p>
<p>“妈的，你还看不出我要说什么吗？你根本不可能长期靠自己喂养自己。”</p>
<p>“为什么不行？”奖品问。</p>
<p>“收益递减定律。”卡莫迪说，感觉脑袋有点发飘。“最后你会把自己吃光，再也没有东西可吃，然后你就死了。”</p>
<p>“这一点我当然知道。”奖品说。“可死亡本来就是不可避免的事实。对自食者如此，对吃别人的他食者也一样。卡莫迪，不管一个东西吃谁、吃什么，最终所有东西、所有人都会死。”</p>
<p>“你在耍我！”卡莫迪嚎叫。“你要真这么吃，一个星期就死了。”</p>
<p>“有些昆虫一生只有一天。”奖品说。“其实从寿命角度而言，我们奖品活得还相当不错。别忘了，我们吃得越多，需要继续供养的身体就越少，剩下的食物反而能维持得越久。而且在自体捕食中，时间是一个极其重要的因素。大多数奖品在婴儿期就先把自己的未来吃掉，因此实际肉身可以一直完整保留到成年。”</p>
<p>“他们怎么吃掉自己的未来？”卡莫迪问。</p>
<p>“我解释不了怎么做到。”奖品说。“我们就是能做到，仅此而已。比如我，我小时候就一口气吃掉了自己八十岁到九十二岁那段生命的实体——顺便一说，那些都是老朽的年份，本来我也不会喜欢。现在只要节制地食用自己，我想应该能活到七十多岁后期。”</p>
<p>“你让我头痛。”卡莫迪说。“而且还有点恶心。”</p>
<p>“是吗？”奖品愤怒地说。“你居然还有脸恶心！你这个血淋淋的屠夫，一辈子吃过多少动物身体的切块？吞掉过多少毫无防备的苹果？又有多少颗生菜被你冷酷地从菜畦里连根拔起？不错，我偶尔确实吃过 orithi；可到了审判日，你得面对被你吞掉的那些畜群。它们会一排排站在你面前，卡莫迪——几百头长着褐色眼睛的母牛，几千只毫无抵抗能力的母鸡，无穷无尽的一排排温顺小羊；更别提那些遭蹂躏的果树林，以及一英亩又一英亩被你血洗的菜园。吃掉的 orithi，我会付出代价；可你呢？那些被你大吃大嚼、堆积如山、尖叫着死去的动物和植物生命，你要怎么赎罪？怎么赎，卡莫迪，怎么赎？”</p>
<p>“闭嘴。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>“哦，好吧。”奖品闷闷不乐地说。</p>
<p>“我吃东西，是因为我必须吃。这就是我的天性。事情到此为止。”</p>
<p>“你说是就是吧。”</p>
<p>“我他妈当然说是！现在你能不能闭嘴，让我集中注意力？”</p>
<p>“我保证一个字都不说。”奖品说，“除了问一句：你到底想集中注意力做什么？”</p>
<p>“这里看起来像我的故乡。”卡莫迪解释。“我正在判断，它到底真的是不是。”</p>
<p>“这总不至于太难吧。”奖品说。“我的意思是，人总该认得自己的故乡，不是吗？”</p>
<p>“不一定。我住在这里的时候，从来没有认真看过它；离开以后，也几乎没怎么想起它。”</p>
<p>“如果连你都分不清哪里是自己的家、哪里不是，”奖品说，“那就再也没有别人能分清。希望你明白这一点。”</p>
<p>“我明白。”卡莫迪说。</p>
<p>他开始沿梅普尔伍德大道慢慢向前走。</p>
<p>突然之间，一种可怕的感觉攫住了他：</p>
<p>无论他最后作出什么判断，那个判断都一定会是错的。</p>
</section>

<p><b>CHAPTER 26</b></p>
<p>With his usual punctuality, Seethwright sent him into another of Earth’s probability-worlds. The transition was somewhat faster than instantaneous. It was so rapid, in fact, that time became ever so slightly retrograde, and Carmody had the eerie experience of giving a response before receiving the requisite stimulus. That was a contradiction, of course, a very small one, but still illegal. Seethwright took care of it by a standard obliteration procedure, and no one bothered to report it to the proper authorities. Its effect was nil except for the wear and tear on the space-time continuum, which Carmody didn’t even notice.</p>
<p>Carmody found himself in a small town. Superficially, there was no problem of identification; this town was, or purported to be, Maplewood, New Jersey. Carmody had lived here between the ages of three and eighteen. This was his home, in so far as he had a home anywhere.</p>
<p>Or, more precisely, this was his home if it was what it seemed to be. But that remained to be proved.</p>
<p>He was standing on the corner of Durand Road and Maplewood Avenue, at the upper end of the town. Straight ahead was the shopping centre. Behind him were suburban streets rich in maple, oak, chestnut, elm, dogwood, and others. On his right was the Christian Science reading room. On his left was the railroad station.</p>
<p>‘How now, voyager?’ said a voice near his right thigh.</p>
<p>Carmody looked down and saw that he was carrying a fair-sized transistor radio. This, he knew at once, was the Prize.</p>
<p>‘So you’ve come back,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Back? I never left.’</p>
<p>‘I didn’t see you in the last probability-world.’</p>
<p>‘That’s because you weren’t looking very hard,’ the Prize said. ‘I was in your pocket in the form of a badly forged denarius.’</p>
<p>‘How am I supposed to know that?’ Carmody inquired.</p>
<p>‘All you have to do is ask,’ the Prize said. ‘I am metamorphic by nature, and unpredictable even to myself. But you know that. Must I announce my presence each and every time we go somewhere?’</p>
<p>‘It would help,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘My pride would not allow such anxiety-ridden behaviour,’ the Prize said firmly. ‘I answer when called; when not called, I do not assume that my presence is required. It was quite obvious that you didn’t need me in the last probability-world. Therefore I took the opportunity to go to Sloklol’s Restaurant for a decent feed, and then to the Haganicht Proparium to have my hide dry-cleaned, and then to Varinell’s Solar Beacon Pub for a few drinks and a chat with a friend who happened to be in the neighbourhood, and then to –’</p>
<p>‘How could you have done all that?’ Carmody asked. ‘I wasn’t in that world for over half an hour.’</p>
<p>‘I told you that our duration-flows are quite dissimilar,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘Yes, so you did … But whereabouts are those places?’</p>
<p>‘That would take quite a little while to explain,’ the Prize said. ‘As a matter of fact, it’s easier to go there than to explain how to go there. Anyhow, they’re the wrong kind of places for you.’</p>
<p>‘Why?’</p>
<p>‘Well … there are many reasons. But to cite only one, you would disapprove of the food eaten at the Solar Beacon Pub.’</p>
<p>‘I’ve already seen you eat <i>orithi,</i>’ Carmody reminded the Prize.</p>
<p>‘Yes, of course. But <i>orithi</i> are an infrequent delicacy, morsels to be eaten once or twice in a lifetime. Whereas at the Solar Beacon Pub we Prizes and related species eat our staple subsistence diet.’</p>
<p>‘And what is that?’</p>
<p>‘You Wouldn’t want to know,’ the Prize warned him.</p>
<p>‘I do want to know.’</p>
<p>‘I know that you <i>do</i> want to know; but after you hear, you will wish that you <i>didn’t</i> know.’</p>
<p>‘Out with it,’ Carmody said. ‘What is your staple diet?’</p>
<p>‘All right, Mr Nosey,’ the Prize said. ‘But remember, you insisted upon knowing. My staple diet is myself.’</p>
<p>‘Is what?’</p>
<p>‘Myself. I said you Wouldn’t like it.’</p>
<p>‘Your diet is yourself? You mean that you feast off your own body?’</p>
<p>‘Precisely.’</p>
<p>‘Damn it all,’ Carmody said, ‘aside from being repulsive, that’s impossible. You can’t live off yourself!’</p>
<p>‘I can and I do,’ the Prize said. ‘And I’m quite proud of the fact. Morally, it is an outstanding example of personal freedom.’</p>
<p>‘But it just is’t possible,’ Carmody said. ‘It violates the law of conservation of energy, or mass, or something like that. It sure as hell violates <i>some</i> natural law.’</p>
<p>‘That’s true, but only in a specialized sense,’ the Prize said. ‘When you come to examine the matter more closely, you can, I think, see that the impossibility is more apparent than real.’</p>
<p>‘What in hell does <i>that</i> mean?’</p>
<p>‘I don’t know,’ the Prize confessed. ‘It’s the answer in all our textbooks. Nobody ever questioned it before.’</p>
<p>‘I want to get this straight,’ Carmody said. ‘Do you mean that you actually and literally <i>eat</i> portions of your own flesh?’</p>
<p>‘Yes,’ the Prize said. ‘That’s what I mean. Though you shouldn’t confine it solely to my flesh. My liver is a tasty morsel, especially when chopped up with a hard-boiled egg and a little chicken fat. And my short ribs have served me well for a quick, casual sort of dinner; whereas my hams ought to be mild-cured for several weeks before –’</p>
<p>‘Enough,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘I’m sorry,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘But just tell me this: how can your body provide enough food for your body (this sounds ridiculous) throughout a lifetime?’</p>
<p>‘Well,’ the Prize said thoughtfully, ‘for one thing, I’m not a particularly heavy eater.’</p>
<p>‘Perhaps I haven’t made myself clear,’ Carmody said. ‘I mean, how can you provide bulk for your body if you are simultaneously <i>using</i> that bulk to feed your body with?’</p>
<p>‘I’m afraid I don’t quite understand that,’ the Prize said.</p>
<p>‘Let me try again. I mean this: if you consume your flesh –’</p>
<p>‘And in fact I do,’ the Prize put in.</p>
<p>‘If you consume your flesh, and utilize the product of that consumption for the nutrition of that same flesh … Just a minute. If you weighed fifty pounds –’</p>
<p>‘In point of fact, on my home planet I weigh precisely fifty pounds.’</p>
<p>‘Excellent! Well, then. If you weigh fifty pounds, and, over the course of, let us say a year, consume forty pounds of yourself in order to support yourself, then what are you left with?’</p>
<p>‘Ten pounds?’ the Prize asked.</p>
<p>‘Goddamn it, can’t you see what I’m driving at? You simply cannot nourish yourself <i>on</i> yourself for any length of time.’</p>
<p>‘Why can’t I?’ the Prize asked.</p>
<p>‘The Law of Diminishing Returns,’ Carmody said, feeling lightheaded. ‘Eventually there will be no more of you left for you to feed upon, and you will die.’</p>
<p>‘I am quite aware of that,’ the Prize said. ‘But death is an inexorable fact, as true and unavoidable for the self-eaters as for the other-eaters. Everything and everybody dies, Carmody, no matter who or what it feeds upon.’</p>
<p>‘You’re putting me on!’ Carmody howled. ‘If you really did feed like that, you’d be dead in a week.’</p>
<p>‘There are insects whose lifespan is but a single day,’ the Prize said. ‘Actually, we Prizes do rather well, longevity-wise. Remember, the more we consume, the less of us there is to be nourished, and the longer the remaining food lasts. And time is a great factor in autopredation. Most Prizes consume their future while in their infancy, thus leaving the actual corpus untouched until they have come into their maturity.’</p>
<p>‘How do they consume their future?’ Carmody asked.</p>
<p>‘I can’t explain how,’ the Prize said. ‘We simply do it, that’s all. I, for example, gobbled up my substance for the ages eighty through ninety-two – senile years, by the way, which I wouldn’t have enjoyed anyway. Now, by rationing my intake of myself, I think I can make it to my late seventies.’</p>
<p>‘You’re giving me a headache,’ Carmody said. ‘And you’re also making me somewhat nauseous.’</p>
<p>‘Indeed?’ said the Prize indignantly. ‘You’ve got a hell of a nerve to feel nauseous! You bloody butcher, how many animal sections have you consumed in your lifetime? How many defenceless apples have you gobbled, how many heads of lettuce have you callously ripped from their beds? I have eaten an occasional <i>orithi,</i> to be sure; but at Judgement Day you will have to face the herds you have devoured. They will stand before you, Carmody, hundreds of brown-eyed cows, thousands of defenceless hens, endless rows of gentle little lambs; to say nothing of the forests of raped fruit trees and the acres of savaged gardens. I will pay for the <i>orithi</i> I have eaten; but how will you ever atone for the shrieking mounds of animal and vegetable life that you have feasted upon? How, Carmody, how?’</p>
<p>‘Shut up,’ Carmody said.</p>
<p>‘Oh, very well,’ the Prize said sulkily.</p>
<p>‘I eat because I must. It’s part of my nature. That’s all there is to it.’</p>
<p>‘If you say so.’</p>
<p>‘I damned well do say so! Now will you shut up and let me concentrate?’</p>
<p>‘I won’t say another word,’ the Prize said, ‘except to ask you what you are trying to concentrate on.’</p>
<p>‘This place looks like my home town,’ Carmody explained. ‘I’m trying to decide if it really is or not.’</p>
<p>‘Surely that can’t be so difficult,’ the Prize said. ‘I mean to say, one knows one’s own home town, doesn’t one?’</p>
<p>‘No. I never looked at it closely while I lived here, and I didn’t think about it much after I left.’</p>
<p>‘If you can’t figure out what is your home and what is not,’ the Prize said, ‘then no one can. I hope you realize that.’</p>
<p>‘I realize it,’ Carmody said. He began to walk slowly down Maplewood Avenue. He had the sudden terrible feeling that any decision he made would be wrong.</p>
