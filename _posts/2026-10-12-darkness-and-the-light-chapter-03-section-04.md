<h2>四、西藏的崛起</h2>
<p>俄罗斯与中国两大帝国最终爆发的生死斗争，中心正是西藏。更重要的是，光明意志与黑暗意志之间的平衡似乎也正是在西藏最终被打破。因此，我们必须较详细地考察西藏人的命运。</p>
<p>这片高耸、封闭而大部分干旱的土地，过去曾是古代中华帝国的边疆，却始终保持一定程度的独立。在中国同日本漫长战争期间，这种独立变成完全独立；此后，西藏的僧侣寡头通过让俄罗斯与中国彼此牵制，维持自由。在西藏境内，俄国秘密宣传者与中国秘密宣传者长期争斗，但西藏政府对两边都进行了强力抵抗。自欧洲商业扩张时代以来，西藏一直努力保存本土文化。外国人被排除在境外，开发西藏自然资源的外国贷款也被拒绝。然而壁垒还是一点点破裂。欧洲和美国的商品与观念，随后俄罗斯和中国的商品与观念，终究进入高原峡谷与平原。现代农业技术、现代交通方式、电影与无线电，似乎都威胁着这个最后一个未经机械化文化堡垒的独特性。</p>
<p>但对西藏而言，“事先有警觉”确实意味着“事先有防备”。经历一段内部斗争后，一个经济上进步、文化上保守的政党夺取权力，并对国家经济生活发动革命。新的统治者，也就是大喇嘛的新顾问们，很明智地区分了现代主义在物质上的成就与它在社会、道德上的荒谬。他们着手使国家在物质上现代化，甚至在心智上也作一定程度的现代化，同时保存本土文化生活的核心。在这一点上，他们不过是在重走日本的道路，却始终把那个暴发式现代社会的悲剧例子摆在眼前。更何况，西藏文化中存在某种比日本文化深得多、更精神化也更坚韧的东西。这个国家天然贫困，反而成了福气。强大邻国认为西藏不值得系统开发或征服；而西藏迟来的、自力更生的发展尝试，即使它真想那样做，也不可能制造出那种奢侈洪流与疯狂商业权力欲——正是后者曾使欧洲统治阶级软弱腐化。从物质资源看，西藏确实微不足道。除东部仍有一些金矿外，矿产很少，农业又被严重缺水束缚，就连牧场起初也极其贫瘠。绵羊、牛，尤其是耐寒的本地牦牛，构成人口生活基础。政府发动大规模灌溉工程，人民则自愿、甚至带着英雄主义热情参与。人们希望几十年内，国家相当一部分土地都能进行集约耕作。</p>
<p>但西藏最主要的资源其实是人民本身。这个民族和平、勤劳而坚韧；领导者鼓励他们不要把自己看作一个注定被外国力量吞没的落后民族，而应把自己看成世界普遍陷入黑暗时古老智慧的保管者。近来的某些领导人还进一步提出：西藏人现在必须成为一种新的、综合性智慧的开拓者，让古代与现代以一种比俄罗斯和中国这些堕落社会中更有意义的方式结合起来。</p>
<p>第一次西藏革命的领导者虽然模糊看到必须修改本土文化，却没有能力在实践中继续推动自己已经开启的伟大发展过程。于是必须发生第二次革命；它由喇嘛阶层中面向未来的一派领导，并得到人民支持。这个新领导阶层本身就是第一次革命的产物。节制的繁荣增加了人民的闲暇与思考能力。人们固然渴望国家获得某些物质改善，却躲过了现代工业主义危险的魔咒，因为这种简单信条此时已经在全世界有思想的人中失去信誉。那些自称“光明的仆人”的人，既欢迎政府提供的科学教育，也欢迎政府对古老智慧的坚持。事实上，青年人开始成群进入寺院，尤其进入那些经过改革、带现代主义色彩的僧团。这个新喇嘛阶层的领导者，通常都先扎实学习佛教原则，成年后又受到现代观念巨大影响，却没有因此背叛本土文化的核心。多数人都曾在中国或印度生活一两年，许多人去过俄罗斯，还有一些人去过美国，并在那里受到贵格会影响。海外接触让他们清楚看见最糟糕的喇嘛制度中包含多少迷信与伪善，也看见大量正统学问何等浅薄、装腔作势。但这种幻灭并没有摧毁信仰，反而使被扭曲的真理显得更清楚。他们宣称，这种真理首先不是理智上的，而是直觉上的；它是一种感受或领悟，使一切事物都回到真正的比例和位置。佛教整个思想大厦，不过是对这种难以言说的发现所作的解释——有时解释得好，有时解释得坏。而这项发现也不可能一击即得，只能通过真实生活中长期纪律逐步获得。他们还认为现代主义同样包含一种情感上的真理。除科学之外，现代文化真正的成就可归纳为三点：第一，它强调个人与社会行动，反对东方的消极静止；第二，它要求一切人类都拥有平等机会；第三，它理解一切人类思想与情感背后那些原始而无意识的根源。</p>
<p>新的僧团起初受到拉萨寡头容忍，甚至得到鼓励，后来却因为煽动不满而遭训斥。因为这些团体虽然各自在陡峭山中的寺院设有总部，成员却定期外出，向民众宣讲。他们实际上介于僧侣、托钵修士与革命者之间。他们传播一种宗教共产主义，要求统治阶级——富裕的旧僧团——退位。真正的危机发生在新喇嘛放弃独身制时；独身制几百年来一直是僧侣阶层的规范。推动这一变化的理由完全是现代主义的。新僧团认识到，人类两种最珍贵的先天社会能力，一是形成真正共同体的倾向，二是进行理智行动的能力。他们同时意识到，西藏的平均智力虽然没有像那些更“先进”的国家下降得那么厉害，却一直有较聪明者流入独身僧团，因而从生育人口中被持续抽走。“光明的仆人”说，这必须停止。他们既承认自我否定对于精神纪律的重要性，也承认让智力继续繁衍的重要性。因此，他们大胆宣布，即使“不借助独身这根拐杖”，也要追求完整的精神纪律与洞察。光明的仆人即便承担其他沉重责任，也不能逃避生物学上的责任。不仅如此，那些承担领导和治理人民职责的人，也不应逃避家庭生活的经验——其中既有考验，也有对心智的丰富。他们承认家庭生活不能吞噬过多注意力，因此主张由国家承担所有儿童养育的最终责任。</p>
<p>放弃独身、并攻击统治阶级，必然导致新旧僧团严重冲突。大喇嘛理所当然地把光明的仆人逐出教门，最后宣布他们非法。内战随即爆发。年轻喇嘛——也就是光明的仆人——得到人民强烈支持，因此取得决定性胜利。恰好在西藏历史这一关键时刻，俄罗斯和中国都无力有效干预，因为任何一方一旦行动，都可能引发另一方进攻；而两帝国内部本来就严重不稳，战争很可能立刻转成内战。于是第二次西藏革命成功完成，一个全新的西藏诞生了——在此前所有政治家眼中，这种社会都会像荒诞的梦。</p>
<p>国家继续维持适度经济发展，但新政府的主要工作是教育人民，使其成为公民，并让他们掌握一种经过净化、重新解释的古老文化。同时，新一代在经济和教育方面的机会平等被提升到绝对原则。新宪法规定，最终权力属于所有成年人。只有由全民选出的大会才能修改宪法，而这个大会也可以罢免政府或拒绝拨款。日常立法却并非由全民大会直接承担，而是交给一个由“积极公民”选出的机构。所谓积极公民，是完成某些社会服务、并通过一定智力测验和学术考试的男女。他们从自己内部选出代表，但只有接受过严格政治训练——既包括实践，也包括理论——的人才有资格参选。与这套制度并行的，还有一种按职业组织起来的苏维埃式体系。所有重要立法都必须同时得到积极公民代表，以及职业体系中经选举产生的最高机构批准。若不是全国本来就已经拥有相当高的政治教育水平，也已经存在一批在革命中证明过自己的可信领导者，这套宪法根本不可能运行。</p>
<p>新政府立刻通过大量进步立法。一切生产资料名义上归国家所有，但在适当监督下，把管理权委托给各职业群体。农民尤其得到自己土地的所有权保障：有些方面实行个体控制，有些方面采取合作控制。政府还向“全世界一切善意之人”发出呼吁，要求大家以新的勇气努力建立一个统一的新世界秩序，“建立自由与精神的统治”。声明说，西藏人将把自己完全奉献给这一目标。</p>
<p>等我开始讲述光明意志取得胜利时，我还会回到人类历史的这个节点。但眼下，我必须从这里继续追踪不断加深的黑暗；因为恰恰就在光明意志仿佛获得前所未有力量的这一刻，黑暗意志也开始聚集力量，准备取得最后胜利。</p>
<p>历史真正的分叉也许早在这一天之前就已开始。它可能始于中国，始于俄罗斯，始于美国，始于英国；也可能在这些国家的不同时间分别开始。但同样有可能，西藏才是真正的关键点。无论实际分叉究竟发生在何处，新西藏同两个强大邻国之间的关系，至少构成了那场伟大复制第一次变得清晰、并且不可逆转的时刻。从此以后，我的经验变成双重。一方面，我看见西藏复兴失败，西藏民族遭到毁灭；随后爆发最后的俄中战争，它统一了全人类，却同时削弱了人类的能力。另一方面，我又看见西藏人在几乎已经张开血盆大口的毁灭面前，创造出一种人类从未实现过的共同体。而正是这个共同体大大增强了两个敌对帝国内部光明力量的信心，以至于战争最终转变成一场革命战争，席卷整个行星，直到光明意志在各处取得胜利才告结束。</p>
</section>

<h2>iv. THE RISE OF TIBET</h2>
<p>THE LIFE AND death struggle which at last broke out between the empires of Russia and China centred upon Tibet. More important, it was seemingly in Tibet that the balance between the will for darkness and the will for the light was finally destroyed. It is necessary therefore to examine the fortunes of the Tibetans in some detail.</p>
<p>Although their lofty, secluded, and mainly arid land had formerly been an outpost of the ancient Chinese Empire, it had always maintained a measure of independence. During China’s long struggle with Japan this independence had become absolute, and henceforth the clerical oligarchy of Tibet maintained its freedom by playing off Russia and China against one another. Within the Tibetan frontiers there was a constant struggle between the secret propagandists of Russia and those of China, but the Tibetan government put up a strong resistance against both. Ever since the age of the commercial expansion of Europe Tibet had fought for the preservation of native culture. Foreigners had been excluded from the country. Foreign loans for exploitation of Tibet’s natural resources had been refused. Little by little, however, the barriers had broken down. European and American, and subsequently Russian and Chinese, goods and ideas had found their way into the high valleys and plains. Modern aids to agriculture, modern methods of transport, the cinema, the radio, seemed to threaten to destroy the individuality of this last stronghold of unmechanized culture.</p>
<p>But in the case of Tibet, forewarned was indeed forearmed. After a period of internal conflict an economically progressive, but culturally conservative, party was able to seize power and effect a revolution in the economic life of the country. The new rulers, the new advisers of the Grand Lama, wisely distinguished between the material achievements of modernism and its social and moral absurdities. They undertook to modernize their country materially and even to some extent mentally, while preserving the essentials of the native cultural life. In this they were but following in the footsteps of the Japanese, but with the tragic example of that upstart modern society ever before them. Moreover in the Tibetan culture there was something far deeper, more spiritual and more hardy than in the culture of Japan. The natural poverty of the country, too, had proved a blessing. Powerful neighbours regarded Tibet as not worth systematic exploitation or conquest; and the belated native attempt to develop the country without foreign aid could not produce, even if it had been intended to do so, anything like the flood of luxury and the insane lust for commercial power which had enervated the dominant class in Europe. Physically Tibetan resources were indeed negligible. Save for certain remaining deposits of gold, mostly in the eastern part of the country, there was little mineral wealth, and agriculture was hobbled by severe shortage of water. Even pasture was at first desperately meagre. Sheep and cattle, however, and particularly the hardy native yak, formed the mainstay of the population. The government undertook a great irrigation scheme; with the willing and even heroic co-operation of the people. Within a few decades, it was hoped, much of the country would be capable of intensive cultivation.</p>
<p>But the main resources of Tibet were the people themselves. A pacific, industrious, and sturdy folk, they had been encouraged to regard themselves not as a backward race doomed to succumb to foreign powers, but as the custodians of the ancient wisdom in a period of worldwide darkness. Some of their recent leaders had suggested also that the Tibetan people must now become the pioneers of a new and comprehensive wisdom in which ancient and modern should be combined more significantly than was possible, for instance, in the depraved communities of Russia and China.</p>
<p>The leaders of the first Tibetan revolution, though they saw vaguely the need to modify the native culture, were not in practice able to carry forward the great process of development which they had started. There had to be a second revolution, which was led by the forward- looking section of the Lama class, with the backing of the people. This new class of leaders had come into being through the first revolution. A measure of frugal prosperity had increased the people’s leisure and thoughtfulness. Though they were eager for certain physical improvements to their country, they had escaped the dangerous spell of modern industrialism, for that simple faith had by now been discredited among thoughtful people throughout the world. Though these ‘servants of the light’, as they called themselves, welcomed the scientific education which the government offered them, they also welcomed its insistence on the ancient wisdom. Indeed the young began flocking into the monasteries, and particularly to the houses of the reformed, modernistic monastic orders. The leaders of this new Lama class were persons who, after being well grounded in the principles of Buddhism, had in their maturity been greatly influenced by modern ideas without being false to the essence of the native culture. Most of them had spent a year or two in China or India, many in Russia, some in America, where they had been impressed by the Friends. Foreign contacts had made them realize fully the superstition and hypocrisy of the worst type of Lamas and the shallow pretentiousness of much of the orthodox learning. But this disillusionment had merely brought out more clearly the truth which had been perverted. This, they affirmed, was a truth not of intellect but of intuition. It was a feeling or apprehension of something which put all things into their true perspective. The whole intellectual edifice of Buddhism, they said, was an attempt, sometimes sound sometimes false, to elucidate this inarticulate discovery. And the discovery itself was to be won not at a stroke but progressively, through a long discipline of actual life. In modernism also they found a truth of feeling. The real achievement of modern culture, apart from science, they summarized under three headings; first, its insistence on action, individual and social, as opposed to Eastern quietism; second, its demand for equality of opportunity for all human beings; and, finally, its understanding of the primitive unconscious sources of all human thought and feeling.</p>
<p>The new monastic orders were at first tolerated and even encouraged by the Lhasa oligarchy, but presently they were reprimanded for stirring up unrest. For though each had its headquarters in some craggy monastery, the inmates travelled periodically, exhorting the people. They were in fact something between monks, friars, and revolutionaries. They preached a sort of religious communism, and demanded the abdication of the ruling class, the wealthy monastic orders. The crisis came when the new Lamas renounced the celibacy which for centuries had been accepted by the monastic class. The motive of this change was a thoroughly modernistic motive. It was realized in the new monasteries that the two most precious innate social capacities were the disposition for genuine community and the capacity for intelligent action. It was realized also that, although the average level of intelligence had not sunk so far in Tibet as in more advanced’ countries, there was a steady drain of the more intelligent into the celibate monastic orders. This, said the servants of the light, must stop. Recognizing the importance of self-denial for spiritual discipline, they recognized also the importance of propagating intelligence. They therefore boldly affirmed their intention of striving for complete spiritual discipline and insight though ‘unsupported by the prop of celibacy’. Biological responsibility, they said, must not be shirked by the servants of the light, even though they must assume other weighty responsibilities. Not only so, but the experience of family life, with all its trials and all its mental enrichment, must not be shirked by those who undertook to lead and govern the people. They recognized that family life must not be allowed to absorb too much attention, but to avoid this they advocated that the state should assume the final responsibility for the upbringing of all children.</p>
<p>The renunciation of celibacy and the attack on the ruling class inevitably caused a serious conflict between the old and the new monastic orders. Inevitably the Grand Lama excommunicated the servants of the light, and finally outlawed them. Civil war followed. Since the Young Lamas, the servants of the light, were strongly supported by the people, their victory was decisive. It happened that at this critical moment of Tibetan history neither Russia nor China was in a position to interfere effectively, because a move by either would have precipitated an attack by the other; and since internal unrest in both empires was grave, war would have turned into civil war. So the second Tibetan revolution was successfully accomplished, and a new Tibet was founded, a society which to all earlier statesmen would have seemed a fantastic dream.</p>
<p>While modest economic development was continued, the main work of the new government was to educate the people in citizenship and in the new, purged version of the ancient culture. At the same time equality of opportunity for the rising generation, opportunity both economic and educational, was made absolute. In the new constitution ultimate power lay with the whole adult population. The constitution could be altered only by their elected assembly, which also could depose the government or withhold supplies. Current legislation, however, was carried out not by the general assembly but by a body elected by a section of the population known as the Active Citizens. These were men and women who had qualified by undertaking certain kinds of social service and by passing certain intelligence tests and academic examinations. The Active Citizens elected representatives from among themselves, but only those who had completed a rigorous political training, practical and theoretical, could stand for election. Parallel with this system there was a kind of Soviet system, based on occupation. All important legislation had to be sanctioned both by the representatives of the Active Citizens and by the body which formed the elected apex of this occupational system. This constitution could never have been put into action had there not already existed throughout the country a high standard of political education and a body of trusted leaders, proved in the revolution.</p>
<p>The new government at once passed a mass of progressive legislation. Ownership of all means of production was vested in the state, but delegated, with suitable checks, to the occupations themselves. In particular, the peasants were assured of ownership of their land. For some purposes their control was individualistic, and for other purposes co-operative. The government also issued ‘an appeal to all persons of goodwill throughout the world’ to work with new courage to found a new and unified world order, ‘to establish freedom and the rule of the spirit’. The Tibetans, it declared, dedicated themselves absolutely to this end.</p>
<p>It is to this point of the history of man that I shall return when I begin to tell of the triumph of the will for light. Meanwhile I must from this point pursue the story of increasing darkness; for at this very moment, when seemingly the will for the light had gained unprecedented power, the will for darkness gathered its strength for final triumph.</p>
<p>The actual bifurcation of history may have begun long before this date. It may have begun in China, in Russia, in America, in Britain, or in all these countries at different dates. But equally it may well be that Tibet was the crucial point. Whatever the truth about the actual bifurcation, the relations of the new Tibet with its two mighty neighbours constituted the occasion on which the great duplication became unmistakable and irrevocable. Henceforth my experience was dual. On the one hand I witnessed the failure of the Tibetan renaissance, and the destruction of the Tibetan people. This was followed by the final Russo-Chinese war which unified the human race but also undermined its capacity. On the other hand I saw the Tibetans create, seemingly in the very jaws of destruction, a community such as man had never before achieved. And this community, I saw, so fortified the forces of the light in the rival empires that the war developed into a revolutionary war which spread over the whole planet, and did not end until the will for the light had gained victory everywhere.</p>
