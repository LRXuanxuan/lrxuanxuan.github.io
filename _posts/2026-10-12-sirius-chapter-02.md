<h3>第二章</h3>
<h3>天狼星的诞生</h3>
<p>普拉克茜的父亲托马斯·特里隆是一位太过杰出的科学家，不可能完全逃开公众的注意；不过，他关于刺激哺乳动物大脑皮层生长的研究，早在他还只是个才华横溢的年轻研究人员时便开始了，而后来始终是在严格保密的状态下继续进行。他对公众关注怀有一种近乎夸张、甚至病态的厌恶。他为这种执念辩解说，自己害怕这套技术落到江湖骗子和逐利者手里，被拿去滥用。因此，多年来，知道这些实验的只有剑桥少数与他关系最密切的专业同僚，以及在实验中也承担了某种角色的妻子。</p>
<p>我看过他的记录，也读过他的论文，但由于没有受过科学训练，我只能以外行的方式介绍他的工作。通过把某种激素引入母体血液，他能够影响尚未出生的幼体的大脑发育。这种激素似乎有双重作用：一方面增加大脑皮层的实际体积，另一方面使神经纤维本身比正常情况细得多，因此在同样体积的脑组织中，可以容纳数量远更多的神经纤维，以及远更多的纤维间连接。据我所知，美国的扎门霍夫也做过某些相似实验；不过两种技术之间有一个重要区别。扎门霍夫只是让幼小动物直接摄入这种激素；而特里隆，正如我已经说过的，是借助母体血液把激素引入胎儿。这本身就是一项相当了不起的成就，因为母体与胎儿的循环系统之间有一层具有过滤作用的膜，彼此隔离得相当严密。特里隆遇到的一个困难是，这种激素会同时刺激母体和胎儿的大脑生长；而母体头骨已经发育成熟、无法扩张，于是脑组织必然会发生极其严重的挤压，除非找到某种办法使母体大脑免受刺激药物的作用，否则最终只能导致死亡。这个难题后来终于被克服。于是，人们总算能够保证未出生的动物拥有一个健康的母体环境。幼体出生后，特里隆会定期把一定剂量的激素加入食物；随着大脑逐渐接近他认为安全的最大尺寸，剂量便逐步减少。他还设计出一套方法，延缓颅骨各骨片之间骨缝的闭合，使头骨能够按需要继续扩张。</p>
<p>为了完善特里隆的技术，大量大鼠和小鼠成了牺牲品。最终，他终于培育出一批非同寻常的动物。他那些大头的大鼠、小鼠、豚鼠和兔子，身体通常并不健康，生命也几乎总会被这样那样的疾病过早截断，但在各自卑微的物种中，它们无疑堪称天才。它们穿越迷宫之类的任务学得异常快。事实上，在所有常见的动物智力测验中，它们都远远胜过本物种的正常成员；它们的心理水平与其说像啮齿动物，不如说更接近狗或猿。</p>
<p>不过，对特里隆来说，这还仅仅是开始。他一面改进技术，希望使实验动物拥有更健康的体质，一面又研究如何改变它们生命进程的节奏，让它们成熟得非常缓慢，并且活得远比同类正常寿命更久。显然，这一点非常重要。更大的大脑需要更长的生命，才能充分发挥它积累并吸收经验的更大潜力。只有在这两个方向都取得令人满意的进展之后，他才开始拿体形更大、等级更高的动物做实验。这是一项艰巨得多的事业，显然不可能很快见效。几年之后，他培育出几只聪明却病恹恹的猫，一只很机灵、却在漫长青春期中死去的猴子，还有一条大脑大到把失明而无用的双眼沿鼻梁向前挤出的狗。那条狗承受了太多痛苦，创造它的人最后只得不情愿地在它幼年时将其处死。</p>
<p>又过了好几年，特里隆的技术才完善到足以让他少关注一些生理问题，而把更多精力转向心理问题。与原来的计划相反，从此以后，他主要研究狗，而不是猿。猿当然更有希望取得引人注目的成功：它们天生的条件比狗优越，大脑更大，视觉更发达，而且还有手。可从特里隆的角度看，狗拥有一个压倒性的优点——它们能在我们的社会里获得远大于猿的行动自由。特里隆承认，若论心理上的独立性，他其实更愿意研究猫；可猫的体形太小，不合适。无论动物本身多大，要达到某种智力水平，大脑都必须具有一定的绝对体积，才能提供足够丰富的联想性神经通路。当然，在智力等级相同的条件下，小动物并不需要像大动物那样大的大脑；庞大的身体本身就需要相应更大的脑来操纵其“机械装置”。狮子的脑必须比猫的大，大象的脑甚至比一个聪明得多、但身体小得多的人还大。另一方面，无论动物体形如何，每一个智力等级都要求神经组织达到某种复杂程度，因而也要求大脑具有一定体积。相对于人的体形而言，人脑比象脑大得多。有些动物的身体足以容纳达到人类智力等级所需的大脑绝对体积，有些则做不到。一条大型犬很容易容纳得下，但对猫来说，如此巨大的增量会严重破坏整个机体的组织结构；至于老鼠，则根本不可能。</p>
<p>当然，在这一阶段，特里隆还从未指望把任何动物的心智能力提升到接近人类的程度。他的目标只不过是——用他自己的话说——造出“一种略高于亚人类水平的智力，一颗‘缺失环节’式的心智”。为此，狗非常合适。人类社会本来就为狗提供了许多需要接近亚人类智力上限才能胜任的工作。特里隆认为，其中最理想的职业就是牧羊犬。他公开承认的雄心，就是培育出一种“超级牧羊犬”。</p>
<p>还有另一个考虑促使他选择狗。而他在研究这么早的阶段就把这一点纳入考虑，本身已经说明，他当时其实已经在试探着构想一种超越“缺失环节心智”的东西。他认为，总体而言，狗的性情比其他动物更有潜力发展到人类水平。猫胜在独立，狗则胜在社会知觉；而特里隆认为，只有社会性动物才能充分利用自己的智力。猫的独立，归根结底并不是一个具有社会意识的生物在坚持自己的个性，而只是由于社会知觉迟钝所造成的盲目个人主义。另一方面，他也承认，狗的社会性使它在面对人类时陷入卑屈的服从。但他希望，随着智力提升，狗也许能够获得一定程度的自尊，以及对人类进行批判性疏离观察的能力。</p>
<p>后来，特里隆终于成功培育出一窝大脑异常发达的幼犬。大多数在成年前便死了，只有两只活了下来，并成长为异常聪明的狗。对特里隆来说，这一结果总体上带来的失望多于满足。他继续实验，终于又让一条古代英国牧羊犬母犬产下一窝大脑发达的幼犬，其中三只活了下来，而且心智能力明确超过了普通犬类的水平。</p>
<p>研究又持续了几年。特里隆发现，他必须更仔细地挑选应用这套技术的“原材料”。他不能忽视这样一个事实：所有犬种中能力最强的，是经过两个世纪为智力和责任感而选择培育的边境牧羊犬。所有现代比赛冠军都属于这个品种，而且都能追溯到一条名叫“老汉普”的卓越名犬；它于1893年出生在诺森伯兰。今天的边境牧羊犬体格强健，却相对偏小。因此，特里隆认定，最理想的原材料应该是国际牧羊犬大赛中的杰出冠军与另一种同样聪明、但体形沉重得多的犬种杂交所得。阿尔萨斯狼犬显然是最自然的选择。经过与牧羊犬冠军的主人以及阿尔萨斯狼犬爱好者反复磋商，他培育出若干不同血系，以不同的比例混合这两种类型。随后，他把改进后的技术应用到这些血系中若干怀孕母犬身上。到了适当的时候，他已经可以给几位朋友送去一些具有“近乎缺失环节级智力”的家犬。不过，这些动物并没有什么惊世骇俗之处；而且不幸的是，它们全都体弱多病，还没度过被人为延长的青春期便死去了。</p>
<p>但最终，技术的进一步改进终于给他带来了真正的成功。他培育出几只异常聪明的动物，体质却与正常狗一样健壮，外形则主要呈现阿尔萨斯狼犬的特征。</p>
<p>他早已说服妻子伊丽莎白：一旦研究成功到这一步，他们就应该在威尔士的牧羊地区找一所房子。她和三个孩子以及即将出生的第四个婴儿住在那里，他本人则利用假期和周末前往。经过长时间寻找，他们终于在离特劳斯瓦尼兹不远的地方找到一座合适的老农舍，名叫“加思”。要把那里改造成舒适的家庭住宅，还得大动一番工程：安装抽水马桶和浴室，扩大几扇窗户，从村里接入电力，还把一间附属屋改造成一座堪称豪华的犬舍。</p>
<p>第四个孩子出生一段时间后，全家搬了过去。和他们一起去的还有凯特——这个在家里服务多年的女佣，不知不觉间早已差不多成了家庭成员。他们又雇了一个村里的姑娘给她打下手。家里还有一位保姆，米尔德里德；当然，还有几个孩子：托马西娜、莫里斯、贾尔斯，以及婴儿普拉克茜。托马斯还带去了两个犬类“家庭”。其中一窝是一条母犬和四只强健的小狗，他打算把它们训练成“超级牧羊犬”。另一窝则有四只孤儿，因为母犬在生产时死了，只能由人工喂养。这些幼犬的大脑比前一窝大得多，可惜其中三只的健康状况也差得多。搬到威尔士后不久，有两只死了；另一只经常发生极其剧烈的痉挛发作，只得被处死。第四只就是天狼星。它健康、开朗，其他那窝小狗早已进入活跃的少年期时，它却还长期停留在毫无自理能力的婴儿阶段。几个月里，它甚至连站都站不起来，只能趴在地上，鼓鼓的大脑袋贴着地面，因为单纯活着就快乐得吱吱叫；它的尾巴几乎总在不停摆动。</p>
<p>就算是另一窝幼犬，成熟速度对于狗来说也非常缓慢，虽然仍比人类儿童快得多。它们接近成年时，除了一只留下做家庭犬，其余都送给了附近的农场。当地大多数农民起初甚至不肯白收这些大头狗。不过，住在凯尔布莱的邻居卢埃林·皮尤先生倒很乐意参与这项尝试；后来，他又买下第二只幼犬，给第一只作同伴。</p>
<p>这些超级牧羊犬以及随后出现的同类，成了托马斯更令人激动的另一项尝试的烟幕，而当时这项尝试唯一的成果，就是天狼星。公众会被引导着相信，超级牧羊犬和其他具有“缺失环节心智”的动物，就是他研究的全部。如果那只小阿尔萨斯狼犬真的发展到了人类的心智高度，也几乎不会有人怀疑。托马斯始终病态地担心它会被人拿去利用。它必须在体面的默默无闻中长大，尽可能自然地成熟。</p>
<p>相反，那些超级牧羊犬倒被允许出了名。起初极不情愿收下它们的农民，很快便发现命运简直送给了自己无价珍珠。那些狗学会牧羊技巧的速度快得惊人，而且执行命令几乎从不出错。命令很少需要重复。它们从不把羊群赶得惊慌失措，却也从不让羊擅自脱队。还不止如此，特里隆的狗有一种近乎诡异的本领：不用人在旁监督，也能理解指令并独立完成。它们能把某些特定牧场、山坡、山谷和荒原的名称同正确地点联系起来。只要告诉它们“去凯文把羊带回来”，或者去莫埃尔法赫、去别的什么地方，它们就能独自完成，而主人只需留在家里等。它们也可以被派到附近的农场或村庄跑腿。给它们一个篮子和一张纸条，让它们去指定的店铺，它们就会把所需的肉类或针织杂货带回来。</p>
<p>这些能力对农民非常实用，对特里隆来说也极有研究价值；他当然得到了充分观察这些动物的机会。他发现，它们表现出惊人的实际创造力，以及一种虽然还很初级、却相当可观的语言理解能力。归根结底，它们仍然是亚人类动物，不可能像我们那样理解语言；但对于熟悉的词和短语，它们的敏感程度已经远非普通狗可比。“去棚里拿木柴”“把篮子送到肉店和面包店”之类简单、熟悉的命令，它们通常都能准确区分并执行，不容易被别的东西分散注意。托马斯写了一篇关于超级牧羊犬的专著，于是世界各地的科学家不断来到加思，观看这些动物工作。它们在整个地区的农民中也声名大噪，许多人都来求幼犬，可真正能提供的极少。有些农民不肯相信，这些聪明动物的后代不会遗传父母的能力。自然，如果不在母犬怀孕时施用那种激素，仅仅让超级牧羊犬彼此繁殖，所有试图得到新一代超级牧羊犬的努力都彻底失败了。</p>
<p>不过，现在该回到那只小阿尔萨斯狼犬——也就是天狼星——身上了。从一开始，特里隆就对这只动物异常兴奋。它越长时间停留在无助的婴儿阶段，他就越激动，因为他从中看见了实现自己近乎最狂野梦想的可能性。他和伊丽莎白讨论这件事，把她的想象力也点燃了，并向她展开自己的计划。这只动物应当尽可能获得与他们自己的婴儿相同类型的心理环境。他告诉她，美国有一对动物心理学家夫妇，曾把一只黑猩猩幼崽与自己的小女儿放在完全相同的条件下抚养：喂食、穿衣、照料都和孩子一样，结果非常有趣。托马斯说，他对小天狼星的打算并不完全一样，因为若把幼犬和婴儿一模一样地对待，反而会违背狗的本性；它的身体构造同婴儿差异太大。他真正想要的是，让天狼星从小感到自己在社会地位上与小普拉克茜完全平等。待遇上的差异绝不能暗示生物等级或社会等级上的差异。他说，伊丽莎白已经证明自己是一个理想的母亲：她让孩子感到自己受到一个神一般智慧而慷慨的存在全心全意的爱，同时又培养他们的独立性，从不以贪婪的情感需求束缚他们。托马斯要求给天狼星的，正是这样的氛围，再加上整个家庭环境。他还告诉她，他们这个家庭使他懂得了一个非常重要的道理。他自己童年时代的不幸经历，曾让他把家庭看成一种无可救药的糟糕制度，甚至认为它理应被废除。她一定还记得，他过去曾经多么疯狂地想拿自己的孩子做教育实验。她巧妙而坚决地抵挡住了每一次把头两个孩子从她身边带走的尝试；而到第三个孩子出生前，托马斯已经被说服，相信真正良好的家庭环境才是儿童成长最适宜的影响。她当然也犯过错误，他本人就更犯过许多。毫无疑问，他们无意中还是在某种程度上伤害了孩子——比如托马西娜偶尔会倔强得像头骡子，莫里斯则有些缺乏自信。但总体而言——嗯，若不承认三个孩子都相当出色，既友善、负责，又独立而富有批判性，那反倒是假谦虚，也对孩子们不公平。对于婴儿天狼星的伟大实验来说，这正是最理想的社会传统。托马斯提醒伊丽莎白，狗很容易变得奴性十足；但这种缺点多半并不是天性里本来就有什么奴性，而是因为狗强烈的社会敏感性，在控制它们的、更发达物种的暴政下，被迫走向了卑屈服从。一条拥有人类智力、并从小被培养出自尊的狗，很可能一点也不奴性，甚至可能发展出一种超越人类的、建立真正社会关系的天赋。</p>
<p>伊丽莎白花了一些时间考虑丈夫的建议，因为主要责任显然会落在她身上。况且，她自然也担心这场实验会不会对自己的婴儿产生影响：小普拉克茜会因此受到任何伤害吗？托马斯说服她相信，不仅不会有害，儿童与超级犬类之间的陪伴反而必定对双方都有益。他满怀热情地坚持说，最有价值的社会关系，恰恰存在于彼此差异尽可能大、却仍能够相互同情的心灵之间。托马斯本人并没有特别出色的洞察他人或共情能力，却能在理智上看见共同体的本质，这或许很值得注意。他说，观察这种困难却蕴含巨大可能性的伙伴关系如何成长，一定会非常有趣。当然，它也可能根本发展不起来，最终只有彼此敌视。伊丽莎白肯定需要极大的技巧，才能防止孩子凭借人类拥有的种种优势压倒那条狗。尤其是小女孩的双手以及更精细的视觉，都是幼犬永远无法获得的资本。整个不可避免地属于人类的环境，对狗而言又天然陌生而别扭；一颗不是人的、却像人一样敏感的心智，很可能因此发展出神经症。必须竭尽一切努力，避免天狼星像那些因自卑感而受苦的人类一样，要么过度屈从，要么以挑衅式的傲慢来反抗。</p>
<p>托马斯还希望伊丽莎白记住另一条原则。狗的天性最终会怎样发展，当然不可能预先知道。天狼星也许终究永远达不到任何接近人类的心智高度。但一切都必须以它能够达到为前提来安排。因此，把它当作一个“人”而不是宠物来抚养极其重要：它是一个个体，到了适当年龄，应当过积极而独立的生活。既然如此，它特有的能力也必须得到培养。当它仍处在——用托马斯的话说——“学龄男孩”阶段时，兴趣当然也会是“男孩子式”的：身体性的、原始的、野蛮的；但因为它是一条狗，这些兴趣的表达必然同真正的人类男孩很不一样。它需要通过正常的犬类活动去释放这些冲动，比如随意游荡、狩猎和打斗。但后来，随着智力向它打开人类世界，它会需要某种持久的“人类式”活动；而显然，牧羊可以给它提供一项职业，即使它最终远远超出典型超级牧羊犬的心智水平也一样。因此，无论未来命运如何，它都必须被养得“硬得像钉子，壮得见鬼”。伊丽莎白对自己的孩子一向也采取这种方针；不过，天狼星总有一天必须面对比最斯巴达式的人类家庭还要严酷得多的生活条件。不能简单粗暴地把它塞进这种环境。她必须设法诱导它主动想要这种艰苦，先是出于对自身天性的骄傲，后来则是为了自己的工作。当然，这并不适用于童年时期；但到了青春期，它应当自愿开始追求严酷的磨炼。再往后，等它的心智不再幼稚，它也许会彻底放弃牧羊犬这条道路，把思想投入更成熟的事业。即便如此，青年时代艰苦而实际的生活也绝不会白费，因为那会赋予它永久的坚韧与自立。</p>
<p>对于天狼星的未来，伊丽莎白远没有丈夫那么乐观。她提出一个托马斯并不在意的担忧：像天狼星这样内部如此分裂的存在，会不会注定终身承受精神上的折磨？尽管如此，她最终还是决定真正投入这场实验，并据此开始安排一切。</p>
</section>
<section data-story-lang="en" lang="en" hidden>
<h3><a id="a569"></a>CHAPTER II</h3>
<p><span class="t28">P</span><span class="t30">LAXY’S </span><span class="t31">FATHER</span><span class="t30">, T</span><span class="t31">HOMAS</span><span class="t30"> </span>Trelone, was too great a scientist to escape all publicity, but his work on the stimulation of cortical growth in the brains of mammals was begun while he was merely a brilliant young research worker, and it was subsequently carried on in strict secrecy. He had an exaggerated, a morbid loathing of limelight. This obsession he justified by explaining that he dreaded the exploitation of his technique by quacks and profit-mongers. Thus it was that for many years his experiments were known only to a few of his most intimate professional colleagues in Cambridge, and to his wife, who had a part to play in them.</p>
<p>Though I have seen his records and read his papers, I can give only a layman’s account of his work, for I am without scientific training. By introducing a certain hormone into the blood-stream of the mother he could affect the growth of the brain in the unborn young. The hormone apparently had a double effect. It increased the actual bulk of the cerebral cortices, and also it made the nerve-fibres themselves much finer than they normally are, so that a far greater number of them, and a far greater number of connections between them, occurred in any given volume of brain. Somewhat similar experiments, I believe, were carried out in America by Zamenhof; but there was an important difference between the two techniques. Zamenhof simply fed the young animal with his hormone; Trelone, as I have said, introduced his hormone into the foetus through the mother’s blood-stream. This in itself was a notable achievement, because the circulatory systems of mother and foetus are fairly well insulated from each other by a filtering membrane. One of Trelone’s difficulties was that the hormone caused growth in the maternal as well as the foetal brain, and since the mother’s skull was adult and rigid there must inevitably be very serious congestion, which would lead to death unless some means were found to insulate her brain from the stimulating drug. This difficulty was eventually overcome. At last it became possible to assure the unborn animal a healthy maternal environment. After its birth Trelone periodically added doses of the hormone to its food, gradually reducing the dose as the growing brain approached what he considered a safe maximum size. He had also devised a technique for delaying the closing of the sutures between the bones of the skull, so that the skull might continue to expand as required.</p>
<p>A large population of rats and mice was sacrificed in the attempt to perfect Trelone’s technique. At last he was able to produce a number of remarkable creatures. His big-headed rats, mice, guinea-pigs, rabbits, though their health was generally bad, and their lives were nearly always cut short by disease of one kind or another, were certainly geniuses of their humble order. They were remarkably quick at finding their way through mazes, and so on. In fact they far excelled their species in all the common tests of animal intelligence, and had the mentality rather of dogs and apes than of rodents.</p>
<p>But this was for Trelone only the beginning. While he was improving his technique so that he could ensure a rather more healthy animal, he at the same time undertook research into methods of altering the tempo of its life so that it should mature very slowly and live much longer than was normal to its kind. Obviously this was very important. A bigger brain needs a longer life-time to fulfil its greater potentiality for amassing and assimilating experience. Not until he had made satisfactory progress in both these enterprises did he begin to experiment on animals of greater size and higher type. This was a much more formidable undertaking, and promised no quick results. After a few years he had produced a number of clever but seedy cats, a bright monkey that died during its protracted adolescence, and a dog with so big a brain that its crushed and useless eyes were pushed forward along its nose. This creature suffered so much that its producer reluctantly destroyed it in infancy.</p>
<p>Not till several more years had elapsed, had Trelone perfected his technique to such an extent that he was able to pay less attention to the physiological and more to the psychological aspect of his problem. Contrary to his original plan, he worked henceforth mainly on dogs rather than apes. Of course apes offered the hope of more spectacular success. They were by nature better equipped than dogs. Their brains were bigger, their sight was more developed, and they had hands. Nevertheless from Trelone’s point of view dogs had one overwhelming advantage. They were capable of a much greater freedom of movement in our society. Trelone confessed that he would have preferred to work on cats, because of their more independent mentality; but their small size made them unsuitable. A certain absolute bulk of brain was necessary, no matter what the size of the animal, so as to afford a wealth of associative neural paths. Of course a small animal did not need as large a brain as a large animal of the same mental rank. A large body needed a correspondingly large brain merely to work its machinery. A lion’s brain had to be bigger than a cat’s. An elephant’s brain was even larger than a much more intelligent but smaller man’s. On the other hand, each rank of intelligence, no matter what the size of the animal, required a certain degree of complexity of neural organization, and so of brain bulk. In proportion to the size of the human body a man’s brain was far bigger than an elephant’s. Some animals were large enough to accommodate the absolute bulk of brain needed for the human order of intelligence; some were not. A large dog could easily do so, but a cat’s organization would be very gravely upset by so great an addition. For a mouse anything of the sort would be impossible.</p>
<p>Not that Trelone had at this stage any expectation of raising any animal so far in mental stature that it would approach human mentality. His aim was merely to produce, as he put it, “a rather super-sub-human intelligence, a missing-link mind.” For this purpose the dog was admirably suited. Human society afforded for dogs many vocations requiring intelligence at the upper limit of the sub-human range. Trelone chose as the best vocation of all for his purpose that of the sheep-dog. His acknowledged ambition was to produce a “super-sheep-dog.”</p>
<p>One other consideration inclined him to choose the dog; and the fact that he took this point into account at all in the early stage of his work shows that he was even then toying with the idea of producing something more than a missing-link mind. He regarded the dog’s temperament as on the whole more capable of development to the human level. If cats excelled in independence, dogs excelled in social awareness; and Trelone argued that only the social animal could make full use of its intelligence. The independence of the cat was not, after all, the independence of the socially aware creature asserting its individuality; it was merely the blind individualism that resulted from social obtuseness. On the other hand he admitted that the dog’s sociality involved it, in relation to man, in abject servility. But he hoped that with increased intelligence it might gain a measure of self-respect, and of critical detachment from humanity.</p>
<p>In due course Trelone succeeded in producing a litter of big-brained puppies. Most of them died before reaching maturity, but two survived, and became exceptionally bright dogs. This result was on the whole less gratifying than disappointing to Trelone. He carried out further experiments, and at last, from an Old English Sheep-dog bitch, produced a big-brained family, three of which survived, and reached a definitely super-canine level of mentality.</p>
<p>The research continued for some years. Trelone found it necessary to take more trouble about the “raw material” to which his technique was to be applied. He could not afford to neglect the fact that the most capable of all the canine races is the Border Collie, bred through a couple of centuries for intelligence and responsibility. All modern champions are of this breed, and all are descendants of a certain brilliant animal, named Old Hemp, who was born in Northumberland in 1893. The Border Collie of to-day is hardy, but rather small. Trelone, therefore, decided that the best raw material would be a cross between some outstanding champion of the International Sheep-Dog Trials and another intelligent but much heavier animal. The Alsatian was the obvious choice. After a good deal of negotiation with owners of champion sheep-dogs and enthusiasts for Alsatians, he produced several strains, which blended the two types in various proportions. He then applied his improved technique to various expectant mothers of these types, and in due season he was able to provide several of his friends with animals of “almost missing-link intelligence” as house-dogs. But there was nothing spectacular about these creatures; and unfortunately all were delicate, and all died before their somewhat protracted adolescence was completed.</p>
<p>But at last further improvements in his technique brought him real success. He achieved several very bright animals with normally strong constitutions, predominantly Alsatian in appearance.</p>
<p>He had persuaded his wife Elizabeth that, if ever he succeeded to this extent, they should take a house in a sheep district in Wales. There she and the three children and the forthcoming baby would live, and he himself would spend the vacations and week-ends. After much exploration they found a suitable old farm-house not far from Trawsfynedd. Its name was “Garth.” A good deal of work had to be done to turn it into a comfortable family home. Water-closets and a bathroom had to be installed. Some of the windows were enlarged. Electricity was laid on from the village. An outhouse was converted into a palatial kennel.</p>
<p>Some time after the fourth baby had been born, the family moved. They were accompanied by Kate, the long-established servant, who had somehow become practically a member of the family. A village girl was engaged as her assistant. There was also a nursemaid, Mildred; and, of course, the children, Thomasina, Maurice, Giles, and the baby Plaxy. Thomas took with him two canine families. One consisted of a bitch and four hardy little animals that he intended to train as “super-sheep-dogs.” The other family of four were orphans, the mother having died in giving birth to them. They had therefore to be hand-nursed. The brains of these animals were very much bigger than the brains of the other family, but unfortunately three of them were much less healthy. Two died shortly after the removal to Wales. Another was subject to such violent fits that it had to be destroyed. The fourth, Sirius, was a healthy and cheerful little creature that remained a helpless infant long after the other litter were active adolescents. For months it could not even stand. It merely lay on its stomach with its bulgy head on the ground, squeaking for sheer joy of life; for its tail was constantly wagging.</p>
<p>Even the other litter matured very slowly for dogs, though far more rapidly than human children. When they were nearly adult all but one of them were disposed of to neighbouring farms. The one was kept as the family dog. Most of the local farmers had proved very reluctant to take on these big-headed animals even as gifts. But a neighbour, Mr. Llewelyn Pugh of Caer Blai, had entered into the spirit of the venture, and he subsequently bought a second pup as a colleague for the first.</p>
<p>The production of these super-sheep-dogs and others which followed formed a camouflage for Thomas’s more exciting venture, of which Sirius was at present the only outcome. The public would be led to believe that super-sheep-dogs and other animals of missing-link mentality were his whole concern. If the little Alsatian really developed to human mental stature, few people would suspect it. Thomas was always morbidly anxious that it should not be exploited. It must grow up in decent obscurity, and mature as naturally as possible.</p>
<p>The super-sheep-dogs, on the other hand, were allowed to gain notoriety. The farmers who had accepted them mostly with great reluctance soon found that fate had given them pearls of great price. The animals learned their technique surprisingly quickly, and carried out their orders with unfailing precision. Commands had seldom to be repeated. Sheep were never hustled, and yet never allowed to break away. Not only so, but Trelone’s dogs had an uncanny way of understanding instructions and carrying them out with no human supervision. They attached the right meanings to the names of particular pastures, hillsides, valleys, moors. Told to “fetch sheep from Cefn” or from Moel Fach or what not, they succeeded in doing so while their master awaited them at home. They could also be sent on errands to neighbouring farms or villages. They would take a basket and a note to a particular shop and bring back the required meat or haberdashery.</p>
<p>All this was very useful to the farmers, and extremely interesting to Trelone, who was of course allowed every chance of studying the animals. He found in them a startlingly high degree of practical inventiveness, and a rudimentary but remarkable understanding of language. Being after all sub-human, they could not understand speech as we do, but they were incomparably more sensitive than ordinary dogs to familiar words and phrases. “Fetch wood from shed,” “Take basket to butcher and baker,” and all such simple familiar orders could be distinguished and obeyed, as a rule without distraction. Thomas wrote a monograph on his super-sheep-dogs, and consequently scientists from all over the world used to turn up at Garth to be shown the animals at work. Throughout the district their fame was fully established among farmers, and there were many demands for puppies. Very few could be supplied. Some farmers refused to believe that the offspring of these bright animals would not inherit their parents’ powers. Naturally, all attempts to breed super-sheep-dogs from super-sheep-dogs without the introduction of the hormone into the mother were a complete failure.</p>
<p>But it is time to return to the little Alsatian, in fact, to Sirius. Trelone was from the first very excited about this animal. The longer it remained a helpless infant, the more excited he became. He saw in it the possibility of the fulfilment of his almost wildest hopes. Discussing it with Elizabeth, he fired her imagination with the prospects of this canine infant, and unfolded his plan before her. This animal must have as far as possible the same kind of psychological environment as their own baby. He told her of an American animal-psychologist and his wife who had brought up a baby chimpanzee in precisely the same conditions as their own little girl. It was fed, clothed, cared for, exactly as the child; and with very interesting results. This, Thomas said, was not quite what he wanted for little Sirius, because one could not treat a puppy precisely as a baby without violating its nature. Its bodily organization was too different from the baby’s. But what he did want was that Sirius should be brought up to feel himself the social equal of little Plaxy. Differences of treatment must never suggest differences of biological or social rank. Elizabeth had already, he said, proved herself an ideal mother, giving the children that precious feeling of being devotedly loved by a divinely wise and generous being, yet fostering their independence and making no greedy emotional claims on them. This was the atmosphere that Thomas demanded for Sirius; this and the family environment. And their family, he told her, had taught him a very important truth. Unfortunate experiences in his own childhood had led him to regard family as a hopelessly bad institution, and one which ought to be abolished. She would remember his wild ideas of experimenting with their own children. She had tactfully and triumphantly resisted every attempt to remove her own first two children from her; and before the third was born Thomas was already convinced that a really good family environment was the right influence for a growing child. No doubt she had made mistakes. Certainly he had made many. No doubt they had to some extent unwittingly damaged their children. There was Tamsy’s occasional mulishness and Maurice’s diffidence. But on the whole — well it would be false modesty and unfair to the children not to recognize that they were all three fine specimens, friendly, responsible, yet independent and critical. This was the ideal social tradition in which to perform the great experiment with baby Sirius. Dogs, Thomas reminded Elizabeth, were prone to servility; but this vice was probably not due to something servile in their nature; it sprang from the fact that their great social sensitivity was forced to take a servile turn by the tyranny of the more developed species which controlled them. A dog with human intelligence, brought up to respect itself, would probably not be servile at all, and might quite well develop a superhuman gift for true social relationship.</p>
<p>Elizabeth took some time to consider her husband’s suggestion, for the responsibility would be mainly hers. Moreover, she was naturally anxious about the effects of the experiment on her own baby. Would her little Plaxy suffer in any way? Thomas persuaded her that no harm would be done, and indeed that the companionship of child and super-canine dog must be beneficial to both. With fervour he insisted that the most valuable social relationships were those between minds as different from one another as possible yet capable of mutual sympathy. It is perhaps remarkable that Thomas, who was not himself gifted with outstanding personal insight or sympathy, should have seen intellectually the essential nature of community. It would be very interesting, he said, to watch the growth of this difficult but pregnant companionship. Of course it might never develop. There might be mere antagonism. Certainly Elizabeth would have to exercise great tact to prevent the child from overpowering the dog with its many human advantages. In particular the little girl’s hands and more subtle eyesight would be assets which the puppy could never attain. And the whole human environment, which was inevitably alien and awkward for the dog, might well breed neurosis in a mind that was not human but humanly sensitive. Everything possible must be done to prevent Sirius from becoming either unduly submissive or defiantly arrogant in the manner so familiar in human beings suffering from a sense of inferiority.</p>
<p>One other principle Thomas wanted Elizabeth to bear in mind. It was, of course, impossible to know beforehand how the dog’s nature would develop. Sirius might, after all, never reach anything like human mental stature. But everything must be done on the assumption that he would do so. Hence it was very important to bring him up not as a pet but as a person, as an individual who would in due season live an active and independent life. This being so, his special powers must be fostered. While he was still, as Thomas put it, a “schoolboy,” his interests would, of course, be “schoolboy” interests, physical, primitive, barbarian; but being a dog, his expression of them would necessarily be very different from a real schoolboy’s. He would have to exercise them in normal canine occupations, such as desultory roaming and hunting and fighting. But later, as his intelligence opened up the human world to him, he would want some kind of persistent “human” activity; and obviously sheep could provide him with a career, even if he far excelled the typical super-sheep-dog mentality. With this in view, and whatever his destiny, he must be brought up “as hard as nails and fit as hell.” This had always been Elizabeth’s policy with her own children; but Sirius would some time need to face up to conditions far more Spartan than those of the most Spartan human family. It would not do simply to force him into such conditions. Somehow she must wile him into wanting them, for sheer pride in his own nature, and later for the sake of his work. This, of course, would not apply to his childhood, but in adolescence he must begin of his own free will to seek hardness. Later still, when his mind was no longer juvenile, he would perhaps drop the sheep-dog career entirely and give his mind to more adult pursuits. Even so, the hard practical life of his youth would not have been in vain. It would endow him with permanent grit and self-reliance.</p>
<p>Elizabeth was a good deal more sceptical than her husband about the future of Sirius. She expressed a fear, which did not trouble Thomas, that such a disunited being as Sirius might be doomed to a life of mental torture. Nevertheless, she finally made up her mind to enter into the spirit of the experiment, and she planned accordingly.</p>
