<h2>i. 海王星上的假日</h2>
<p>每当我置身于你们的世界、你们的时代，我常常会想起我自己的世界里一个僻静的地方，也想起我所谓的“现在”。那里是海岸向海中伸出的一角，无数裂碎的岩石杂乱地拥挤着，仿佛一群巨兽争先恐后地挤入水中。很久以前，地下力量曾在这里把行星的地壳拱成一座山；然而，重力——一切巨大世界中那冷酷无情的精灵——立刻把它撕裂、压碎。如今剩下的只有这些岩石。海王星上没有山，海洋也没有波涛。这颗结实的球体把自己的水之披风紧紧箍在身上，哪怕最猛烈的飓风，也只能在海面上激起些许涟漪。</p>
<p>散布在岩石之间的是一张细小峡湾织成的网，它们的岩壁与海床上密密镶嵌着形形色色的生命。透过水晶般清澈的海水，你会看见各种鼓包、结节和鲜艳的旋纹；看见种种华丽的花朵，有的用花瓣四处探寻，有的有节奏地咂动嘴唇；还会看见团团纠结的海藻，绿的、褐的、紫的、绯红的。海藻深处偶尔会突然伸出一只螯，抓向一条昏昏欲睡的小鱼；这里那里，又有一条周身生着腿状流苏的虫子钻出来，在阳光照亮的沙质海床上探索。</p>
<p>就在这些岩石与峡湾之间，我度过了出发前最后一个闲暇日。此后，我将投入一次漫长的过去探索；正是这种探索，使我对你们的世界几乎像对自己的世界一样熟悉。我的任务，是告诉你们：在遥远未来的眼中，你们这个种族究竟是什么样子。但在此之前，我必须先帮助你们在想象中重建未来本身的一角，以及那个我们用来观察你们的世界。要做到这一点，最好的办法是先讲述那一天的欢乐——那一天，我在那座碎裂的山体倾入海中的地方度过；然后再讲一件更为庄严的事，即“种族心灵”的短暂苏醒。按照安排，这次苏醒发生在探险者启程深入往昔亿万年幽暗腹地的前夕，用来提升他们的精神。最后，我还会谈谈自己的成长经历与生涯。</p>
<p>那天休假的最初几个时刻，便给了我一幅此后永远萦绕在记忆中的画面。太阳已从燃烧般的海洋上升起。你们也许以为，在我们如此遥远的时代，太阳理应已经变得又小又暗；其实不然。因为在你们的时代与我们的时代之间，一次碰撞增加了它的体量与光辉，使它比你们所熟悉的太阳还要稍大、稍亮。</p>
<p>头顶的天空是蓝色的。但在海王星人的眼中，那深沉的蔚蓝里还浸染着另一种独立的基本色，而你们的视觉根本无法察觉它。朝着日出的方向，这种来自天顶的色调渐渐让位于绿色、金色、火红、紫色，以及另一种同样超出原始眼睛能力范围的色彩。与日出相反的一边仍是一片黑暗。但在低处的黑暗中，有某个东西发着光；你们也许会把它看成一座极其遥远、覆雪的尖峰，山脚沉没在夜色里，山巅却被晨光染成橙色。然而再看一眼，你们便会发现，它对于一座山来说过于陡峭，也过于几何化。事实上，那是我们的一座大型公共建筑，距离此地数十英里，高度将近二十英里。在一个连山岳都会被自身重量压垮的世界里，若没有不可思议的刚性材料，这样高耸的建筑绝无可能站立；而那些材料的主要成分，是人工原子。此刻显现出来的这座巨大石质峭壁，在我们的尺度上还算年轻，却足以与地球上较年轻的山脉比肩其年龄。</p>
<p>它的扶壁和山墙背阴的一面，以及附近岩石与每一块石头处于阴影中的表面，都泛着一层紫色的光华，那是来自一颗炫目紫星的光。我们把这可怕的天兆称作“狂星”。它是一颗独一无二的天体，正以不可想象的速度挥霍自身能量，因此很快便会燃尽。与此同时，它已经开始把自己的灾变传染给近邻。再过几千年，我们自己的太阳也不可避免地会以同样方式失控，把所有行星烧成白炽气体。不过眼下——我是说，在我称作“现在”的时代——狂星还只是我们夜空里一颗异常辉煌的天体。</p>
<p>我说的那个早晨，在一处小悬崖边缘，俯卧着一个属于我世界的女人。她正低头看着下面的水潭。对我而言，她美丽、精致，几乎就是美本身的化身；可在你们眼里，她大概会像一头奇怪的半人怪物。她胸部贴着岩石，一只手臂伸进水中；在我看来，她整个身体都具有黑豹般的轻盈与柔韧。可在你们眼里，她也许会显得笨重得像大象，每一个特征都古怪可怖。然而，如果你们真的看见她在自己的世界中运动，我想你们就会明白，为什么她在我们语言中的名字，恰好相当于你们的“黑豹”。</p>
<p>如果你们中的任何人来到我们的世界，而且竟能奇迹般在陌生的大气中活上几分钟，那么单是重力，就几乎足以让你们连支撑自己的身体都做不到。但我们不同：我们的骨骼和建筑一样，大部分由人工原子构成，比钢铁坚硬得多；我们的肌肉细胞也经过极其精巧的设计，因此能够轻松奔跑跳跃。不过，即使拥有如此出色的组织，我们的身体仍必须比地球人结实粗壮得多；你们那些细长的四肢，在我们看来总令人不快地联想到昆虫。</p>
<p>岩石上的那个女人一定会让你们吃惊，因为她属于我们最近才出现的几代人之一，她的皮肤与肌肉呈暗色半透明状。阳光浸透她的四肢时，你们也许会以为她是一尊用酒红色雪花石膏或红宝石雕成的塑像；只是她每一次移动手臂，肩膀与腰胁内部的暗夜里都会荡起深红、黄玉和金褐色的光。她身体优美的曲线与平面之内，整个物质似乎已经不太像固体，倒像一团暗淡的火焰与烟雾，被悬托在岩石之上。她头顶蓬松的头发也像火、像烟；这种毛发是一种返祖特征，她自己始终拿不准究竟该为它羞惭，还是该引以为傲。最初吸引我走向她的，正是这种史前装饰。若靠近些看，你还会发现，她背部与四肢外侧的透明皮肤上，有极淡的豹纹斑驳。我身上也有同样的斑纹；但我属于肌肉不透明的类型，我那青铜绿色的皮肤，质地也比我希望的略粗糙些。至于她——这一点我的手再清楚不过——她的皮肤柔软而丰润。</p>
<p>我看着她时，她从观察水中生物的专注中抬起脸，笑着望向我。正是这个眼神，给了我一次短暂却有着奇异意义的体验；后来在你们那个粗糙的世界里，对它的回忆曾无数次使我重新振作。并不只是因为她的脸被欢笑与温柔照亮；更因为在那一闪而过的神情中，仿佛整个人类的精神本身都在注视我。我无法让你们真正理解这一眼的力量，因为你们自己同类的脸上几乎没有这种辉光的迹象。我只能断言，在我们的物种中，面部表情远比你们发达。脸上的肌肉会对经验与情感的每一点闪动立刻作出回应，就像池水会随着每一丝风而出现千百道交错的细波。</p>
<p>此刻望着我的那张脸，与地球人的面孔截然不同：一方面，它有微妙而陌生的轮廓；另一方面，它呈暗色半透明状，隐约显出下方苍白的骨骼。它像一池被搅动、跳舞的深色暖酒，阳光在其中尽情嬉戏。但她的眼睛却像明亮的宝石，色泽可以在蓝宝石与祖母绿之间变幻出许多阶段。</p>
<p>和我们其他人一样，这个女孩脑后还生着两只额外的明亮眼睛，它们在她那古老而华丽的头发之间俏皮地闪光。也和我们所有人一样，她头顶还有一个更重要的视觉器官，我们称之为“天文眼”。平时它收拢着，与头发齐平；需要时却可以像一架粗短的望远镜那样向上伸出。她这个器官发育得极为精妙，这也决定了她的职业。我们所有人从某种意义上说都是天文学家；而她则以天文学为专业。</p>
<p>她整张脸虽然如此鲜活明亮，却与本书读者所熟悉的任何人类类型都不相同，因为其中的动物性要强烈得多。她的鼻子宽阔，有猫科动物的形状，却塑造得十分精细；丰润的嘴唇在嘴角处极富表情；她的耳朵则像狮子的耳朵一样，在发间灵活转动。</p>
<p>你们会说：古怪，非人！不。和这张脸相比，你们那些最负盛名的美人，那些不透明而迟钝的面孔，简直不过是泥团，或昆虫的面具。</p>
<p>然而，你们确实不可能像我当时那样看见她。因为在她的目光中，仿佛既表现出了我们整个种族已经取得的一切成就，也表现出了对即将降临的悲剧的全部认识。与此同时，那眼神中又有一种略带调皮的虔敬，既投向她方才观察的那些微小而简单的生命，也似乎同样投向群星，以及人类本身。</p>
<p>她微笑着对我说话——如果可以把从她脑中侵入我脑中的心灵感应影响称作“说话”的话。</p>
<p>为了让你们理解她话里的意思，我必须先解释，我们海王星的这些岩池里生活着三种来源截然不同的生命。第一种很少见。在某个受到遮蔽的石缝中，你也许会遇到一层模糊的绿色黏泥。这是海王星原生生命唯一的遗迹；它们早已被后来入侵的生命彻底压倒。第二种在我们的世界里远比其他种类常见。我们绝大多数现存生命类型，都是少数动物和植物的成功后裔；差不多在我的时代之前十亿年，也就是在你们所谓“现在”之后将近同样漫长的时间里，人类有意或无意地把这些生命从金星带到了海王星。第三，即使在这些小峡湾里，也还生活着一些远古人类本身的后裔。这些与我所属人类物种有极遥远亲缘关系的生物，当然已经退化得不可思议。它们中的大多数早已完全看不出人类模样；但其中有一种，我们语言中的名称可以译作“小人（Homunculus）”，大自然竟在它身上造出了一幅微小而精致的人类讽刺画。两只向外撇开的脚把它黏在岩石上；脚上竖着一个鼓胀的球状腹部，腹部顶端长着一张仰起的脸。那张令人不适地像人的嘴不断一开一合；眼睛只剩两道皱纹；鼻子像一对张得很开的喇叭。耳朵虽然已经聋了，却仍能活动，变成两把宽阔摆动的扇子，把水流导向嘴巴。每只耳朵下方还有一个疣状的小突起，那就是人类手臂与手掌所剩下的全部痕迹。</p>
<p>其中一个退化的人——也就是你们自己这种生物的堕落后裔——吸引了我的同伴。她笑着说：“小人儿闻到它伴侣的气味了，可它的脚还黏在石头上，没法过去找她。站了整整一个月不动之后再启程，对它来说这可真是一场朝圣！”</p>
<p>她又低头看去，忽然叫道：“快！过来看！它挣开了，挪了一英寸。瞧它摇摇摆摆，简直在拼命狂奔！现在它追上她了，她也愿意。”停了一会儿，她又感叹道：“这个水潭真是一个完整的世界！就像你马上要一头扎进去的那个世界。”</p>
<p>随后她抬头望向那颗凶烈的星，脸上的光忽然变了，冷了下来。她变得像你们的埃及狮身人面像，越过沙漠凝望、等待着某件未知而可怖、却又注定要到来的事情——那既定的终局。</p>
<p>忽然，她又笑起来，一跃而起，沿着岩缘跑向海边，纵身跳了下去。我跟在后面。整个上午剩下的时间，我们都在游泳：有时远远游到海湾中央，有时穿梭在小岛与峡湾之间；有时彼此追逐，钻过海底的天然石拱；有时则攀住一丛沉没在水下的海草，观看海床上正在上演的某场小小戏剧。</p>
<p>终于，太阳升得很高，我们回到先前睡过的草地，从飞行服的口袋里取出一顿营养丰富的“太阳制品”。其中一部分是在木星的光合作用站生产的，另一部分来自天王星上的殖民地；但其中真正的珍馐美味，则是我们亲手从自己的果园和花园中采来的。</p>
<p>吃过以后，我们仰躺在草地上，谈论大事，也谈论小事。</p>
<p>我向她挑衅道：“这是我们所有结合里最好的一次。”</p>
<p>“是啊，”她回答，“因为最短吗？因为我们在彼此分离时经历得最丰富？也许还因为那颗星。”</p>
<p>我得意地说：“尽管你有那么多情人，你还是会回到我这里。尽管有你的老虎、你的公牛，还有那些被你宠着、任你挥霍自己的哈巴狗情人，你还是会回来。”</p>
<p>“是啊，老蟒蛇，我回到你这里来了，而且正因为那番挥霍，我反而变得更丰富。你不也一样？尽管一路摘下又丢掉那么多报春花、紫罗兰、俗艳的玫瑰和香得过分的百合；流浪了一千年以后，你还不是又回到了我这里。”</p>
<p>“我会一次又一次回来——只要我能从地球人那里全身而退，也只要那颗星允许。”</p>
<p>我再说一遍，我们这番谈话是通过心灵感应进行的。如果我要把那个下午我们躺在阳光下、两颗心灵之间传递的一切都记录下来，那么单是这些内容就足以写成一本书；因为心灵感应的交流远比有声语言迅速，也远比它精细。我们谈了各种各样的题目：从她的眼睛与我那双深红色眼睛的不同，一直谈到数千年前我们曾经共同经历、而马上又要再次经历的“种族心灵”苏醒。我们也谈到各自所属的婚姻群体，以及我们这种处在各自群体之外、奇异而不规则、却似乎又长久不变的结合。我们谈到我们的儿子——那个本性令人困惑却又可爱的孩子，上一次见面时，她获准由我使她怀上了他。我们也谈到她在一座大型天文台里的工作。她和其他天文学家一道，正试图在银河系某个遥远区域寻找一处可能的家园，好让人类的生命种子在我们毁灭以前被播撒到群星之间。我们还谈到我的工作：我是那一百万名专家之一，我们试图通过直接参与人类的过去，把对整个人类往昔的探索彻底完成。当然，我们也不可避免地谈到狂星，谈到我们世界即将来临的毁灭；还谈到，如果那时我们两人都还活着，我们将彼此紧紧相依。</p>
<p>日落之前，我们翻过岩石，向内陆跑去，越过铺满鲜花的草地。但最后，我们又回到那处草丛中的小窝。吃过晚饭，夜色降临，我们忽然想唱歌，于是有时合唱，有时轮流独唱。有时我们选择最新、最狂放、在节奏与旋律上最闪耀的作品；有时唱她民族或我民族的古老歌曲；有时则唱我们这些探险者从远古民族和已经灭绝的世界中发现的粗朴歌谣。尤其是，我教了她一首由我自己发现的地球歌曲。我们费了很大力气，才把那些生涩的音节发出来，也费了很大力气才抓住它的节奏。我们同样艰难地在想象中唤起孕育这首歌的那片黑暗混乱：人与人之间的残酷，以及对更好世界的渴望。本书的一些读者也许知道这首歌。它的名字叫《老人河》（Old Man River）。</p>
<p>我们还在琢磨这段古老旋律时，晚霞的颜色已经退去，海王星人在夜里所见的天空，渐渐变成深不可测的蔚蓝。星辰开始出现，起初零星而微弱，随后成群涌现，组成明亮而有形的星座。辽阔天穹为它们所闪耀。银河从一个地平线横跨到另一个地平线，在我们眼中并不是一条淡淡的云带，而是一支数量多到不可思议的光之大军。这里那里，我们的天文眼甚至能看见那些雾状的小点，而它们实际上是遥远的宇宙。此刻，我们的歌声已低成无词的轻吟；因为夜空这个相对简单、却意义压倒一切的视觉景象，对我们所有人都有一种在你们看来也许近乎夸张的支配力。可以说，它是我们全部思想与感受的可见缩影。对我们而言，它的力量甚至超过你们最受崇敬的宗教象征。它不但搅动我们存在的表层，也触及祖先留下的最深处；与此同时，它又和我们理智所进行的最现代冒险息息相关。</p>
<p>就在我们仍用声音和精神向这庄严、永远看不厌的景象致敬时，狂星再次升起，把冰冷的钢铁般光芒撒满海面。我们沉默下来，只是望着它。那可怕的辉光把一个个星座都淹没了。</p>
<p>我不知道，究竟是我们刚才品味的那些野蛮古调在作祟，还是我的天性中本来就有一个缺口；总之，我忽然听见自己喊了出来：“太丑恶了！像我们这样的世界竟然要被烧掉、被浪费掉——这样一个由精神和甜美肉身组成的世界！”</p>
<p>她猛地看进我的眼睛，惊讶的笑意勉强压住了不安。但在她开口之前，我已经重新看清了事情。</p>
<p>“不，”我说，“不是丑恶，而是可怕。真奇怪，我们的思想竟会有那么一瞬间滑回那些糟糕的旧路上去，仿佛我们突然看不见那种宏大的美，变得和过去那些盲目的灵魂一样。”</p>
<p>她说：“等终局真正开始时，我们还能看见那种宏大的美吗？连在火焰里，也能看见吗？”</p>
<p>我说：“谁知道呢？但此刻我们看得见。”</p>
<p>我们沉默地看着狂星越升越高，紫光与紫外辐射也愈发辉煌。最后，我们一同躺回那处被草丛遮蔽的小窝，亲密地享受彼此。</p>
<p>黎明时我们起身。短暂游了一会儿后，我们穿上飞行服。那是一种连体服，脚底、手掌以及身体整个正面都密布着微小的亚原子能量源。在我们的世界里，所有短途飞行都靠它完成。操纵这种飞行需要相当技巧，也需要一定的肌肉用力，因此飞行本身就是一种乐趣。我们并肩向空中攀升，直到脚下的海岸看起来像一张地图。随后我们朝内陆飞去，先越过大片裸岩、草原、沼泽和灌木地，再越过谷物田与果园；无数住宅星星点点散布其间。有一次，我们从一座大型建筑附近经过，那些水晶般的峭壁高高矗立在我们上方，檐缘堆着积雪。一团白云遮住了建筑上部，只有一根纤细尖塔从云中伸出，踮着脚尖探进阳光里。时而会有飞行艇从墙体上脱离，或从云层中钻出。随着我们越过人口更稠密的地区，私人住宅和小屋分布得也稍稍密集一些；更多飞行艇持续不断地从我们头顶掠过，沿各个方向贯穿地平线，速度快得像成群飞射的昆虫。我们还遇见许多和我们一样单独飞行的人。更多的人在我们下方，从一座住宅飞到另一座住宅，掠过中间的耕地。在某处，一条海湾横在航线上；我们看见一艘长达五英里的以太船正在进港，它刚从木星或天王星返回，载着一船食品。那是一条由不锈金属构成的巨鱼，背部和侧腹布满舷窗。水下隐藏的部分当然也有窗；因为这个看似海洋怪兽的东西，可以像鸬鹚一样从水中升起，在一片巨大的翻腾浪花中脱离海面，几分钟后便发现自己上下左右都被群星包围。</p>
<p>正午时，我们到达另一片荒野，在一群我只能称之为“海王星兔子”的生物洞穴附近吃饭。这些毛茸茸的东西，是某个已经灭绝的人类物种的后裔；和我们世界上的许多动物一样，它们的形态主要由重力决定。为了支撑身体，它们不但使用短而粗壮的四肢，还把皮革般厚实的腹部与尾巴一同压在地面上。我们刚坐下来吃东西，这些哺乳动物似的蜥蜴就全围了上来，希望讨到一点残羹。其中一只竟然叼走了一整条面包；不过它还没来得及钻回洞里，我就把它捉住，狠狠教训了一顿。从那以后，整群家伙对我们都恭敬多了。</p>
<p>吃完以后，我们便彼此告别。这是一场既轻快又庄重的分别；因为我们知道，也许要过几千年才能再次相见，甚至可能直到太阳已经被狂星感染，开始毁灭我们深爱的世界时，才会再见。我们长久地注视着彼此的眼睛，然后重新扣好飞行服，朝相反方向升入天空。第二天黎明以前，我们必须抵达各自指定的岗位；那些岗位分散在这颗巨大行星的各处。我们要在那里共同参加“种族觉醒”——那场崇高的事件，行星上的每一个成年人都必须贡献自己的一份力量。</p>

<h2>i. HOLIDAY ON NEPTUNE</h2>
<p>WHEN I AM in your world and your epoch I remember often a certain lonely place in my own world, and in the time that I call present. It is a comer where the land juts out into the sea as a confusion of split rocks, like a herd of monsters crowding into the water. Subterranean forces acting at this point once buckled the planet’s crust into a mountain; but it was immediately tom and shattered by gravity, that implacable djin of all great worlds. Nothing is now left of it but these rocks. On Neptune we have no mountains, and the oceans are waveless. The stout sphere holds its watery cloak so tightly to it that even the most violent hurricanes fail to raise more than a ripple.</p>
<p>Scattered among these rocks lies a network of tiny fjords, whose walls and floors are embossed with variegated life. There you may see beneath the crystal water all manner of blobs and knobs and brilliant whorls, all manner of gaudy flowers, that search with their petals, or rhythmically smack their lips, all manner of clotted sea-weeds, green, brown, purple or crimson, from whose depths sometimes a claw reaches after a drowsing sprat, while here and there a worm, fringed with legs, emerges to explore the sandy sunlit bottom.</p>
<p>Among these rocks and fjords I spent my last day of leisure before setting out on one of those lengthy explorations of the past which have made me almost as familiar with your world as with my own. It is my task to tell you of your own race as it appears through the eyes of the far future; but first I must help you to reconstruct in imagination something of the future itself, and of the world from which we regard you. This I can best achieve by describing, first that day of delight, spent where the broken mountain sprawls into the sea, and then a more august event, namely the brief awakening of the Racial Mind, which was appointed for the exaltation of the explorers upon the eve of their departure into the obscure recesses of past aeons. Finally I shall tell you something of my own upbringing and career.</p>
<p>Almost the first moments of that day of recreation afforded me one of those pictures which haunt the memory ever after. The sun had risen over a burning ocean. He was not, as you might expect in our remote world, a small and feeble sun; for between your age and ours a collision had increased his bulk and splendour to a magnitude somewhat greater than that with which you are familiar.</p>
<p>Overhead the sky was blue. But for Neptunian eyes its deep azure was infused with another unique primary colour, which your vision could not have detected. Toward the sunrise, this tincture of the zenith gave place to green, gold, fire-red, purple, and yet another of the hues which elude the primitive eye. Opposite there lay darkness. But low in the darkness gleamed something which you would have taken for a very distant snowy horn, whose base was lost in night, though its crest glowed orange in the morning. A second glance would have revealed it as too precipitous and too geometrical for any mountain. It was in fact one of our great public buildings, many scores of miles distant, and nearly one score in height. In a world where mountains are crushed by their own weight these towering edifices could not stand, were it not for their incredibly rigid materials, wherein artificial atoms play the chief part. The huge crag of masonry now visible was relatively new, but it could compare in age with the younger of your terrestrial mountains.</p>
<p>The shadowed sides of its buttresses and gables, and also the shadowed faces of the near rocks and of every stone, glowed with a purple bloom, the light from a blinding violet star. This portent we call the Mad Star. It is a unique heavenly body, whose energies are being squandered with inconceivable haste, so that it will soon be burnt out. Meanwhile it is already infecting its neighbours with its plague. In a few thousand years our own sun will inevitably run amok in the same manner, and turn all his planets to white-hot gas. But at present, I mean in the age which I call present, the Mad Star is only a brilliant feature of our night sky.</p>
<p>On the morning of which I am speaking there lay full length on the brink of a little cliff, and gazing into the pool beneath her, a woman of my world. To me she is lovely, exquisite, the very embodiment of beauty; to you she would seem a strange half-human monster. To me, as she lay there with her breasts against the rock and one arm reaching down into the water, her whole form expressed the lightness and suppleness of a panther. To you she would have seemed unwieldy, elephantine, and grotesque in every feature. Yet if you were to see her moving in her own world, you would know, I think, why her name in our speech is the equivalent of Panther in yours.</p>
<p>If you or any of your kind were to visit our world, and if by miracle you were to survive for a few moments in our alien atmosphere, gravity would make it almost impossible for you to support yourselves at all. But we, since our bones, like our buildings, are formed largely of artificial atoms, and are far more rigid than steel, since moreover our muscle cells have been most cunningly designed, can run and jump with ease. It is true, however, that in spite of our splendid tissues we have to be more solidly built than the Terrestrials, whose limbs remind us unpleasantly of insects.</p>
<p>The woman on the rock would certainly have surprised you, for she is a member of one of our most recent generations, whose skin and flesh are darkly translucent. Seeing her there, with the sunlight drenching her limbs, you might have taken her for a statue, cut from some wine-dark alabaster, or from carbuncle; save that, with every movement of her arm, sunken gleams of crimson, topaz, and gold-brown rippled the inner night of her shoulder and flank. Her whole substance, within its lovely curves and planes, looked scarcely solid, but rather a volume of obscure flame and smoke poised on the rock. On her head a mass of hair, flame-like, smoke-like, was a reversion to the primitive in respect of which she could never decide whether it was a thing for shame or complacency. It was this pre-historic decoration which first drew me toward her. In a closer view you would have noticed that on her back and the outer sides of her limbs the skin’s translucency was complicated by a very faint leopard-like mottling. I also bear that mottling; but I am of the sort whose flesh is opaque, and my bronze-green skin is of a texture somewhat harsher than I should choose. In her, how well I know it, the skin is soft and rich to the exploring hand.</p>
<p>While I watched her, she raised her face from studying the water-dwellers, and looked at me, laughing. It was that look which gave me the brief but strangely significant experience the memory of which was to refresh me so often in your uncouth world. It was not only that her face was lit up with merriment and tenderness; but in that fleeting expression the very spirit of humanity seemed to regard me. I cannot make you realize the potency of that glance, for the faces of your own kind afford almost no hint of such illumination. I can only assert that in our species, facial expression is more developed than in yours. The facial muscles respond to every changing flicker of experience and emotion, as pools respond to every breath of wind with a thousand criss-cross rippling tremors.</p>
<p>The face that now looked at me was unlike terrestrial countenances both in its subtly alien contours and in its dark translucency, which half-revealed the underlying paleness of bone. It was like a stirred and dancing pool of dark but warm-tinted wine, in which the sunlight revelled. But the eyes were bright jewels capable of many phases from sapphire to emerald.</p>
<p>Like others of our kind, this girl bore two additional bright eyes in the back of her head. They sparkled quaintly in the archaic glory of her hair. Like the rest of us, she had on her crown yet another and more important organ of vision which we call the astronomical eye. Normally sunken level with her hair, it could at will be projected upwards like a squat telescope. The exquisite development of this organ in her had determined her career. All of us are in a manner astronomers; but she is an astronomer by profession.</p>
<p>Her whole face, though so brilliantly alive, was unlike any human type known to readers of this book, since it was so much more animal. Her nose was broad and feline, but delicately moulded. Her full lips were subtle at the corners. Her ears moved among her locks like the ears of a lion.</p>
<p>Grotesque, you say, inhuman! No! Beside this the opaque and sluggish faces of your proudest beauties are little better than lumps of clay, or the masks of insects.</p>
<p>But indeed it is impossible for you to see her as I saw her then. For in that look there seemed to find expression the whole achievement of our race, and the full knowledge of its impending tragedy. At the same time there was in it a half-mischievous piety toward the little simple creatures that she had been watching, and equally, it seemed, toward the stars and man himself.</p>
<p>Smiling, she now spoke to me; if I may call ‘speech’ the telepathic influence which invaded my brain from hers.</p>
<p>That you may understand the significance of her words, I must explain that in our Neptunian rock-pools there are living things of three very different stocks. The first is rare. One may encounter in some sheltered cranny a vague greenish slime. This is the only relic of primeval Neptunian life, long since outclassed by invaders. The second stock is by far the commonest throughout our world. Nearly all our living types are triumphant descendants of the few animals and plants which men brought, deliberately or by accident, to Neptune from Venus, nearly a thousand million years before my day, and almost as long after the age that you call present. Third, there are also, even in these little fjords, a few descendants of those ancient men themselves. These very remote cousins of my own human species are, of course, fantastically degenerate. Most have long ago ceased to be recognizably human; but in one, whose name in our language you might translate ‘Homunculus’, nature has achieved a minute and exquisite caricature of humanity. Two splay feet glue him to the rock. From these rises an erect and bulbous belly, wearing on its summit an upturned face. The unpleasantly human mouth keeps opening and shutting. The eyes are mere wrinkles, the nose a wide double trumpet. The ears, deaf but mobile, have become two broad waving fans, that direct a current of water toward the mouth. Beneath each ear is a little wart-like excrescence, all that is left of the human arm and hand.</p>
<p>One of these degenerate human beings, one of these fallen descendants of your own kind, had attracted my companion’s attention. Laughing, she said, ‘Little Homunculus has got wind of his mate, and he can’t unstick his feet to go after her. What a pilgrimage it will be for him after a whole month of standing still!’</p>
<p>She looked down again and cried, ‘Quick! Come and see! He’s loose, he’s moved an inch. He’s waddling at breakneck speed. Now he’s got her, and she’s willing.’ After a pause she exclaimed, ‘What a world this pond is! Like the world that you are to plunge into so soon.’</p>
<p>Then she looked up at the fierce star, and the light in her face changed and chilled. She became like your Egyptian Sphinx, which looks across the desert and waits, for something unknown and terrible, but the appointed end.</p>
<p>Suddenly she laughed, sprang to her feet, ran down the rock-edge to the sea, and dived. I followed; and the rest of the morning we spent swimming, either far out in the bay or among the islets and fjords, chasing each other sometimes through submarine rock-arches, or clinging to a sunken tussock of weed to watch some drama of the sea-bottom.</p>
<p>At last, when the sun was high, we returned to the grassy place where we had slept, and took from the pockets of our flying-suits our meal of rich sun-products. Of these, some had been prepared in the photosynthesis stations on Jupiter, others came from the colonies on Uranus; but we ourselves had gathered the delicacies in our own orchards and gardens.</p>
<p>Having eaten, we lay back on the grass and talked of matters great and small.</p>
<p>I challenged her: ‘This has been the best of all our matings.’ ‘Yes,’ she answered, ‘because the shortest? Because after the richest experience in separation. And perhaps because of the Star.’</p>
<p>‘In spite of all your lovers,’ I exulted, ‘you come back to me. In spite of your tigers, your bulls, and all your lap-dog lovers, where you squander yourself.’</p>
<p>‘Yes, old python, I come back to you, the richer for that squandering. And you in spite of all the primroses and violets and blowsy roses and over-scented lilies that you have plucked and dropped, you come back to me, after your thousand years of roaming.’</p>
<p>‘Again and again I shall come back, if I escape from the Terrestrials, and if the Star permits.’</p>
<p>Our conversation, let me repeat, was telepathic. If I were to report all that passed from mind to mind as we lay in the sun that afternoon, I should fill a book; for telepathic communication is incomparably swifter and more subtle than vocal speech. We ranged over all manner of subjects, from the difference between her eyes and mine, which are crimson, to the awakening of the Racial Mind, which we had experienced some thousands of years earlier, and were so soon to experience again. We talked also about the marriage groups to which we severally belonged, and of our own strange irregular yet seemingly permanent union outside our respective groups. We talked about the perplexing but lovely nature of our son, whom she had been allowed to conceive at our last meeting. We spoke also of her work in one of the great observatories. With other astronomers she was trying to discover in some distant region of our galaxy a possible home for the human seed which, it was hoped, would be scattered among the stars before man’s destruction. We spoke also of the work on which I am engaged as one of the million specialists who are trying to complete the exploration of the human past by direct participation in it. Inevitably we spoke also of the Star, and of the coming destruction of our world; and of how, if we were both alive in that age we would cling together.</p>
<p>Before sundown we clambered over the rocks and ran inland over the flower-strewn turf. But at last we returned to our grassy nest; and after supper, when darkness had fallen, we were moved to sing, together or each in turn. Sometimes our choice fell upon the latest, wildest, scintillations of rhythm and melody, sometimes on the old songs of her nation or mine, sometimes on the crude chants which we explorers had discovered among the ancient peoples and in extinct worlds. In particular I taught her a Terrestrial song which I myself had found. With difficulty we formed the uncouth syllables and caught the lilt. With difficulty we called up in imagination the dark confusion that gave it birth, the harshness of man to man, and the yearning for a better world. Readers of this book may know the song. Old Man River is its name.</p>
<p>While we were still striving with this archaic melody, the colours of the sunset gave place to the profound azure which the Neptunian sky assumes by night for Neptunian eyes. Stars began to appear, first singly and faintly, then in companies and brightly patterned constellations. The wide heaven flashed with them. The Milky Way, flung from horizon to horizon, revealed itself to us, not as a pale cloud-zone, but as an incredibly multitudinous host of lights. Here and there our astronomical eyes could detect even those misty points which are in fact the remote universes. Our singing was now hushed into a murmuring and wordless chant; for this comparatively simple yet overwhelmingly significant percept of the night sky commands us all with a power which might seem to you extravagant. It is, as it were, the visible epitome of our whole thought and feeling. It has for us more than the potency of your most venerated religious symbols. It stirs not only the surface of our being but the ancestral depths, and yet it is relevant to the most modern ventures of our intellect.</p>
<p>While we were still saluting with our voices and our spirits this august and never-too-familiar presentation, the Mad Star rose once more, and scattered its cold steel across the sea. We fell silent, watching. Its dread effulgence extinguished the constellations.</p>
<p>Whether it was the influence of those barbarian melodies that we had been savouring, or a mere flaw in my nature, I know not; but suddenly I heard myself cry out. Hideous! That such a world as ours should be burnt, wasted such a world of spirit and sweet flesh!’</p>
<p>She looked quickly in my eyes, with amazed laughter thrusting down dismay. But before she could speak I saw rightly again. ‘No!’ I said, not hideous, but terrible. Strange, how our thoughts can slip back for a moment into the bad old ways, as though we were to lose sight of the great beauty, and be like the blind spirits of the past.’</p>
<p>And she, ‘When the end begins, shall we still see the great beauty? Shall we see it even in the fire?’</p>
<p>And I, ‘Who knows? But we see it now.’</p>
<p>Silently we watched the Star climb with increasing splendour of violet and ultra-violet illumination. But at last we lay down together in our sheltered grassy nest, and took intimate delight in one another.</p>
<p>At dawn we rose. After a short swim, we put on our flying-suits, those overalls studded with minute sources of sub-atomic energy on the soles of the feet, the palms of the hands, and the whole front surface of the body. It is with these that all lesser flights are performed in our world; and as the action entails much skill and some muscular exertion, it is a delight in itself. Side by side we climbed the air, until the coast was like a map beneath us. We headed inland, first over a wide tract of rock and prairie, marsh and scrub, then over corn and orchard, sprinkled with innumerable homes. Once we passed near a great building, whose crystal precipices towered over us with snow on their cornices. A white cloud covered its upper parts, save for one slender pinnacle, which tiptoed into the sunlight. Sometimes a flying-boat would detach itself from the walls or emerge from the cloud. As we travelled over more densely inhabited regions, sprinkled somewhat more closely with private houses and cottages, a more numerous swarm of these flying-boats continually passed over our heads in all directions from horizon to horizon at so great a speed that they seemed darting insects. We encountered also many fliers like ourselves. Many we saw beneath us, moving from house to house across the intervening tillage. At one point an arm of the sea lay across our route; and there we saw, entering dock, a five-mile long ether-ship, lately returned from Jupiter or Uranus with a cargo of foodstuffs. She was a great fish of stainless metal, studded with windows over her back and flanks. Hidden under water also there would be windows; for this seemingly marine monster could rise like cormorant, with a great churning of the ocean, to find herself within a few minutes surrounded above and below by stars.</p>
<p>At noon we reached another wild district, and took our meal near a warren of creatures which I can best describe as Neptunian rabbits. The form of these furry descendants of an extinct human species was chiefly determined, as so often with our fauna, by gravity. For support they use, not only their short thick legs, but also their leathern bellies and tails. No sooner had we sat down to eat than these mammalian lizards crowded round us in the hope of titbits, One of them actually made off with a loaf; but before he could reach his earth I captured him, and administered such punishment that henceforth the whole tribe treated us with more respect.</p>
<p>When our meal was over, we took leave of one another. It was a gay, grave parting; for we knew that we might not meet again for thousands of years, perhaps not before the sun, infected by the Mad Star, should already have begun the destruction of our beloved world. When we had looked in one another’s eyes for a long moment, we buckled on our flying-suits once more; then, in opposite directions, we climbed the air. By dawn next day we had to be at our appointed stations, far asunder on the great planet’s surface, to participate in the Racial Awakening, the sublime event to which every adult person on the planet must needs contribute.</p>
