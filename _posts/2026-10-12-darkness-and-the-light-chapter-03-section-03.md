<h2>三、俄罗斯与中国</h2>
<p>如今，全世界只剩下一个强国尚未落入俄罗斯掌握，而它潜在地恰恰是所有强国中最强大的——中国。正是在俄中关系上，我的经验第一次明显出现分歧，人类两条平行历史由此浮现。既然这两个伟大民族在后面的故事中占据如此重要的位置，我必须先停下来谈谈塑造它们的力量。</p>
<p>列宁领导的第一次俄国革命，主要是一种摸索着前进、却真诚的“真正共同体”意志的表达，同时也是对残酷而低效的统治阶级的一次复仇。革命领导者建立权力以后，着手重塑俄罗斯整个经济，使其服务于劳动者。然而外国敌意迫使他们为了军事需要牺牲很多东西。人口的物质繁荣受到损害，精神繁荣也一样。原本本应形成一个由自由探究、善于批判并负责任的心灵构成的社会，最终却变成思想上整齐列队的人口，容易陷入群体狂热，并蔑视一切非正统观念。危险有利于单人独裁，也有利于一个纪律森严、军事化政党的统治。追求真正共同体的意志于是越来越容易退化为要求全体在兽群中保持一致、并战胜兽群敌人的激情。</p>
<p>很长一段时间——也许数十年，也许几百年——俄国内部光明与黑暗的斗争反复摇摆。有时看起来，为了教育上的自由进步，纪律终于会有所放松；可是很快，真实或虚构的外国威胁，或者某种国内冲突的危险，就又成为强化暴政的借口。成千上万官员被枪决，军队和工厂被“清洗”掉所谓不忠者，教育中一切鼓励批判思考的倾向也被清除。</p>
<p>如今为争夺整个行星控制权而彼此竞争的两个军事政权，在许多方面很相似。两者都由一个少数集团掌握整个社会的实际权力，同时又由一个个人既充当这一权力的工具，又成为权力的操纵者。两种独裁都对臣民施加严格纪律，也都灌输一种高度定型的意识形态；尽管双方都极力强调彼此差异，这两套意识形态至少有一点完全一致：它们都要求个人绝对服从国家。然而在两国民众中，仍然普遍存在一种信念，认为社会计划最终应以所有个人的充实生活为目标。</p>
<p>这两个世界强权之间也存在巨大差异。俄罗斯更早走上这条道路，并在很大程度上利用欧洲文明的精神破产取得胜利。俄罗斯文化本身当然也是欧洲文明的产物，可俄罗斯民族相对而言并未被高度文明化，因此要摆脱一套浅浅覆盖在自己身上的外来意识形态，并不十分困难。中国则不同。它拥有地球上最古老的文明，而且比任何文明都更保守。再者，俄罗斯人过去反抗的是一个虽然衰朽、却仍部分文明化的欧洲；他们内心始终被欧洲的文化成就压得有些自惭。而中国人当年反抗的，是他们眼中的暴发户与蛮族——日本人。因此，与俄国人相比，中国人更清楚地意识到自己争取的不仅是社会正义，也是文明本身，是文化以及传统的延续。</p>
<p>无论中国传统有多少缺陷，它至少在一个方面间接地产生了巨大价值。无论贫富，对家庭的崇奉贯穿中国历史，并且甚至熬过了现代革命时期。这种崇奉、这种近乎执念的家庭观念，在很多方面当然是保守力量，但它也有两个好处：它阻止了人口下降；更重要的是，也阻止了智力水平下降。在中国和别处一样，较聪明的人往往会上升到生活更舒适的阶层。但欧洲与美洲的富裕阶层没有充分繁殖，而中国根深蒂固的家族观却确保富裕者仍会生育。革命后的中国，于是可以在旧日的家庭之爱上嫁接一种新的、从生物学角度得到辩护的尊重——不只是尊重“家庭”本身，也尊重那些表现出更高智力或更强社会情感的血统。可惜，公众舆论虽然一度向这个方向发展，旧有金融统治家族却看到自己的支配地位会受到新兴血统挑战，于是动用全部宣传与压迫力量，消灭这种把旧传统重新解释后的“异端”。因此，尽管整个中国帝国的智力资源比俄罗斯更丰富，它仍严重浪费了最宝贵的社会资产。后来，我还会讲到统治阶层的反动政策怎样差点让这个伟大民族彻底丧失心智能力。</p>
<p>在社会组织方面，帝国俄罗斯与帝国中国也有差异。在俄罗斯，那场建立共产主义国家的英雄尝试最终因为共产党自身的道德衰败而走入歧途。一个起初充满献身精神的革命团体，逐步发展成一种实际上占有整个帝国财富的官僚集团。共同所有制理论上仍存在，实际上却只存在于党内；于是这个党变成一种富得难以想象的修道会。在早期，入党要经过严格的社会与道德考验；到了后期，世袭原则却悄悄混了进来，政党遂成为排他的统治种姓。中国在俄罗斯共产主义与欧洲资本主义的双重影响下，也形成类似体系，但统治种姓的“共同所有”又因各大家族拥有高度经济自治而复杂化。正如更早阶段的日本，只是更彻底、更明确，中国每一个重要生产部门都成为某个贵族——更准确说，是财阀——家族的特权领域；但在每个家族内部，共同所有制又被严格维持。</p>
<p>两个民族的气质也存在深刻差异。俄罗斯革命者虽然以自己的唯物主义自豪，俄罗斯人民却一直保有强烈而不自知的神秘主义倾向。他们对列宁的崇敬，以克里姆林宫中防腐保存的遗体为中心，起初不过是对新秩序奠基者的朴素尊敬；可是渐渐地，它获得了一种列宁本人若在世必定会斥责并嘲笑的宗教性质。辩证唯物主义的术语被越来越奇异地重新解释，以至普通人能够把“物质”想象成某种神灵，把马克思视为至高先知，把列宁视为神本身在尘世的化身。马克思体系的本意是科学性的，声称自己是理智自由地处理社会生活材料所得的产物。不过早期马克思主义者也曾完全正确地指出，理性绝不是不会出错的向导；它本身也会受到社会原因影响，并通过个人的情感需要表现出来。这个健全的心理学原则后来却变成一条神圣教条；在俄罗斯帝国力量达到巅峰时，对理性的拒斥已经像昔日纳粹德国一样彻底而迷信。人们完全可以一面接受马克思全部社会与哲学理论，一面沉溺于各种神秘主义幻想。</p>
<p>在这方面，中国人与俄罗斯人非常不同。无论古代中国的真实情况如何，摆脱日本统治后的中国，对于经验的神秘主义维度兴趣很少。那个时代的中国人把常识看作最高标准。哪怕面对科学——对许多俄罗斯人来说，科学几乎已经变成一种宗教——中国人仍保持常识态度。在他们看来，科学不是福音，而是一套极其有用的准则，可以用来获得舒适或者力量。受教育的俄罗斯人若谈起唯物主义科学深远的哲学意义，受教育的中国人大多只会微笑耸肩。奇怪的是，狂热的唯物主义者反而更沉迷形而上学推测与神秘幻想，而那个不爱思辨、信奉常识的人，在这一点上反而更能对现实深处不可知的奥秘保持一种虔敬。</p>
<p>新中国的文化常被认为在精神上带有“十八世纪”气质；而在最好的时候，它又包含一种默默的、直觉性的敬畏，面对包围着人类存在的神秘。即使在同日本进行惨烈斗争之后，受教育的中国人仍保留某种十八世纪风度：旧式的温雅，以及对体面与秩序的偏好。对学问的传统尊重也保留下来，只是新政府官员所需掌握的“学问”已经与早年不同。旧时代，唯一要求只是熟读经典；如今，候选者必须同样细致地掌握物理学、生物学、心理学、经济学与社会科学知识。无论新中国还是旧中国，知识阶层最高的兴趣都不是像希腊人那样的理论，不是像犹太人那样的宗教，不是像印度人那样的神秘体验，也不是像欧洲人那样的科学与工业，而是社会。对他们而言，如同对那些仍受尊敬的祖先一样，压倒一切的问题始终是：人究竟应当怎样共同生活。</p>
<p>若要理解这个时代中国的社会思想——一方面强调自由，另一方面又强调为了共同任务而自我约束——就必须记住对日战争留下的影响。战争开始时，中国人还深深分裂，日本人正是利用这种不和获利；但入侵使他们团结起来，而且出乎全世界意料，他们表现出巨大的组织才华与献身精神，重建整个经济体系以抵抗无情的敌人。虽然军队一路向内陆撤退，他们却在西部设法创造一个新中国。大型工厂在那里建立，庞大的大学也在那里诞生。新中国的青年男女就是在那里开始相信：他们民族负有一种使命，要把世界从暴政中解放出来，并建立一种结合古代与现代优点的世界文明。</p>
<p>在抗日战争第一阶段，在那种新的民族意识——同时也是一种新的人类意识——形成时，国家全部资源和人民全部精力都集中于防御。必须购买或制造武器，必须组建军队；而新士兵还必须接受政治训练，使每个人不仅是高效战士，也是新思想向外辐射的中心。军事教育和民用教育都成为国家最主要的事务。在若干杰出头脑影响下，一种“古老而崭新”的文化逐渐显出轮廓。它以古代中国伦理为根基，又同时受到基督教、欧洲民主、欧洲科学以及俄罗斯共产主义的影响；然而作为整体，它又从头到尾都是新的。</p>
<p>遗憾的是，尽管鼓舞新中国的思想包括共同服务、共同牺牲与共同所有，中国社会结构仍有一部分保持资本主义性质。在战争压力下，商业与金融寡头确实作出了许多牺牲——有些自愿，有些被迫——但他们仍设法保住作为人民代表政权、后来又是独裁者背后实际权力的地位。在危险最严重的时期，这种力量秘密运作，甚至同日本的类似势力暗中勾结。后来形势逆转，日本军队不是被包围就是向沿海溃退，国家危机的借口已经不够迫切，再也无法压制或掩饰金融势力恢复自身地位的努力。剧烈的内部紧张之后爆发内战。稻田平原再次被军队与坦克踩过，铁路被破坏，城市遭轰炸，残酷屠杀则分别以自由、正义或安全的名义进行。</p>
<p>战争的结果是北方由共产主义者获胜，南方由资本主义者获胜。两国一度维持独立，并不断彼此阴谋。北方当然高度依赖俄罗斯援助；而俄罗斯此时正胜利地扩张到整个欧洲，因此看起来华南很快就会屈服。然而俄罗斯虽然已是世界最强大的军事力量，却早已不再具有革命性和感召力。共产主义术语仍被官方沿用，其精神却已经消失——正如更早时代，自由民主的话语也曾被用来为资本主义剥削辩护。于是南方领导人能够依靠热烈的中国民族主义号召，在本国乃至北方击败共产主义宣传。结果不久以后，民族主义者在北方夺取权力。随后南北两个中国政权庄严宣布合并。可怕的中国金融—军事独裁由此诞生。</p>
<p>当俄罗斯帝国忙于消化美洲与非洲时，那个正在成形的中国帝国也在整个东亚巩固自己。北面，日本、朝鲜、满洲与蒙古一个接一个纳入新帝国；南面，阿萨姆、暹罗、缅甸和东印度群岛也接连被吞并。西藏曾经属于古代中华帝国，此时却通过让两个强大邻国相互牵制，维持着岌岌可危的独立。</p>
<p>我刚才描述的这段人类历史也许显得仿佛是黑暗意志取得胜利的时期，其实并非如此。正如我已经说过，它只不过是光明与黑暗长期保持平衡时代中的一个阶段。如今争夺全球主宰地位的两个帝国，没有一个是彻底反动的。在每一大群人民中，很大一部分人口——也许甚至是多数——仍相信友善与合乎理性的行为，也试图付诸实践。只要所需牺牲并不过分，他们甚至常常能够成功。在个人直接交往中，基督教式行为或古代中国伦理的形式——并且往往还有精神——依旧可见。即使在间接社会关系里，自由主义冲动也偶尔会取胜。此外，在两个帝国内部，都有一个积极少数群体为光明努力工作，要求实行人道行为，并传播这样一种社会秩序理念：其中所有人都有机会实现自身。事实上，双方较聪明的光明支持者都自信地期待着一场宏大而光荣的变革；即使不是近期，也会发生在他们子女有生之年。甚至统治者自己——那些控制两帝国的军事—政治集团——也真诚地相信某种使命。他们当然并不相信激进变革，却相信自己应当统治世界，并把人类带向一个模糊想象中的乌托邦：那里充满纪律与武德。此时两个帝国都还没有出现那种曾短暂主宰德国的、赤裸裸的权力欲和以残酷为乐的心理。两国统治者之间存在一种暧昧关系。双方都想通过外交或战争征服对方，也都厌恶对方传播的社会观念与社会行为；然而他们同时同意，还有一件事比对方更令人厌恶——那就是自己的国家被国内进步少数派推翻。因此，两边政策不仅取决于自身力量相对于敌人的涨落，也取决于国内进步派的强弱。他们真诚地、甚至有时颇不情愿地，以外部危险为借口加强国内纪律；然而一旦社会革命似乎即将爆发，他们又毫不犹豫地请求外部敌人暂时减轻压力。而这种请求也从不落空，因为没有任何一个统治集团愿意看到自己的对手被革命推翻。</p>

<h2>iii. RUSSIA AND CHINA</h2>
<p>ONE POWER ALONE in all the world now remained to be brought within the Russian grasp, and this was potentially the greatest power of all, namely China. It was in the relations between Russia and China that the discrepancy in my experience first became evident, and the two parallel histories of mankind emerged. Since these two great peoples bulk so largely in my story, I shall dwell for a while on the forces which had moulded them.</p>
<p>The first Russian revolution, under Lenin, had been mainly a groping but sincere expression of the will for true community, and also an act of vengeance against a cruel and inefficient master class. When the leaders of the Revolution had established their power they proceeded to remake the whole economy of Russia for the benefit of the workers. Foreign hostility, however, forced them to sacrifice much to military necessity. Not only the physical but also the mental prosperity of the population suffered. What should have become a population of freely inquiring, critical, and responsible minds became instead a mentally- regimented population, prone to mob enthusiasm and contempt for unorthodoxy. Danger favoured the dictatorship of one man and the dominance of a disciplined and militarized party. The will for true community tended more and more to degenerate into the passion for conformity within the herd and for triumph over the herd’s enemies.</p>
<p>For a long while, for many decades or possibly a few centuries, the struggle between the light and the darkness in Russia fluctuated. There were periods when it seemed that discipline would be relaxed for the sake of liberal advancement in education. But presently foreign danger, real or fictitious, or else some threat of internal conflict would become an excuse for the intensification of tyranny. Thousands of officials would be shot, the army and the factories purged of disaffected persons. Education would be cleansed of all tendency to foster critical thought.</p>
<p>The two military regimes which now vied with one another for control of the planet were in many respects alike. In each of them a minority held effective power over the whole society, and in each a single individual was at once the instrument and the wielder of that power. Each dictatorship imposed upon its subjects a strict discipline and a stereotyped ideology which, in spite of its much emphasized idiosyncracies, was in one respect at least identical with the ideology of its opponent; for both insisted on the absolute subordination of the individual to the state, yet in both peoples there was still a popular conviction that the aim of social planning should be fullness of life for all individuals.</p>
<p>Between the two world powers there were great differences. Russia had been first in the field, and had triumphed largely through the mental bankruptcy of European civilization. Though the Russian culture was itself an expression of that civilization, the Russians were relatively an uncivilized race which had found no great difficulty in breaking away from a lightly imposed alien ideology. China, on the other hand, boasted the oldest civilization of the planet, and one which was more conservative than any other. Moreover, while the Russians had asserted themselves against a decadent but partially civilized Europe, and had always been secretly overawed by Europe’s cultural achievement, the Chinese had asserted themselves against a people whom they regarded as upstarts and barbarians, the Japanese. More consciously than the Russians they had fought not only for social justice but for civilization, for culture, and the continuity of their tradition.</p>
<p>Whatever the defects of the Chinese tradition, in one respect it had been indirectly of immense value. Among both rich and poor the cult of the family had persisted throughout Chinese history, and had survived even the modern revolutionary period. In many ways this cult, this obsession, had been a reactionary influence, but in two respects it had been beneficial. It had prevented decline of population; and, more important, it had prevented a decline of intelligence. In China as elsewhere the more intelligent had tended to rise into the more comfortable circumstances. But whereas in Europe and America the more prosperous classes had failed to breed adequately, in China the inveterate cult of family ensured that they should do so. In post- revolutionary China the old love of family was a useful stock on which to graft a new biologically-justified respect not merely for family as such but for those stocks which showed superior intelligence or superior social feeling. Unfortunately, though public opinion did for a while move in this direction, the old financial ruling families, seeing their dominance threatened by upstart strains, used all their power of propaganda and oppression to stamp out this new and heretical version of the old tradition. Thus, though on the whole the Chinese Empire was richer in intelligence than the Russian, it seriously squandered its resources in this most precious social asset. And later, as I shall tell, the reactionary policy of the ruling caste threatened this great people with complete bankruptcy of mental capacity.</p>
<p>In social organization there were differences between imperial Russia and imperial China. In Russia the heroic attempt to create a communist state had finally gone astray through the moral deterioration of the Communist Party. What had started as a devoted revolutionary corps had developed as a bureaucracy which in effect owned the whole wealth of the empire. Common ownership theoretically existed, but in effect it was confined to the Party, which thus became a sort of fabulously wealthy monastic order. In its earlier phase the Party was recruited by strict social and moral testing, but latterly the hereditary principle had crept in, so that the Party became an exclusive ruling caste. In China, under the influence partly of Russian communism, partly of European capitalism, a similar system evolved, but one in which the common ownership of the ruling caste as a whole was complicated by the fact that the great families of the caste secured a large measure of economic autonomy. As in Japan at an earlier stage, but more completely and definitely, each great department of production became the perquisite of a particular aristocratic, or rather plutocratic, family. Within each family, common ownership was strictly maintained.</p>
<p>There was a deep difference of temper between the two peoples. Though the Russian revolutionaries had prided themselves on their materialism, the Russian people retained a strong though unacknowledged tendency towards mysticism. Their veneration of Lenin, which centred round his embalmed body in the Kremlin, was originally simple respect for the founder of the new order; but little by little it acquired a character which would have called from Lenin himself condemnation and ridicule. The phraseology of dialectical materialism came to be fantastically reinterpreted in such a way as to enable the populace to think of ‘matter’ as a kind of deity, with Marx as the supreme prophet and Lenin as the terrestrial incarnation of the God himself. Marx’s system was scientific in intention, and it claimed to be an expression of intelligence operating freely on the data of social life. But the early Marxists had insisted, quite rightly, that reason was no infallible guide, that it was an expression of social causes working through the individual’s emotional needs. This sound psychological principle became in time a sacred dogma, and during the height of Russian imperial power the rejection of reason was as complete and as superstitious as it had been in Nazi Germany. Men were able, while accepting all the social and philosophical theories of Marx, to indulge in all kinds of mystical fantasies.</p>
<p>In this matter the Chinese were very different from the Russians. Whatever the truth about ancient China, the China that had freed itself from Japan was little interested in the mystical aspect of experience. For the Chinese of this period common sense was absolute. Even in regard to science, which for so many Russians had become almost a religion, the Chinese maintained their common-sense attitude. Science for them was not a gospel but an extremely useful collection of precepts for gaining comfort or power. When the educated Russian spoke of the far-reaching philosophical significance of materialistic science, the educated Chinese would generally smile and shrug his shoulders. Strange that the fanatical materialist was more addicted to metaphysical speculation and mystical fantasy, and the unspeculative adherent of common sense was in this respect capable of greater piety towards the occult depth of reality.</p>
<p>The culture of the new China was often regarded as ‘Eighteenth Century’ in spirit, but at its best it included also a tacit intuitive reverence for the mystery which encloses human existence. Even after the bitter struggle against the Japanese there remained something eighteenth century about the educated Chinese, something of the old urbanity and liking for decency and order. The old respect for learning, too, remained, though the kind of learning which was now necessary to the aspiring government official was very different from that which was required in an earlier age. Then, all that was demanded was familiarity with classical texts; now, the candidate had to show an equally minute acquaintance with the lore of physics, biology, psychology, economics, and social science. In the new China as in old, the supreme interest of the intellectuals was not theoretical, as it had been with the Greeks, nor religious, as with the Jews, nor mystical, as with the Indians, nor scientific and industrial, as with the Europeans, but social. For them, as for their still-revered ancestors, the all absorbing problem was to discover and practise the right way of living together.</p>
<p>To understand the Chinese social ideas of this period with their emphasis at once on freedom and self-discipline for the common task, one must bear in mind the effects of the Japanese wars. At the outset the Chinese had been hopelessly divided against themselves, and the Japanese had profited by their discord. But invasion united them, and to the surprise of the World they showed great skill and devotion in reorganizing their whole economy to resist the ruthless enemy. Though their armies were driven inland, they contrived to create a new China in the West. There, great factories sprang up, great universities were founded. There, the young men and women of the new China learned to believe in their people’s mission to free the world from tyranny and to found a world-civilization which should combine the virtues of the ancient and the Modern.</p>
<p>During the first phase of the resistance against Japan, during the emergence of the new national consciousness which was also a new consciousness of mankind, the whole resources of the state and the whole energy of the people were concentrated on defence. Arms had to be bought or made, armies raised. And the new soldiers had to be politically trained so that each of them should be not merely an efficient fighter but also a radiating centre of the new ideas. Education, military and civilian, was one of the state’s main cares. Under the influence of a number of brilliant minds there appeared the outline of the old new culture. Based on the ethics of the ancient China, but influenced also by Christianity, by European democracy, by European science, by Russian communism, it was at the same time novel through and through.</p>
<p>Unfortunately, though the ideas that inspired the new China included common service, common sacrifice, and common ownership, the structure of Chinese society was still in part capitalist. Though under the stress of War the commercial and financial oligarchy sacrificed much, freely or under compulsion, it managed to retain its position as the effective power behind the throne of the people’s representatives, and later behind the dictator. In the period of acute danger this power had been exercised secretly, and had effected intrigues with the similar power in Japan. Later, when the tide had turned, when the Japanese armies were either surrounded or in flight to the coast, the plea of national danger was no longer sufficiently urgent to subdue or disguise the efforts of finance to re-establish itself. A period of violent internal strain was followed by a civil war. Once more the rice plains were overrun by troops and tanks, railways were destroyed, cities bombed, savage massacres perpetrated in the name of freedom or justice or security.</p>
<p>The result of the war was that Communism triumphed in the North, Capitalism in the South. For a while the two states maintained their independence, constantly intriguing against one another. The North, of course, depended largely on Russian support, and as Russia was at this time triumphantly expanding over Europe, it looked as though South China must soon succumb. But Russia, though by now the greatest military power in the world, was no longer a revolutionary and inspiring influence. The jargon of communism was still officially used, but its spirit had vanished; much as, in an earlier age, the jargon of liberal democracy was used in support of capitalist exploitation. Consequently the leaders of the South were able to defeat communist propaganda both in their own country and in the North by ardent appeals to Chinese nationalism. The result was that after a while the nationalists seized power in the North. There followed a solemn act of union between the North and South Chinese states. And thus was created the formidable Chinese financial-military dictatorship.</p>
<p>While the Russian Empire was busy digesting America and Africa, the Chinese would-be empire was consolidating itself throughout eastern Asia. In the north, Japan, Korea, Manchuria and Mongolia, in the south, Assam, Siam, Burma and the East Indies, were one by one brought within the new empire. Tibet, which had formerly been part of the ancient Chinese Empire, was able to main’ a precarious independence by playing off each of its formidable neighbours against the other.</p>
<p>The period of human history that I have been describing may seem to have been one in which the will for darkness triumphed, but in fact it was not. It was merely as I have said, a phase in the long age of balance between the light and the dark. Neither of the two empires that now competed for mastery over the planet was wholly reactionary. In each great group of peoples a large part of the population, perhaps the majority, still believed in friendliness and reasonableness, and tried to practice them. When the sacrifice was not too great, they even succeeded. In personal contacts the form and often the spirit of Christian behaviour or of the ancient Chinese morality were still evident. Even in indirect social relations liberal impulses sometimes triumphed. Moreover in, both empires an active minority worked vigorously for the light, urging humane conduct and propagating the idea of a just social order in which all might find fulfilment. In fact on both sides the more intelligent of the adherents of the light confidently looked forward to a great and glorious Change, if not in the near future, at least in the lifetime of their children. Even the rulers themselves, the military-political groups which controlled the two empires, believed sincerely not indeed in radical change, but in their mission to rule the world and lead it to a vaguely conceived Utopia of discipline and martial virtue. In neither empire was there at this time the ruthless lust for power and delight in cruelty which had for a’ while dominated Germany. Between the rulers of the two empires there was an ambiguous relationship. Though each desired to conquer the other by diplomacy or war, and though to each the social ideas and the forms of social behaviour propagated by the other were repugnant, yet, both agreed in regarding something else as more repugnant, namely the overthrow of their own state by their own progressive minority. Consequently their policy was guided not only by fluctuations in their power in relation to the enemy but also by the strength or weakness of their own progressives. Sincerely, and sometimes even with sincere reluctance, they used the plea of external danger to enforce stricter discipline at home. Yet at times when social upheaval seemed imminent they would not scruple to ask the external enemy to ease his pressure for a while. And invariably the request was granted; for neither of the ruling groups wished to see its opponents overthrown in revolution.</p>
